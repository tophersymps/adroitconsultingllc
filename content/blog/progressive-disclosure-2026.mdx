Every mature interface carries a version of the same fight. The team keeps adding settings, filters, fields, and configuration until the screen that used to fit one idea now fits none. The first reaction is to hide everything behind an "Advanced options" control and call the job done. The second reaction, weeks later, is a flood of confused support tickets and power users who grumble that they cannot find the one thing they actually change every day.

Progressive disclosure is the discipline that sits between those two failures. It is the practice of showing people only what they need to act on, and letting the rest surface on demand. Nielsen Norman Group has documented the pattern for two decades because it is one of the few moves that improves learnability for new users and speed for experts at the same time[^1]. But it only works when you decide what to hide and what to leave open with the same rigor you would bring to a content audit, and when you treat the hidden state as an accessibility problem rather than a storage closet.

This article walks the decision from both directions. I define the pattern and its two main shapes, show how to pick the split so you hide the right things, lay out when hiding is the wrong call, and then go through the accessibility contract that a disclosure control has to honor before it ships. Everything traces to a live source, and the guidance is specific enough to apply the same afternoon.

## What progressive disclosure actually is

The core move is a two-step handoff. First you display the small set of controls that cover the common case, the options a typical user reaches for on an ordinary visit. Then you let people ask for more with a trigger, and only the details they requested appear[^1]. Nielsen's original example is the classic file print dialog: it shows the handful of settings almost everyone needs, and the mouse-sensitive "more options" section stays out of the way for people who do not need it[^1].

![Progressive disclosure core mechanism: the initial view shows a small set of core controls, a trigger reads "Show advanced options," and the revealed view adds a secondary group while the core options stay put](</diagrams/progressive-disclosure-2026/1-core-mechanism.png>)

The benefit is not just a cleaner screen. Three of the five famous usability attributes improve at once. Learnability rises because a new user has fewer choices to parse and fewer distracting details to dismiss. Efficiency rises because the people who know the system do not have to wade past the advanced noise to reach the setting they use constantly. Error rates fall because a dense control surface is exactly where misclicks happen[^1]. A screen that shows everything asks every user to pay for the complexity that only some of them need.

The pattern earns its name from the tension it keeps alive. You are not hiding capability from your users, which is what teams charge progressive disclosure for when they are actually just burying defaults. You are deferring it. The advanced option still exists, it is one deliberate action away, and the user made that action count by choosing it. The moment a piece of functionality becomes undiscoverable because no trigger leads to it, you have stopped doing progressive disclosure and started doing subtraction.

## Two shapes: progressive and staged disclosure

Nielsen draws a line between two related but different patterns that people routinely collapse into one[^1]. Progressive disclosure keeps the user on a single screen. The interface reveals or emphasizes additional detail in place, usually through an expander, a details element, or a collapsible panel, and the person never leaves the context they are working in. Staged disclosure, what most of us call a wizard, split the same job across a sequence of screens with Next and Back navigation, so the user answers one small question at a time and the interface assembles the whole at the end[^1].

![Progressive disclosure versus staged disclosure: progressive keeps the user on one screen and reveals detail in place, while staged walks a linear sequence of Next and Back steps across multiple screens](</diagrams/progressive-disclosure-2026/2-progressive-vs-staged.png>)

The two shapes optimize for different situations, and picking the wrong one is a real cost. A wizard shines when a task is a genuine sequence of dependent choices, the classic case being a multi-page checkout or an account setup flow, where each step's answer changes what the next step should ask[^2]. Forcing that kind of flow onto a single long scrolling form buries the first decisions under the later ones. The reverse is just as wasteful. When you have a handful of independent settings that a user would typically change together, a wizard forces them through empty screens and pointless clicks, and a single screen with an in-place reveal would have served better. The unit of analysis is the dependency between the decisions, not the raw number of fields[^1][^2].

The group-by-group habit shows up in real design systems. Teams reach for whichever pattern was easiest to build, then retrofit the other when complaints arrive. The better habit is to name the dependency structure first, a question this article's [form design guidance](/field-notes/form-ux-design-2026) covers in detail, and let that answer choose the shape rather than the other way around.

## How to pick what to hide

Choosing the split is where progressive disclosure actually succeeds or fails, and it is an evidence task, not a taste task. Nielsen's guide is blunt: base the decision on how often each control is used and on observed behavior, not on what the most senior person in the room happens to prefer[^1]. The candidates to hide are the options a minority of users needs, or that a particular user reaches for rarely. The candidates to keep in the open are the ones the majority touches on a routine visit, because hiding a frequently used control forces your most common users to take an extra click every single time[^1].

![When to hide: a decision tree asks whether only some users need a piece of information, whether it is one self-contained section, and whether the task breaks into independent steps, then maps each answer to details, an accordion, tabs, or a wizard](</diagrams/progressive-disclosure-2026/3-when-to-hide.png>)

Two research methods feed the decision. Usage analytics tell you frequency, which options are actually exercised and which sit dead in the interface. Observational usability testing catches the failures frequency cannot, the markets and sales users who search hopelessly for a control you hid because your analytics said it was rarely used[^1]. The two together beat either alone. Frequency data tells you what is safe to defer. Observation tells you when a deferred control still matters enough to surface, because the people who use it are exactly the ones you are trying to keep.

Keep the depth honest. Research has repeatedly found that a second level of disclosure is usable, but that going deeper than that starts to throw people off[^1]. If your interface needs "settings, then a submenu, then an advanced tab, then a developer mode," you have not designed progressive disclosure, you have designed a hole. The fix is to flatten, to group the options into at most two coherent levels where each level maps to a real audience, and then to test that the second level is reachable by the people who actually need it.

## When hiding is the wrong call

The GOV.UK Design System puts a useful boundary on the whole exercise in its guidance for the details component, the browser's expandable disclosure element[^3]. Only use it when some users need a piece of information and others do not. If most people need the content, leave it open; hiding it forces the majority to take an extra step to reach what they came for, and it hides the existence of the content from people who did not know to look[^3]. The details element is also the wrong tool when the hidden region is large or multi-part. It is built to hold one self-contained section of supporting text, so a sprawling block belongs instead in an accordion or navigation that the reader can scan[^3].

That guidance surfaces a failure mode worth naming: disclosure becomes a way to bury design debt. When a team hides a bad default, or a setting they are embarrassed to surface, behind an expander, the pattern stops helping anyone. The advanced users still fight the bad default, and the new users never learn the capability exists. A disclosure control is not a confessions drawer. If the content underneath is complex enough that hiding it feels like the only way to make the page presentable, the page has a structure problem, not a hiding problem.

There is also a pacing cost that most discussions skip. Revealed content arrives after a click, so the user has to hold the context of what they asked about while the panel opens. For genuinely supporting material, reference text, a license, a term's definition, that cost is small and worth paying. For material the user actually has to act on, a field they must fill or a setting they must choose, the reveal adds friction to the moment of action. Match the pattern to the job: disclose to reduce the reading load for a minority, not to make your primary action harder.

## The accessibility contract

Hidden content is the part of this pattern that most often breaks in production, and it breaks for screen reader and keyboard users first. A disclosure control has to tell assistive technology what is going on, and the affordances are concrete and checkable. The authoritative shape is the W3C ARIA disclosure pattern: the trigger is a button, it carries an `aria-expanded` state that flips between true and false, and an `aria-controls` value pointing at the revealed region's id[^6].

![Accessible disclosure anatomy: a button trigger carries aria-expanded and aria-controls, the revealed region toggles its hidden state, and keyboard users land on the trigger so they never enter collapsed content](</diagrams/progressive-disclosure-2026/4-accessible-anatomy.png>)

The design questions around those attributes are where the experience lives. Senior accessibility practitioners recommend choosing a button when the action controls the visibility of something, and a link when the action moves the user somewhere[^4]. That distinction is what resolves the tempting question of whether to auto-focus the revealed content: if you are using a button to show a panel, the user has not navigated anywhere, so moving their focus for them is disorienting, and you leave the focus on the trigger and let them find the new content in place. If you are actually linking to another place in the document, then yes, you take them there and focus it[^4].

Two past failures make the contract concrete. The GOV.UK team found that its progressive disclosure element, hidden on load and shown on activation, would not open for JAWS or NVDA users in older Internet Explorer because those screen readers only dispatch the `click` event, while the code listened for `keyup`, `keypress`, and `mouseup`[^5]. The fix was to change the event handler so the code honors the `click` event that assistive technology actually dispatches, and the episode is the classic warning against writing activation handlers that ignore the click that assistive technology sends. The second subtlety is focus order: collapsed content must not contain focusable elements that a keyboard user can tab into, because a person can reach hidden controls they were never told exist. Keep the collapsed region truly inert until it opens.

These mechanics are exactly the kind of thing that belongs pinned in a reusable component rather than rewritten by hand in every screen. If your [component library](/field-notes/headless-ui-component-libraries-2026) does not already ship a disclosure primitive with these attributes baked in, that is the single most valuable addition it can make, because the accessibility contract is where per-screen discretion reliably goes wrong.

## How to ship it without breaking anything

The practical sequence comes from treating progressive disclosure as a content decision with a reusable component, not as a styling choice. Start by listing every control, setting, filter, and field that currently ships. Split the list into the majority path, the options most users touch on a routine visit, and the long tail of expert or rare settings. Resist the urge to hide more than one level deep, and verify the boundary with usage data, because that tells you which of the "obvious" hides will actually turn your common path into a detour[^1].

Then encode the trigger as the accessible pattern above, a button with `aria-expanded` and `aria-controls`, wired to the `click` event, with the collapsed content kept out of the tab order. Test it twice, once with a keyboard and once with a screen reader, and check that the revealed content is reachable and the state is announced. Finally, treat the pattern as a design-system citizen. A disclosure control that every screen implements slightly differently is not progressive disclosure, it is six different ways to hide the same content.

We reached this conclusion the hard way inside Adroit's own delivery pipeline, where a reporting screen used by consultants grew thirteen filters over two years and collapsed to nothing but a wall labeled "configure." Hiding the filters fixed the visual noise and instantly broke the daily users, who now had to open a panel before every single report. The resolution was not to unhide everything, which is just surrendering, but to move the two filters the majority set every day out of the panel and onto the surface, and keep the long tail behind the trigger. The daily users stopped tapping, and the screen got its breathing room back. The lesson generalizes beyond that one screen: the split is only correct when the people who need it most are not the ones paying the extra click.
