Most product teams treat research like a quarterly weather report. They run one study, harvest a report, then build for months on what that single snapshot told them. By the time they ship, the market has moved, the report is stale, and the product answers a question nobody is asking anymore. The fix is not a bigger study. It is a different cadence.

Continuous discovery is the practice of touching your customers every week, not every quarter, and letting each small interaction nudge what you build next. Teresa Torres, who formalized the label in 2021, defines it as weekly touchpoints with customers, run by the team building the product, doing small research activities in pursuit of a desired outcome[^1]. The emphasis lands on three words: weekly, by the team, and small. Not biannual, by a research consultant, and not a 40-participant marathon. If you are weighing how AI tools fit into this habit, our [AI-assisted UX research guide](/field-notes/ai-in-ux-research-2026) covers which parts of the pipeline you can safely automate.

This article walks through why the project-shaped approach fails, how the weekly cadence works, and the one artifact that ties the whole loop together: the opportunity solution tree.

## Why the project shape fails

A research project has a start, a middle, and an end. It produces a deliverable, often a deck or a written report, and the implicit promise is that the finding stays true until the next one. That promise is the flaw.

Discovery and delivery run at different speeds. Delivery is continuous; you release every two weeks or faster. Discovery under the project model is episodic, a burst that happens once every few months. The two rhythms never align. You research in January, build from February through May, and your decisions are locked to a picture of the market that is already three months old by mid-build[^1].

The financial cost of that split is quiet because it never shows up as a line item. Nobody bills an invoice for the feature nobody wanted. But the waste is real. A team can spend eight months building against a plausible-sounding need and land exactly at break even. That is what happened at Grailed, a peer-to-peer marketplace for fashion. Their Discovery team spent eight months building a follow-and-curate feed, the obvious answer to "help people manage their own feed," and the result was underwhelming[^5]. The project was not stupid; it was just frozen in time. It answered a framing their own customers had never actually given them.

## Continuous discovery is a habit, not a project

The name is the point. Torres frames the shift as building habits the way you build a muscle, one small repeated motion rather than one large periodic event[^1].

The defining unit of the habit is the weekly customer touchpoint. The team building the product does the talking, not a delegated researcher who hands over a summary. A product manager, a designer, and an engineer interview a customer each week, in rotation. That is the entire habit set in its seed form: one person, one week, one customer[^1].

The repetition does the work that a quarterly study cannot. Because you interview every week, you build a running picture instead of a frozen one. When a customer surprises you, that surprise lands in the following Monday's decision, not in a report that reaches you next quarter. The cadence is what makes the insight actionable, because it arrives while the product is still being shaped.

Torres calls continuous interviewing the keystone habit. Once a product trio starts talking to customers weekly, the other habits follow: mapping what they learn, prototyping more often, and experimenting[^2]. The interview is the entry point that unlocks the rest, which is why the weekly rhythm matters more than the research technique you use inside it.

## The opportunity solution tree holds it together

A weekly interview produces a pile of loose observations. Left alone, they drift. The opportunity solution tree is the practice that gives them a home, a single living map of what the team believes and why[^3].

The tree has four levels. At the top sits the desired outcome, the business measure the team is trying to move, like growing lifetime value from a home feed. Below it sits the opportunity space, the customer needs and problems that, if addressed, would move that outcome. Below that sit the solutions the team is exploring, and below the solutions sit the assumption tests that will show which solution actually works[^3].

The tree is not a one-time diagram. It is a working document that changes every week. New opportunities get added when an interview surfaces one; dead ends get pruned when a test disproves them; solutions appear and vanish as evidence accumulates[^3]. Grailed's tree grew from a first draft with a single branch labelled "increase relevance" into a much richer structure whose branches read like notes from real conversations: "show me stuff that is popular," "there should always be new stuff when I come," "show me things I interact with"[^5].

![The opportunity solution tree, built from the Grailed feed case: a desired outcome at the top, opportunities voiced in customer words in the middle, solutions below them, and assumption tests at the bottom, each level connected to the next](</diagrams/continuous-discovery-weekly-habit-2026/1-ost-anatomy.png>)

The tree's real value is that it forces the team to separate an opportunity from a solution. An opportunity is an unmet customer need, problem, or desire. A solution is a candidate way to meet it. Teams collapse the two constantly, jumping straight to the build. The tree keeps them apart so the team can attack the same opportunity from multiple solution angles instead of falling in love with the first idea[^3]. Grailed's own words carry the lesson: "It's easy to lull yourself into the idea that you don't need to do any of this stuff and it might seem like extra work when you get it right, but very often you don't get it right the first time"[^5].

## The weekly loop, in practice

A week of continuous discovery is small enough that a single team can run it without a research department. The shape is always the same[^4].

Start with an interview. Select for variation, not for a representative sample. With qualitative interviewing you cannot interview enough people to model the population, so the equivalent of a representative sample is talking to a spread of people: power users, first-time users, disengaged ones, people who use one feature and people who use every feature[^2]. The goal is not prediction; it is surfacing as much of the experience as possible[^2].

Then run the interview about the customer, not your product. Story-based interviewing spends most of the conversation collecting the customer's story rather than pitching features at them. It works on two fronts. The customer usually ends the call wanting to do it again, because being genuinely listened to is rare, and the signal you get is cleaner, because you are hearing their framing rather than their reaction to yours[^4].

Map what you heard onto the tree. Then, instead of waiting to gather more proof, run a tiny assumption test on the next hypothesis. Some teams get stuck expecting a representative sample before they will trust a finding; Torres's answer is the opposite, start talking to whoever is easiest to reach, then widen the variation as the habit builds[^2]. The loop closes when you take the small evidence and decide to keep, adjust, or drop the idea, and then you start the next week's interview with a sharper question.

## Selecting the people takes care of itself early

The biggest objection to a weekly cadence is that customers are too rare or too busy to talk to that often. Torres has heard it from teams that serve car salespeople, a group famous for cancelling interviews the moment a customer walks in the door. The workaround her team landed on was meeting the salespeople where they were: ask them to text when they had fifteen free minutes, and take the call in that pocket of downtime[^4].

The principle generalizes. No customer matches your schedule perfectly, but every customer has a pocket of downtime, a wait in line, a gap between meetings. The habit is fit around the customer's rhythm, not yours[^4]. And if you are just starting, recruit whoever is easiest. Even a complete outlier will teach you something on your first interview; you optimize the selection later, once the habit is real[^2].

The worry that weekly contact will annoy customers also dissolves if the interview is about them. The draw that keeps people coming back is not the incentive; it is the rare experience of being listened to. A customer who feels heard will typically end an interview asking when the next one is[^4].

## The cadence versus the project

The contrast between the two shapes is worth making explicit, because teams tend to assume continuous discovery is just more research done faster. It is not.

The project model concentrates research into a block, then hands it off to a long build that runs on autopilot. The weekly model interleaves research and building so every release is checked against fresh evidence and direction changes as you go. The project model assumes the answer to the research question will hold. The weekly model assumes it will not, so it keeps a small loop running to catch the drift early[^2].

![One research project per quarter versus a weekly cadence, shown as six-month timelines: the project stacks a short research block under a long build and passes control to autopilot with a lukewarm launch, while the weekly cadence interleaves small touchpoints with small builds so direction corrects continuously](</diagrams/continuous-discovery-weekly-habit-2026/2-bigbang-vs-weekly.png>)

The difference is visible in the build itself. Grailed's first feed took eight months and landed at break even. After the team switched to mapping opportunities and interviewing weekly, the reworked feed drove discoveries and gross merchandise volume up four to five times, and users who activated through the feed opened the app about 30% more and discovered about 40% more than the average user, lifting lifetime value by roughly 20%[^5]. The same company, the same feed, a different cadence, a different outcome.

## Take the habit on a walk before you scale it

Adopting continuous discovery does not require a mandate or a reorganization. It starts with one team running one weekly interview, and it spreads by example.

Grailed's team started with a book club and a first draft of a tree. They did not announce a transformation; they got together, dumped out everything they thought they knew, and structured it into an opportunity solution tree. The first version was thin, a single branch labelled "increase relevance"[^5]. From there they started talking to users to check whether each branch was true, and the tree got denser as the evidence did.

That is the pattern worth copying. Pick one outcome your team owns. Draw the tree's first draft from what you already believe. Then start interviewing to check it, one customer a week, one team member at a time. The tree prunes itself as the interviews accumulate, and the keystone habit takes root before you ever try to change the whole organization[^3].

![Same feature, two framings: the business framing "I wish I could control what my feed showed me" implies a curation panel, while the customer framing "show me more stuff I like and less stuff I don't" implies better relevance, and the wording of the opportunity decides what better means](</diagrams/continuous-discovery-weekly-habit-2026/4-framing-shift.png>)

The strongest part of this approach is how cheap it is to start. No major investment in tools or a larger research team. One person, one phone call, one week. The habit does not ask you to believe in it in advance. It asks you to run the interview, map what you learn, and run one small test. After a month of that, the evidence decides whether it works.

Run a single weekly interview this week. That is the whole first step, and it is the only one that matters.
