Three engagement leads sit in the same Tuesday staffing call and get asked the same question about the same person, a data migration architect who is the only one on the bench who has run a Snowflake-to-Databricks cutover. Each lead gives the same true answer. He is on my engagement 40 percent of the time, and my plan is fine.

No plan is lying. Divide the same man three ways and he is committed at 120 percent for the next six weeks. The first person to notice will be the client, when a deliverable slips in a sprint that no single project plan could ever have seen.

That is not a scheduling error you can fix by asking people to be more careful. It is what happens when a portfolio-level decision gets made, by default, inside three separate project plans.

## A plan is a claim on somebody else's capacity

A project plan reads like a schedule. In a consultancy it is a financial claim on a shared and finite pool, denominated in people rather than in tasks, and the claim is only as good as the availability behind it.

The tooling makes this worse rather than better. Almost every project management tool is built around a single engagement, so it will render three plans that each say "on track" while one person is at 120 percent across them. The conflict does not live in any of the three plans. It lives in the union of them, and no single plan is capable of computing it.

The industry's own numbers say the default outcome is not good. SPI Research's 2026 Professional Services Maturity Benchmark, now in its nineteenth year and built from 509 professional services organizations representing more than 245,000 employees [^1], puts on-time project delivery at 73.8 percent, essentially flat against 73.4 percent the year before and below the 76 percent five-year average and the 80.2 percent recorded in 2021 [^2]. Project overrun came in at 10.7 percent, better than the year before but still above the 10 percent line that SPI flags as the point where overruns begin damaging client relationships and future bookings [^2].

Read those two figures together and the conclusion is uncomfortable but useful. Roughly one project in four is late at firms that measure themselves hard enough to fill in the survey. Lateness at that rate is not evidence of bad project managers. It is what the operating model produces when the allocation decision is pushed to the layer that cannot see the whole board. The interesting question is not who to blame. It is which decisions sit at the wrong layer.

## What one plan cannot see

Two different numbers get called "availability", and only one of them survives contact with a calendar.

**Nominal availability** is what a person's schedule says they have. A consultant is 100 percent available on Monday morning, minus nothing, because nothing has been subtracted yet. A full-time year is roughly 2,080 hours on paper, and nominal availability spends every one of them. **Real availability** is what remains after public holidays, approved leave, internal work, pre-sales support, practice commitments and training. The second number is the one any allocation has to be built on, and it is the one almost nobody writes down.

Then there is contention, which is a different failure and needs different arithmetic. If the data migration architect is committed at 40 percent on three engagements that all peak in the same six weeks, weeks 12 to 17, every one of those commitments was individually satisfiable at the moment it was granted, and collectively they are impossible. Nobody had to make a mistake. The grants simply arrived in three separate conversations.

Two more properties make this harder in a consultancy than in a product team.

**Skill is not fungible.** Contention concentrates. A firm can have a hundred consultants and twenty available seats and still be unable to move the one person who has done a Snowflake cutover, because the other ninety-nine do not substitute. Scarce skills turn a capacity problem into a key-person risk, and the conflict always lands on the same handful of names.

**Capacity moves while you deliver.** SPI puts total attrition at 11.3 percent and headcount growth at 2.8 percent, with subcontractors contributing 10.4 percent of professional services revenue [^2]. Every one of those movements changes the pool mid-flight. If your record of who is available lives in the heads of three engagement leads, the record is already wrong, and subcontractors who are not in it are not capacity you can plan against.

## Why the local decision is rational

It is tempting to read the 120 percent outcome as a discipline problem. It is not. It is a collision of objectives.

An engagement lead is measured on their engagement: the date they promised, the margin they carry, the client who escalates to them and nobody else. Nothing in that job description mentions the other two engagements that want the same person in the same week. Given a choice between staffing their own plan fully and voluntarily releasing capacity to a project they are not measured on, the locally rational move is obvious, and it is the move that produces the portfolio problem.

This is not a consulting-specific observation about incentives. It is a studied property of multi-project scheduling. Browning and Yassine analysed twenty priority rules across 12,320 test problems and found that several widely advocated rules perform poorly in identifiable situations, and, more pointedly, that portfolio managers and project managers prefer different rules because their objectives differ [^4]. Mao and Yuan reached the same conclusion from a different direction, testing thirty rules over 16,000 portfolios containing 48,000 projects, and found that applying one rule everywhere produces unnecessarily poor performance; the best rule depends on the shape of the portfolio itself [^5].

The practical consequence is that contention does not get solved by exhortation. You cannot ask a project layer to optimise for a portfolio objective it does not own and cannot see. The optimisation has to happen at a layer that has both the objective and the visibility, which is why the research keeps landing on the same architecture: local scheduling inside each engagement, plus a coordination layer that detects conflicts across them and holds a single rule for resolving them [^8].

PMI's 2026 Pulse of the Profession describes the same friction from the people side. Its organisational dimension of complexity is exactly this shape: unclear decision-making authority, siloed teams and competing priorities, in projects where escalations take longer, approvals stall in hidden layers, and "who owns this?" becomes a recurring question [^7].

## The cost of spreading a person thin

When two engagements want the same specialist, the fast answer is to split the person: two days here, three days there, everyone gets something. It feels like a compromise. It is usually a transfer of cost from the decision-maker to the delivery date.

The evidence for switching costs comes from cognitive psychology rather than from billing data. Rubinstein, Meyer and Evans measured what happens when people alternate between tasks instead of repeating one, and found switching costs that increased with the complexity of the task and were larger when moving from a familiar task to a less familiar one [^6]. That is a laboratory result and should be read as one, but the direction is unambiguous: "40 percent of a person" is not "40 percent of an unbroken person", and the loss is largest exactly where it hurts most, which is an expert moving between two unfamiliar client contexts.

Sequencing looks worse than spreading, because a queue is visible and a switching cost is not. Put the specialist on one engagement for three unbroken weeks and the other two leads see a start date pushed out. They will object, correctly, that their own dates are now at risk. Split the person and everybody's plan stays green while the work takes longer in aggregate. This is the same reflex that makes utilisation targets backfire, and it is worth understanding as one problem rather than two.

## Four parts of an allocation that holds

None of this requires a resource management platform in the first instance, and buying one before the four decisions below are made is how firms end up with accurate data about a process nobody follows. The decisions come first.

### 1. One record, and it answers a different question than the plan

The plan asks whether an engagement is on track. The allocation record asks a different question: who is committed where, in which week, and at whose decision. Those are different tables and they belong to different owners.

A working allocation record needs seven fields and no more to start: the person; the skill or seniority the assignment actually requires; hours per week; start and end week; engagement and phase; priority rank; and the decision owner with the date it was decided. The last two are the ones that get left out and the ones that make the record allocative rather than descriptive. A record that shows a conflict but not who resolved it is a report. A record that shows the resolution is a system. The interface between the two layers is exactly this line: the moment an assignment is granted it stops being a local decision and becomes a portfolio commitment, and the record is where that transition gets written down. It also settles where the record lives: outside every engagement's own board, because a board that belongs to one engagement cannot hold a claim that spans the firm.

Tooling follows the record, not the other way around, but the tooling gap is real. PSA adoption reached 68.9 percent while only 38.7 percent of firms have adopted a project-based ERP that unifies delivery and financials, and those that have report 20 percent faster year-over-year revenue growth and EBITDA of 10.2 percent against 8.6 percent for firms without one [^2]. The pattern is consistent across the benchmarks: firms with connected data make better allocation decisions, and disconnected point tools produce visible workarounds.

We ran into the same constraint inside our own delivery pipeline, where the scarce resource is not build capacity but review attention. An agent can draft in minutes; a named reviewer still has to read it, and the same reviewer sits across several workstreams. The record that made the difference was not any single workstream's own board, because each board was correct. It was the one list of who was holding review capacity that week, kept outside all of them.

### 2. Availability that has been subtracted

Write the definition once and use it everywhere: available hours equals working days, minus public holidays, minus approved leave, minus non-billable commitments. Then publish the number with the date it was computed, because a stale availability figure is worse than none.

The industry's own utilisation data shows how much room there is to get this wrong. Billable utilisation fell to 66.4 percent in 2025, the lowest in the nineteen years SPI has run the benchmark, against the 75 percent level SPI treats as optimal [^2]. High performers average 75.0 percent against 64.9 percent for everyone else [^3]. The gap between those firms and the rest is not that one group works harder. It is that the high group is staffed against a number that reflects reality, so its commitments are smaller and its promises are kept.

Two less obvious subtractions matter as much as leave. The first is ramp: new hires in leading firms reach productivity in 54 days against an industry average of 65 [^3]. Those eleven days are real, unpaid, and almost never reserved in a plan that starts a person on a project the week they arrive. The second is internal work, which is where under-utilisation actually hides. A consultant at a 66 percent utilisation rate is not idle a third of the time. They are doing pre-sales, practice, hiring, internal systems and administration, and all of it consumes the same hours an allocation model is competing for.

### 3. A priority rule agreed before the conflict

When two engagements want the same person in the same week, one of them gets them. The only question is whether that decision was made by an agreed rule before the conflict, or by whoever escalated loudest during it.

Write the rule down and circulate it: nearest contractual date first, then a committed client milestone, then contribution margin, then the sequence that was already published. The exact ordering matters far less than three properties. The rule exists before the fight. It is the same rule for every engagement. And the lead who loses can see why, because an allocation that feels arbitrary is an allocation that gets relitigated next month. If the skill does not exist in the firm at all, the request is a hire or a partner rather than an assignment, and both carry a lead time that has to be started weeks before the conflict instead of during it.

Give the decision a deadline as well as an owner. An unfilled slot with no decision date behaves like an unfilled slot nobody knows about, and decision latency is one of the most reliable early signals that an engagement is drifting. It is also a direct delivery cost rather than a reporting artefact, because the work cannot start while the decision is open. This is the same discipline a decision log enforces on requirements: once every open question has a name and a date next to it, the queue becomes measurable and the drift becomes visible while it is still cheap to fix. We wrote about that mechanism separately in [the case for a consulting decision log](/field-notes/decision-log-consulting-delivery-2026).

### 4. A cadence at three horizons

Allocation decays. Commitments made six weeks ago against availability computed then need revisiting now, and there is no single meeting that can hold every horizon at once. Split it into three.

**Weekly**, the shortest useful window: look four weeks ahead, surface every conflict where a person is committed above real availability, and produce either an assignment or an explicit queue position with a date. This meeting decides, it does not report. If it ends without a staffing change or a queued date, it was a status meeting wearing a resource meeting's name.

**Monthly**: compare committed demand against pipeline, adjust the pool, and set the hiring or subcontracting triggers. This is where forecast accuracy gets measured, which is the number most teams never compute: of the assignments we planned for last month, how many actually happened as planned.

**Quarterly**: the skills inventory for the next two quarters, the bench policy, and the subcontractor mix. If the priority rule keeps sending work to the same two clients, that is a strategy signal rather than a capacity one.

The metrics that matter at these horizons are allocation accuracy and decision latency, not utilisation. Utilisation is the output of a staffing process, read as a result rather than a target, and the moment it becomes the input the staffing process starts filling hours instead of honouring commitments, which is [how 100 percent utilisation ends up guaranteeing late delivery](/field-notes/utilization-trap-capacity-planning-2026).

## Five ways this quietly fails

| Anti-pattern | What it looks like in practice | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Staffing from the bench | The first available person is assigned because they are free, not because they fit | Match on skill and context first, then on availability; leave the bench visible rather than filling it |
| The rule invented in the meeting | The specialist goes to whoever escalates highest on the day | Publish the rule before the conflict and apply it to every engagement |
| A record only project managers can see | Each lead keeps their own spreadsheet and reconciles by email | One record, one owner, readable by everyone who commits capacity |
| Contractors outside the model | Subcontractors deliver 10.4 percent of revenue with no entry in the capacity record | Enter them as capacity with the same fields, including ramp and availability |
| Utilisation as the target | Every billable hour is filled, and the commitments made to fill them are impossible | Track allocation accuracy and decision latency; treat utilisation as a result |

## The test to run on Monday

Ask every engagement lead two questions. Name the people on your plan whose real availability you verified this week from a source outside your own project. Then name the one person you depend on who is committed elsewhere and whom you have not spoken to.

If they can answer both, you have an allocation practice, and the plans you are reading are worth the paper or the dashboard they sit on. If they cannot, your plans are probably fine and your portfolio is not, and the difference will show up in a client conversation rather than in your own reporting.

The first move is smaller than it sounds: one record, one decision owner, one thirty-minute weekly conflict review governed by a rule you publish in advance. The plans can stay exactly as they are. They were never the problem.
