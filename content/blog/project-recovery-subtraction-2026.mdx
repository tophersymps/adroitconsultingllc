Every recovery plan that fails starts the same way. The project is behind, so the room reaches for the three things that feel like speed: more people, more hours, more process. Six weeks later the project is further behind, the team is frayed, and nobody can say why more effort produced less output.

The arithmetic of a late project is counterintuitive in one specific way. Most of what you can add to a late project is not free capacity. It is new work. A new teammate has to be recruited into the codebase, the domain, and the decision history before producing anything. Ten extra hours a week has to be paid for in attention and in defects, and attention does not scale linearly with hours. A new ceremony is attended by the same people who are already the constraint. Every addition carries a ramp-up cost, and the ramp-up lands on exactly the people the project cannot afford to slow down.

That is why recovery is a subtraction discipline. The moves that genuinely shorten a late project's path to done are the ones that remove work, collapse parallelism, and reduce the number of things that must be true before it can finish. This piece is the playbook: how to triage before touching the plan, why each addition makes the date worse, why the same failing project keeps getting funded anyway, and the sequence of cuts that puts a credible date back on the calendar.

If the status reporting that produced the bad news is itself suspect, start with the companion piece on why [percent complete misleads](/field-notes/percent-complete-status-reporting-2026). This article assumes you have a number you trust and now have to act on it.

## Triage before you re-plan

A project that is late is not automatically a project that is broken, and the three common causes of a red status need three different responses. Classify first, because a re-plan applied to the wrong cause buys you a month and a second bad status.

The first cause is a genuine delivery problem: the work is harder or larger than the plan assumed, the estimates were optimistic, and the remaining effort is simply more than the remaining calendar. The recovery is subtraction. Cut the work back to what the date can hold.

The second cause is a measurement artifact: the work is fine and the reporting is wrong. Milestones were defined as activities rather than outcomes, so "90 percent done" has been true for three months, or the baseline was never updated after an approved change, so the plan and the project describe different things. The recovery is to fix the milestone definitions and the baseline, then re-status honestly. It costs a day, not a quarter.

The third cause is a commitment problem: the work is fine, the estimate is fine, and the date was never possible. It was promised before the requirements existed, or it comes from a business event no plan can move. The recovery is a renegotiation with the sponsor, and no amount of engineering will substitute for it.

![Triage decision: a red status routes to three causes, a genuine delivery problem, a measurement artifact, or a commitment problem, each with a different recovery](</diagrams/project-recovery-subtraction-2026/1-triage.png>)

The tell for the commitment problem is arithmetic. If the remaining work, at the team's demonstrated rate, cannot fit in the remaining time even with everything else removed, no recovery plan will work and the only honest deliverable is a new date or a smaller scope. Say that in the first meeting, not the fifth.

## The three additions, and what each actually costs

### Adding people

Fred Brooks wrote the sentence in 1975 and nothing in twenty years of tooling has repealed it: "Adding manpower to a late software project makes it later"[^1]. He named three costs that arrive with the new person. The work has to be repartitioned, which is a design task in itself. The new person has to be trained on the system, and the training is done by the people who are already behind. And the communication paths multiply: with n people there are n(n-1)/2 pairwise channels, so three workers carry three times the pairwise coordination of two, and four carry six times as much[^1]. Coordination is not a rounding error on a project whose remaining work is mostly integration.

There are honest exceptions, and Brooks named them too. A person who already knows the domain and the codebase and is trusted by the team can be net positive quickly. But that person is usually the one you cannot spare from what they are already doing, which is why the exception is rarer in practice than it is in the meeting where it gets proposed. The other exception is real but narrow: help that removes a blocker from the critical path's predecessor is not the same as help that joins the critical path. Adding hands to the path itself lengthens it; adding hands to the queue in front of it can shorten it.

### Adding hours

Overtime looks like the cheapest lever because it does not require a hiring request. The data on what it does to sustained output has been available since 1980, when the Business Roundtable's construction task force published its analysis of scheduled overtime[^2]. Two findings matter for a late project.

The first is that productivity decays on a curve, not a step. Output drops sharply in the first days of a sixty-hour week, recovers partially, holds for two to three weeks, then declines for the next two to three, and levels out at a low point after nine to twelve weeks of sustained overtime[^2]. The second is the consequence: where a schedule of sixty or more hours per week continues beyond about two months, the cumulative productivity loss makes the completion date later than it would have been with the same crew on forty-hour weeks[^2]. You can work harder and finish behind.

![Overtime productivity curve: hours of work accomplished per week on 50-hour and 60-hour schedules fall against a 40-hour week baseline, crossing below it around week nine](</diagrams/project-recovery-subtraction-2026/2-overtime-curve.png>)

Software has the same shape with an extra tail. Extra hours produce code faster in the short term and defects faster in the long term, and the defect load arrives during integration and acceptance, which is the phase a late project is already entering. The Roundtable data carries the same warning from the occupational side: injuries and absenteeism both rose as hours rose[^2].

### Adding process and tools

Process added mid-flight is the quietest of the three failures. A new ceremony, a new board, a new reporting cadence, or a new governance review is attended by the same constrained people, and the cost lands before the benefit. A tool migration is worse: it is a project of its own, with its own cutover and its own data problems, scheduled inside the project that is already late. Both are reasonable things to do on a healthy project and unreasonable things to do to a project that is behind.

## The commitment trap that keeps the project funded

If adding does not work, why does a failing project keep getting more resources? The research on this is old and consistent.

Barry Staw's 1976 experiment found that people committed the most resources to a chosen course of action precisely when they were personally responsible for the negative consequences[^3]. Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer showed the same pull from the money alone: having already spent on a course of action made people more likely to continue, and people who had incurred a sunk cost inflated their own estimate of how likely the project was to succeed[^4]. In information systems specifically, Mark Keil, Joan Mann, and Arun Rai surveyed IS audit and control professionals and found that between 30 and 40 percent of IS projects exhibit some degree of escalation, that escalating projects performed significantly worse, and that the best single predictor of escalation was the completion effect, the pull of being nearly finished[^5].

A later meta-analysis of 166 samples complicated the popular story in a useful way. The size of the sunk cost was a weaker driver than expected; personal responsibility for the original decision and proximity to completion were stronger, and one of the most reliable de-escalation levers was giving the decision maker a face-saving way to withdraw[^6].

![Escalation loop: sunk cost, personal responsibility, and nearness to completion feed continued funding, which creates new sunk cost, with three labeled break points where the loop can be cut](</diagrams/project-recovery-subtraction-2026/3-escalation-loop.png>)

The practical translation is uncomfortable. The person who chose the project is the worst-placed person to judge whether it should continue, and the closer it looks to done, the stronger the pull to keep going. Both facts argue for making the continue-or-change decision somewhere other than the gut of the person who is accountable for the original choice.

## Recovery is a subtraction sequence

The sequence below is ordered. Doing step four before step one produces a new baseline built on top of the old work, which is how a project ends up with two plans and no truth.

First, freeze the scope. A change freeze with one exception path for legal, safety, or regulatory obligations. This is not theoretical stinginess; scope creep is the norm. PMI's 2018 Pulse of the Profession found that 52 percent of projects completed in the previous year experienced scope creep or uncontrolled change[^8]. A freeze is the only moment at which the remaining work stops growing while you measure it.

Second, collapse parallelism to a single critical path. Cut the streams that are not on the path to the first usable outcome, and run the rest in series. This is where the [critical path](/field-notes/critical-path-dependency-scheduling-2026) stops being a scheduling artifact and becomes the recovery instrument: the date is set by the longest chain of dependent work, and every parallel stream you keep alive adds coordination and contention without moving that chain.

Third, clear work in progress and shorten the queues. A late project usually has more items open than the team can finish, and every open item carries a switching cost. Flow metrics measure exactly this: [waiting, not working, is where the calendar goes](/field-notes/flow-metrics-consulting-delivery-2026). Finishing three things and dropping seven beats advancing ten.

Fourth, re-baseline against the work that remains. Not the work that was planned, and not the original contract. Re-estimate the remainder from the current state with the current team, then set the date from that number.

Fifth, re-commit in writing with one owner and one date, and record what was removed to make the date possible.

![Subtractive recovery sequence: freeze scope, collapse to one critical path, clear work in progress, re-baseline the remainder, and re-commit, with what each move buys](</diagrams/project-recovery-subtraction-2026/4-subtraction-sequence.png>)

## The cut list, in order

When you have to remove work, remove it in this order.

Cut scope first. Features that are not on the critical path to the business outcome the project exists to deliver. If nobody can name the outcome, that is the first thing to fix. Then cut parallel work, the streams that run alongside the critical path for convenience rather than necessity. Then cut the optional quality-of-life items on the list, the reporting, the nice-to-have integrations, the polish that a later phase can carry. Then renegotiate the calendar with the sponsor.

Some things do not go on the cut list at all. Testing of the critical path, security and access controls, regulatory and accessibility obligations, and the correctness of data migration all get more expensive when skipped, and they get skipped last and discovered latest. A date bought by cutting them is a date you will pay for twice, and the [acceptance gate](/field-notes/acceptance-gate-consulting-delivery-2026) is where the second payment arrives.

## Pre-agree the exit before you need it

The strongest structural defence against the commitment trap is to decide the exit condition while the project still looks healthy.

Bent Flyvbjerg's analysis of megaprojects is blunt about the direction of travel: projects run over budget and over time over and over again, and planners should identify problems up front and cancel nonviable projects before implementation rather than during it[^7]. Researchers working on forecasting practice have extended that into a concrete mechanism: pre-agreed quit criteria, written down in advance, so that a project which deviates negatively from expectations can be terminated quickly and its resources freed for other work[^10]. Annie Duke's account of the same idea is the behavioral case: quitting on time almost always feels like quitting too early, which is why the decision has to be made before the pressure arrives[^11].

![Pre-agreed tripwire: a baseline with checkpoints, a named tripwire condition, a named decision owner, and two pre-agreed outcomes, continue on a new baseline or stop](</diagrams/project-recovery-subtraction-2026/5-tripwire.png>)

For a consulting engagement the version is smaller and more enforceable. At baseline, write one sentence with a condition and a date: if the data migration has not cleared the staging validation by the checkpoint date, the integration scope comes out and the date holds. Name the person who will make that call, and write both outcomes in advance so that stopping is a decision the project already took rather than a decision someone has to defend under pressure.

## The sponsor conversation

The recovery conversation with a sponsor has one page and four parts: what changed, what the date is if the scope holds, what the scope is if the date holds, and the single decision you need. Bring the arithmetic and let it do the arguing, because the arithmetic is the part nobody can reinterpret.

This is where honesty has a price and dishonesty has a bigger one. Flyvbjerg's work names strategic misrepresentation as a primary cause of the gap between forecasts and outcomes, and it is usually encouraged by incentives rather than malice: the plan that gets approved is the optimistic one[^7]. The aggregate cost of the pattern is not abstract. PMI's 2018 data put the waste at 9.9 percent of every dollar spent on projects, with 48 percent of projects not completed on time and 43 percent not completed within budget[^8][^9].

Presenting a recovery as a catch-up plan rather than a re-baseline is the same mistake in miniature. A catch-up plan keeps the original date, keeps the original scope, and adds the three additions that make both worse. A re-baseline changes one variable in public and gives the team something it can actually hit.

## When stopping is the right recovery

The uncomfortable option is sometimes correct. If the premise that justified the project has changed, if the remaining work costs more than the value it will deliver, or if the same constraint is needed on work that matters more, stopping is a portfolio decision rather than a failure, and it is the fastest recovery available because it returns the team to productive work immediately. The tripwire exists so that this decision can be taken calmly, at a checkpoint, by a named owner, using a condition agreed when the information was cleaner.

For everything else, the recovery is the same discipline. Subtract the work the date cannot hold, cut the parallelism that is not on the critical path, re-baseline against what remains, and re-commit once. A late project does not get faster by adding. It gets faster when there is less left to finish.
