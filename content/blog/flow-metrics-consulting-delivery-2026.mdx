Ask a consulting firm how its delivery is doing and you hear utilization. Billable seats are filled, so the practice feels full. Utilization is a resource number, and it answers a resource question: how many of the hours we pay for are we selling. It tells you nothing about how fast work gets to the client. An engagement can run at 90 percent utilization and still deliver every deliverable three weeks late, because the consultants, everyone of them occupied, spent those weeks waiting on each other, on review, on sign-off [^1]. The discipline that finds that wait is flow analysis, and it is nearly absent from how consulting teams measure themselves. This article is about the three flow numbers a delivery leader should track (lead time, cycle time, flow efficiency), the two tools that surface the wait (Little's Law and the cumulative flow diagram), and what changes when a team stops reporting busyness and starts reporting flow.

## The single number that exposes the wait

Flow efficiency is the share of an item's total journey that is actual work rather than waiting: work time divided by work time plus waiting time [^2]. Most processes that have not been deliberately optimized for flow sit below 15 percent, meaning more than 85 percent of an item's lead time is spent in a queue [^2].

For consulting, the mechanic matters more than the number. A marketplace engagement provides a clean worked example. One feature moved through four departments over a total lead time of 216 hours: 24 hours of hands-on work and 192 hours of waiting between handoffs, an 11 percent flow efficiency [^2]. The popular fix in that situation is to hire more people at the slowest step. It fails, because the bottleneck step is not where the time goes. Even if extra people remove 75 percent of that step's work time, the overall lead time barely moves, because the bulk of it was never work at all [^2]. Adding people to a waiting process just produces more people who are waiting [^2]. That is the insight a firm should want on one slide: the delivery is not slow because people are slow; it is slow because work sits between the times people touch it.

## Lead time, cycle time, and the honesty problem

Flow analysis leans on two clocks that consulting reporting routinely conflates [^3]. Lead time runs from the moment the client requests something to the moment it is delivered. Cycle time runs from the moment work starts to the moment it is done. The difference is the backlog wait: the weeks an item sits before anyone touches it. A team can report a healthy cycle time while its clients wait months, because cycle time simply starts the clock after the wait is already over [^3]. When a delivery review shows rising cycle time, the number is hiding the more damning one. The client's experience is lead time, and lead time always includes the wait.

The distinction matters for what gets optimized. A delivery lead who tracks only cycle time will believe the delivery is healthy while a queue builds in the intake or refinement backlog, invisible to the metric. The honest number is lead time, and forecasting should hang off it, not off the narrower number. This is the same rigor our [delivery diagnostic for complicated versus complex work](/field-notes/complicated-vs-complex-delivery-diagnostic) applies to estimation: when the shape of the work is ambiguous, the reliable signal is measured flow data, not a single-point claim about effort. A lead-time distribution, drawn from the team's own history, beats an estimate that pretends to certainty [^5].

![Lead time versus cycle time: cycle time starts when work begins, so a team can look fast on cycle time while clients wait months on lead time](</diagrams/flow-metrics-consulting-delivery-2026/3-lead-vs-cycle-time.png>)

## Little's Law and the WIP reflex

The most counterintuitive and most useful law in flow analysis is Little's Law, which states that for a stable system, cycle time equals work in progress divided by throughput [^3]. It is a relationship of averages, not a prediction for an individual item, and it holds when the system is not trending [^4]. The practical reading is blunt: the number of items started at once determines how long each one takes. With twenty items in progress and a throughput of five per week, average cycle time is four weeks [^3]. Halve the work in progress to ten and, at the same throughput, cycle time drops to two weeks, with no one working harder [^3]. Nothing was sped up. There is simply less waiting in the queues.

Consulting teams violate this constantly, and the reflex comes from a well-intentioned place. A stakeholder asks for something, the team wants to be responsive, and it starts the work to show good faith. Every such start raises the work in progress, which lengthens every other item's cycle time [^3]. High work in progress is expensive in four compounding ways: every parallel item costs re-orientation time when a consultant switches back to it, feedback arrives late and the original author has moved on, half-finished work has produced no value and is fragile to a change in priority, and a blocked item hides among ten open ones instead of being escalated [^3]. The remedy is a WIP limit on each column of the board, set from the team's own throughput history, tightened until the board starts to hurt, because the pain is exactly the bottleneck being surfaced [^3].

![Little's Law in action: cutting work in progress from twenty items to ten at the same throughput halves cycle time from four weeks to two, without anyone working harder](</diagrams/flow-metrics-consulting-delivery-2026/2-littles-law.png>)

## Where the wait lives: the review step

Flow analysis does not just produce a number; it produces a location. The cumulative flow diagram strips away the daily story and shows, as a stack of colored bands over time, how many items sit in each state [^3]. A widening band is a queue growing in that state. For consulting engagements the queue that most often widens is the review and sign-off step. The consultants finish the work (the in-progress band holds steady or shrinks), and then it waits on a stakeholder, an approver, or a handoff, so the completed band stops moving toward the client [^3].

That discovery changes what a delivery lead optimizes. It is rarely worth adding consultants when the constraint is a single approver who reviews in weekly batches. It is worth negotiating the review cadence, giving the approver a standing slot, or shrinking the batch so review feedback arrives before context is lost. The engagement's [cross-team dependency coordination](/field-notes/cross-team-dependency-coordination-2026) becomes visible as a specific queue with an owner, rather than a vague feeling that work is "stuck somewhere." Findings that live in one firm's measured data carry weight that a status report cannot [^5].

## Measured results from delivery teams

The numbers above are not theory confined to textbooks; firms that run flow analysis on client delivery report the same shape. One consulting engagement that began with a value stream mapping session found the real bottleneck was not the team's capacity but an upstream dependency queue that had never been made visible [^5]. Work there routinely took three to four times longer than estimated [^5]. After the team switched to Kanban, set WIP limits calculated from its own throughput history, and started a periodic flow review where the team analyzed its own metrics instead of receiving top-down status, the delivery changed within six months [^5]. The 85th-percentile cycle time fell 42 percent, from 62 days to 36 days, throughput rose 59 percent, and average work in progress dropped from 14 items per team to 5 [^5].

A second engagement measured flow efficiency directly before and after the same treatment. On the measured team, flow efficiency sat at 15.86 percent, meaning the majority of each item's lifecycle was spent in a queue [^6]. After value stream mapping, WIP management, and a coached improvement cadence, the same team more than doubled its flow efficiency to 32.8 percent, while a DevOps team cut cycle time 57 percent and a separate SAP team cut it 24 percent [^6]. The point of citing numbers here is not to promise the same deltas; every team's starting point differs. It is to show that the move from reporting busyness to reporting flow is measurable, and that the wait is where the wins live [^5][^6].

## Making it concrete for a consulting team

The questionnaire for a delivery leader is short, and it should be run against a real engagement, not an idealized one. First, which clock are you reporting? If the dashboard shows cycle time, add the lead time number next to it, and note the difference out loud, because that difference is the backlog wait [^3]. Second, what is your flow efficiency? Time-box a quick measurement of one representative item through the whole pipeline, work versus wait, and accept the surprise [^2]. Third, what does your cumulative flow diagram show widening? That band names the bottleneck, and it is usually the review or sign-off step, not the work [^3][^5]. Fourth, where are your WIP limits? If no column has one, the team is running unconstrained, which is the functional equivalent of starting everything at once [^3].

Then run the change as a habit rather than a project. Set WIP limits from measured throughput, hold a standing flow review where the team reads its own cumulative flow diagram and its own cycle-time scatterplot, and let the team propose the lever [^3]. This mirrors the move our [sprint planning piece](/field-notes/sprint-planning-routing-review-2026) argues for: planning stops being a rationing ritual and becomes routing work to where the constraint actually is. A delivery organization that manages flow does not need to ask "who is the bottleneck?" The data answers it, in the colour of the widest band, every week.

The shift is from reporting how busy the team is to reporting how fast the client gets value. Utilization will not go away; it is a cost control and it belongs in the P&L. But it is a resource number, and it has been standing in for a delivery number it cannot answer. Flow efficiency, lead time, and the queue that does not move are the delivery numbers, and they are all measurable from the board and the ticketing system a consulting team already uses. The firm that starts measuring them stops selling the wait, and that is a different, better delivery.
