Every number on an engagement's dashboard can come back green. Milestones met, scope delivered, sign-off signed, invoice paid. Then a year passes and nobody can say what changed in the business.

Gartner's October survey of 2,002 CIOs and technology executives found that only 13 percent report their enterprise has realized significant or transformational value from AI, and that the chance of any single use case succeeding is about one in five [^1]. PMI's 2026 Pulse of the Profession puts a number on the same pattern in ordinary project work: 31 percent of complex projects fail to achieve the full scope of their originally intended benefits, more than double the 12 percent reported in 2024 [^2]. Both figures describe the same failure, and it is not a delivery failure. The output was produced. The benefit was never claimed.

## The scoreboard measures the half the team controls

A delivery team is measured on quantities it controls: milestones, scope, budget, velocity, sign-off. A benefit is a result it does not control. Gartner's analyst Alicia Mullery put the distinction in five words at the October symposium: "Volume is not value," adding that AI is "creating a reckoning for organizations that lack fundamental disciplines, such as defining use-case value ahead of time and establishing criteria for when to walk away" [^1].

Those criteria are the part most organizations skip. Gartner found that CIOs whose organizations have a rule for when a pilot should not move into production are 2.3 times more likely to be high performers, and those with a rule for abandoning low-value initiatives already in production are 2.6 times more likely. Yet only 31 percent and 17 percent respectively report having either practice in place [^1].

PMI's research shows the same erosion from the inside. In complex projects, 61 percent of project professionals report experiencing some form of value loss as a result of complexity, and four in five complex projects see fallout that is not visible at the time: strategic drift, diminished value, teams slowly burning out [^2]. None of it shows up on a project dashboard until the engagement is over. Neither does the reporting problem we wrote about in [percent-complete status reporting](/field-notes/percent-complete-status-reporting-2026), where the same optimism is dressed as progress.

## Output, outcome, benefit: three different addresses

The three words get used interchangeably, which is how the loss stays invisible. An output is what the team builds and hands over. An outcome is the change in how people work once the output is in use, and operations owns it. A benefit is the measurable value that change creates, which the business owns, and which is usually measured months after the project closes.

The asymmetry is structural, not a discipline problem. PMI's benefits research notes that organizations running projects for external customers often conclude that benefits are the customer's responsibility, since "the contract with the customer is to produce the deliverables, and it will be the customer who obtains the benefits from them" [^3]. In the same study, fewer than 40 percent of practitioners reported that project managers were accountable for identifying the benefits to be delivered [^3].

Naming the benefit early is the intervention with the clearest evidence behind it. When benefits were identified before the project started, 74 percent of projects met their goals and business intent, against 48 percent where they were not identified. When formal project management was used to run the identification process itself, that rose to 80 percent against 54 percent [^3].

## A benefit profile, not a wish

"Improve operational efficiency" cannot be reviewed. It has no baseline, so no movement can be proved, and no owner, so nobody is answerable when it does not move. A benefit profile is the smallest object that survives a review, and it carries six fields: the statement, the baseline, the target, the measurement method, the owner, and the realization date.

A worked version reads like this. Statement: order activation time. Baseline: nine working days, measured in March. Target: three working days by the end of Q2. Measurement: the median from the order system, not a satisfaction survey. Owner: the Head of Order Operations, a named person in the business rather than the IT sponsor who funded the work. Realization date: a first check 90 days after go-live, then a second at 12 months.

The owner field is where most profiles die, and the guidance is explicit about why. Benefits "are typically realized late in a project's life cycle," and "the responsibilities for delivery can often sit outside the project team, even occasionally entirely outside a department" [^4]. If the owner is the person who commissioned the work rather than the person whose numbers move, the review has nobody to argue with.

Two fields are usually left out of consulting charters and belong in them. The first is the disbenefit: the negative effect the change creates, which the assurance guidance requires to be valued and monitored in the same way as the benefit, and netted off the net benefits position [^4]. A process that speeds up order handling but pushes exceptions onto a support desk carries a disbenefit with a real cost, and leaving it out makes the business case look healthier than it is.

The second is timing. Benefits management runs the whole life of the work, not the delivery phase. The guidance sets five stages mapped to five assurance gates: identify benefits, value and appraise, plan to realize, work to realize, review performance. UK appraisal guidance requires benefits to be valued at the outline business case stage, with a realization plan and monitoring arrangements in place by the full business case [^5]. The gate that matters most for a consulting team is the third, whose key question is simply "is a robust plan to realize the benefits in place?" [^4]. A plan, with owners and metrics, is what separates a business case from a wish list.

## Review it after go-live, or it was never a benefit

The financial case for this discipline is not small. Organizations with high benefits realization maturity waste 67 percent less of the money invested in projects and programs than those with low maturity: $54 million per $1 billion invested against $166 million. The same research found that 45 percent more of their projects meet their original goals and business intent, and that 83 percent of organizations still lack maturity in the practice [^3].

For a consulting team, that translates into three things written into the engagement before work starts and honored after the invoice:

1. A named business owner for every material benefit, inside the client's organization rather than the delivery team.
2. A measurement that already exists in a system the client runs, with the baseline captured before the change.
3. A review date inside the exit criteria, typically 90 days after go-live and again at 12 months.

Gartner's in-flight counterpart is the stopping rule. A team that has agreed in advance what would make it halt a pilot before production, or abandon a low-value initiative already running, has built the same discipline facing the other way [^1]. Both amount to one commitment: the value claim is written down, owned, and reviewed, instead of assumed at kickoff and mourned at the post-mortem.

We put the benefits review into the [handover package](/field-notes/handover-hypercare-exit-criteria-2026) alongside the runbooks, because the exit criteria and the value claim are the same conversation. A system going live is an output. Whether the client can name what changed is the only thing that ever justified the spend.
