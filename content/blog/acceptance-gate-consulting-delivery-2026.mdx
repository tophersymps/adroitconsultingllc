A team can do everything its Definition of Done asks and still hand over a piece of work the client never uses. It can merge clean code, pass every automated test, clear code review, and meet every item on the team's own quality bar, and that bar says nothing about whether the person who pays the invoice considers the work acceptable. The two judgments are different things, and the distance between them is the single most expensive gap in consulting delivery.

Think about what the Definition of Done actually commits to. The Scrum Guide defines it as a formal description of the state the Increment must reach to meet the product's quality measures, and it makes that commitment a contract between the Developers and the Increment[^1]. Done here means the team's standards are met. It is a technical contract. It says nothing about the client standing in their own environment, running their own users against the work, and reaching a verdict.

That verdict is acceptance. It is a separate decision, made by a different person, against a different standard. Delivery is not complete when the code is integrated. It is complete when the client says the work does what they asked, in their context, to the standard they can afford to live with. Until that happens, the project has shipped work but not closed value.

This is the acceptance gate, and it is the real end of any engagement.

## The cost is not in the build

The accepted story about project failure is that the build is where things go wrong. The data paints a different picture. McKinsey and Oxford examined more than 5,400 large IT projects, those with initial budgets above roughly $15 million, and found they run 45 percent over budget and 7 percent over time while delivering 56 percent less value than predicted[^2]. The overruns are the visible symptom. The value loss is the quiet one. Software projects carry the highest overrun rates of any project type in that sample[^3].

A project can come in on schedule and on budget and still fail on value. That gap is not a build failure. It is an acceptance failure, and it shows up precisely where the team's handoff and the client's expectations stop lining up.

The economics make the same point from the other direction. Boehm's 1981 dataset, assembled at TRW and corroborated by IBM, GTE, and the Bell Labs Safeguard program, priced the relative cost of fixing a defect by the phase where it gets caught[^4]. A defect introduced in requirements and caught there cost roughly $1. Caught in design, $5 to $10. In code, $10 to $20. In development testing, $50 to $100. In acceptance testing, $100 to $200. In operations, $200 to $1,000[^4]. The slope is the story: every phase you push a problem later, the fix gets an order of magnitude more expensive.

![Defect fix cost rises steeply by the phase where the problem is caught, from about one dollar at requirements to hundreds at acceptance and operations](</diagrams/acceptance-gate-consulting-delivery-2026/2-defect-cost-curve.png>)

Two honest caveats make the curve more useful, not less. First, the exact multipliers are heuristic. The tidy 1:10:100 framing is a downstream popularization from the IBM Systems Sciences Institute, and it obscures Boehm's ranges and his project-type breakdowns[^4]. Second, Boehm and Basili's 2001 revision found the slope flattens meaningfully for small, agile, CI/CD-equipped teams, closer to a 5:1 gradient, because the design context is still fresh when a defect is caught minutes after commit[^4][^5]. For large, high-dependability, regulated systems, the original steep curve holds or steepens[^5].

Here is what survives both caveats. Late detection still costs more than early detection. The direction never reversed, in either Boehm's data or the 2001 update. And the acceptance phase sits at the expensive end of that slope, which is exactly where a consulting team does not want to discover it had the wrong idea about what the client wanted.

## The gate has two sides

The reason the acceptance gate is so easy to miss is that both sides of it look like completion.

On the team side sits the Definition of Done, a checklist the team owns and controls. Code merged, tests green, review passed, no known defects. All of it is true and all of it is inside the team's authority to produce. It is a commitment by the Developers for the Increment[^1]. When the team checks every box, it has genuinely finished the work as it defined the work.

On the client side sits acceptance, and none of its checks are inside the team's control. Does it do what I asked for, not what you built? Does it work for my users in my environment, with my data, at my volume? Can my people use it without retraining the whole department? Is the change one I am willing to own and defend? The client answers these questions, and the answers often have nothing to do with whether the tests passed.

The confusion is that most teams treat acceptance as a synonym for an acceptance test, a UAT round at the end of a project where a user clicks through a scripted flow and signs off. That is a ritual, and it consistently misleads. A UAT execution at the end is a quality pass over work the team already believes is finished. It is framed as verification, so it rarely surprises anyone, because the sessions are designed to confirm rather than to challenge.

Real acceptance is not a test phase. It is a negotiation about meaning, and it has to start before the first line of code is written.

![The acceptance gate sits between the team's Definition of Done and the client's acceptance, and the two can diverge even when the build is flawless](</diagrams/acceptance-gate-consulting-delivery-2026/1-done-vs-accepted.png>)

## Define acceptance before you build

The team that wants to close the gate cleanly writes the acceptance criteria before the work starts, in language the client can say yes or no to. This is distinct from the Definition of Done. The DoD is the team's standards for its own work, the quality bar for every Increment[^1]. Acceptance criteria are per-item, behavioral, and client-side: they describe what observable outcome proves the request was satisfied. The scrum.org learning series draws exactly this line, between the DoD's team-standard function and the acceptance criteria's per-backlog-item verification function[^1].

A well-written acceptance criterion is a sentence a non-technical client can read and then watch for. It names the actor, the action, the condition, and the verifiable result. This sits downstream of the requirements work: if the requirements do not produce checkable criteria, the acceptance gate has nothing to audit against. [We wrote about that requirements bottleneck](/field-notes/ai-requirements-bottleneck-2026). Here is one from a real consulting engagement: "When a requisitioner submits a purchase order over the approval threshold, the system routes the request to the delegated approver, and the approver sees the request in their queue within one minute." Every clause in that sentence is observable. The client can audit each one. There is no room for interpretation drift between what the team built and what the client meant.

Critically, the client writes or approves these criteria. The consultant drafts them from the requirements, but the client confirms them, because the whole point is to capture the client's standard, not the team's guess at it. This is where the value gap closes before it can widen. McKinsey's finding that projects deliver 56 percent less value than predicted is, in large part, a statement about acceptance criteria that were never written down as checkable outcomes, so nobody could tell the difference between delivered and bought[^2].

## Run the gate as a cadence, not an event

Acceptance criteria written up front buy nothing unless they are verified continuously. The consulting team that treats acceptance as a single end-of-project event has already accepted the expensive end of Boehm's curve. The team that runs the gate every sprint cuts the distance to a handful of days.

Four practices separate a cadence from a ceremony.

![Acceptance runs as a cadence when criteria are checked each sprint, not a single end-of-project UAT event that surfaces surprises at the expensive end of the cost curve](</diagrams/acceptance-gate-consulting-delivery-2026/3-cadence-vs-event.png>)

First, demo the acceptance criteria, not the tech. At the end of each sprint, walk the client through the criteria as completed items, one by one, using the words they wrote. If a criterion cannot be demonstrated because the work is not there yet, that item is not accepted, and it is transparently not done. The demo becomes an acceptance checkpoint rather than a show-and-tell.

Second, assign a single named acceptor. The client side of the gate collapses when responsibility for the verdict is diffuse. One person, usually the product owner or a named business owner, signs off on each criterion. This is the client's Definition of Accepted, and it needs an owner the same way the team's Definition of Done has the Developers as its committed owners[^1].

Third, rehearse the walkthrough before the client sees it. A consulting team that hits a live demo with a broken acceptance criterion wastes the client's trust and the sprint's credibility. Rehearsing against the written criteria also catches the gap between what the team thinks it built and what the criteria actually said, which is exactly the drift acceptance exists to catch.

![One acceptance criterion travels from being written with the client, to build, to a demo in the client's own words, to a sign-off by a single named acceptor](</diagrams/acceptance-gate-consulting-delivery-2026/4-criterion-loop.png>)

Fourth, track acceptance as its own backlog. Accepted work is a distinct state from done work, and it should be tracked that way. A dedicated column, a count of unaccepted items, and an aging metric for how long accepted items have waited make the gate visible. When the accepted count trails the done count, the team has a real problem to solve, not a milestone to decorate.

## The gate is where consulting value closes

For an internal product team, missing the gate means building something the market may not want. For a consulting team, it is more direct. The engagement closes, the invoice gets paid, and the reference gets a phone call, all of which depend on the client accepting that value actually landed in their operation. Fixed-fee work makes this brutally explicit. On a fixed-fee engagement, the team pays for every hour of rework the acceptance gap creates. Every criterion that was never written, every sprint where acceptance was treated as somebody else's job, every late discovery that pushed a fix to the acceptance phase, each of those is margin leaving the firm.

The delivery habit that protects margin is the same one that protects value. Write the client's acceptance criteria into the contract before build starts. Name the single acceptor. Demo against the criteria every sprint. Track accepted work as a distinct, visible state. When the team does this, the gate stops being a surprise at the end of the project and becomes a settled expectation that runs through every week of it.

None of this is glamorous. It is a decision about where to spend the team's attention, made before the build starts and repeated every sprint until the last criterion is accepted. The Definition of Done earns the team the right to call its work finished. Only acceptance earns the right to call it delivered, and delivered is the word that closes the sale.

## The one-line version

Done is a team standard; accepted is a client verdict, and the consulting team that writes the client's acceptance criteria up front, names one acceptor, and demos against those criteria every sprint closes the most expensive gap in delivery.
