A delivery review went sideways in the first ten minutes. The client's program director had the Gantt chart open, and she was pointing at a row near the bottom. "This integration task is three weeks behind," she said. "Why is nobody treating that as an emergency?" The room went quiet, because the honest answer was that the task did not matter. It sat on a branch of the schedule with six weeks of float, and every engineer in the room knew it. The project was on track. But nobody had told her which tasks actually decided the finish date, so she had no way to tell a real problem from a cosmetic one.

That is the gap this article closes. The critical path method (CPM) is the oldest and most durable tool in the project manager's kit for answering one question: which sequence of tasks, if any one of them slips, pushes the whole delivery date back by exactly that amount? Everything else in the schedule is, to some degree, negotiable. The critical path is not. For consulting teams that sell fixed dates and live on their reputation for hitting them, knowing the difference is the difference between managing a schedule and just watching one.

## What the critical path actually is

The critical path is the longest chain of dependent tasks from the start of a project to its finish[^1]. Because it is the longest chain, it sets the minimum possible duration of the project: you cannot finish faster than the longest sequence of work that has to happen in order[^2]. Every task on that chain has zero float, meaning it cannot slip at all without delaying the end date by the same amount[^3]. A task off the path has positive float, meaning it can absorb some delay before it starts to matter[^4].

The method was formalized in the late 1950s by Morgan R. Walker of DuPont and James E. Kelley Jr. of Remington Rand, and it has been a fixture of scheduling ever since[^5]. It is not a relic. The Project Management Institute still treats it as a core technique of the Schedule Management knowledge area, and it is tested on the PMP and CAPM exams[^6]. The reason it has survived is that it answers a question no other tool answers cleanly: of the dozens of tasks on a board, which ones actually control the date?

The key insight is that most tasks do not. A project is a network of paths, and only the longest one is critical[^7]. In a typical schedule, a task can be three weeks late and the project still lands on time, because that task sits on a branch with slack. The project manager who treats every late task as an emergency is spending attention on the wrong rows. The project manager who knows the critical path spends it exactly where a delay actually costs a day of the finish date.

## The four numbers that run the calculation

CPM works by computing four values for every activity: earliest start (ES), earliest finish (EF), latest start (LS), and latest finish (LF)[^8]. The first two come from a forward pass through the network, the second two from a backward pass, and the difference between them is the float.

The forward pass starts at the beginning of the project with an ES of zero for the first activity. For each activity, the earliest finish is the earliest start plus the duration, and the earliest start of any activity is the maximum earliest finish of its immediate predecessors[^9]. The reason it is the maximum, not the average, is that an activity waits on whichever predecessor finishes last. If two tasks feed a third, the third cannot start until both are done, so it starts when the slower one finishes.

The backward pass runs the same logic in reverse. It starts at the end of the project with the latest finish equal to the earliest finish of the final activity, and works backward. The latest start of an activity is its latest finish minus its duration, and the latest finish of any activity is the minimum latest start of its immediate successors[^10]. The float of an activity is the difference between its latest and earliest values, either LS minus ES or LF minus EF[^11]. An activity with zero float is on the critical path. An activity with positive float can slip by that many days before it starts to push the end date.

There are two flavors of float, and the distinction matters in practice. Total float is how long an activity can be delayed without delaying the project's finish date. Free float is the narrower measure: how long it can be delayed without delaying the start of the next activity[^12]. Free float is always less than or equal to total float[^13]. A task can have three days of total float but only one day of free float, because delaying it further would still be fine for the overall deadline but would start bumping into the next task's schedule[^14]. When you are deciding whether a late task is a real problem, total float is the number that tells you whether the finish date moves. Free float tells you whether the immediate downstream work is affected.

## Dependencies are the whole game

The critical path is only as good as the dependency map it is built on, and most of the value of CPM comes from getting that map right. It is the scheduling half of the delivery picture that the sprint-planning and estimation articles on this site cover from the other side: those pieces decide how much work fits in a sprint and how long a task takes, and the critical path decides how those durations and dependencies chain into a finish date[^42]. Dependencies define the relationships between activities, and there are four types[^15]:

- Finish-to-Start (FS): the next task cannot start until the previous one finishes. This is the default and by far the most common, accounting for roughly 90 percent of task relationships in typical projects[^16].
- Start-to-Start (SS): the next task can start once the previous one starts, without waiting for it to finish.
- Finish-to-Finish (FF): the next task cannot finish until the previous one finishes.
- Start-to-Finish (SF): the next task cannot finish until the previous one starts. This is rare and often a sign of a modeling error.

The most common mistake in building the map is adding dependencies that are not real. Making "write the report" depend on "order office supplies" because they happen to be on the same person's list creates a false critical path and an unnecessary constraint[^17]. The test for every dependency is simple: does this task genuinely need that task to finish first? If the answer is no, the link is invented, and it is quietly making your schedule look tighter and more fragile than it is.

The other common mistake is modeling only the immediate predecessor. If task C depends on task B, and task B depends on task A, then B is the immediate predecessor of C, not A[^18]. Listing A as a direct dependency of C does not change the math much, but it clutters the network and makes the critical path harder to read. Keep the map to the immediate relationships and let the path emerge from the chain.

## Why the path changes under you

The critical path is not a static artifact you compute once at kickoff and file away. It is dynamic, and it can change as the project moves[^19]. Durations change as work runs faster or slower than planned. Dependencies get added or removed. New tasks appear. Resources become constrained. Any of these can move the longest path, and a task that had float last week can be critical this week.

The most common reason the path shifts is resource constraints. CPM in its pure form assumes unlimited resources, which is why the calculated critical path can disagree with reality when a team is small[^20]. If the schedule shows electrical and plumbing running in parallel but only one qualified contractor exists, one of them waits, and the wait creates a new critical path that is resource-dependent rather than duration-dependent[^21]. This is the gap that resource levelling and the critical chain method exist to close, but the practical lesson is simpler: when you recalculate the path, do it against the real resource picture, not the idealized one.

The practical discipline is to recalculate the critical path weekly, or after any significant change, and to watch the near-critical paths as closely as the critical one[^22]. A task with one or two days of float can become critical the moment anything slips. The Institute of Project Management recommends treating any path with less than five days of float as a watchlist item, because those are the ones most likely to become critical next[^23]. The project manager who only watches the current critical path is always one slip behind.

## Negative float is the alarm that means something

Float is not always positive. Negative float occurs when a task's late start date is earlier than its early start date, which means the activity is already behind schedule and will delay the finish date if nothing changes[^24]. It usually appears when a hard constraint, like a fixed finish date, meets a task that cannot fit inside it. The scheduling engine works backward from the deadline, finds that the task would have to start in the past, and reports a negative number[^25].

Negative float is not a failure of the tool. It is the tool telling you the truth about an impossible plan. The causes are familiar: an unrealistic deadline set by a stakeholder, a hard "must finish on" constraint, tightly linked tasks with no slack, a resource that arrives later than planned, or scope added after the baseline with no time added to absorb it[^26]. Each of these produces the same signal, and the signal is worth taking seriously. Ignoring negative float is like ignoring a check engine light: the damage piles up while you keep driving[^27].

The fix is not to delete the number. It is to trace the root cause and change the plan. That means compressing the schedule, re-sequencing tasks, or renegotiating the deadline, and doing it early enough that the change is a plan adjustment rather than a crisis[^28]. A project manager who sees negative float and treats it as a signal to act is doing the job. One who sees it and hopes it resolves on its own is gambling.

## Compressing the schedule without breaking it

When the finish date has to move earlier, the only place to act is the critical path. Compressing a non-critical task does nothing to the end date, because the float absorbs it. Two techniques exist, and they work in opposite ways[^29].

Fast tracking runs critical path activities in parallel instead of sequentially, overlapping work that was planned to happen one after the other[^30]. It is the right choice when the activities have low dependency between them, and it is risky because starting a task before its predecessor finishes means building on incomplete work, and rework can erase the time the overlap was meant to save[^31]. Crashing adds resources to critical path activities to complete them faster, hiring an extra person or working longer hours[^32]. It is the right choice when the constraint is capacity and the task genuinely responds to more resources, and it usually increases cost[^33].

Both keep scope intact, and both act on the critical path directly[^34]. The discipline is to pick the technique that matches the constraint. If the task is slow because it is waiting on a person, crashing helps. If it is slow because the work is sequential and could be parallel, fast tracking helps. Applying the wrong one spends money or adds risk without moving the date.

## How consulting teams run it as a habit

The critical path method earns its keep in consulting delivery, where the finish date is often a contractual commitment and the margin lives or dies on hitting it. The practice is not complicated, but it is a habit, and it has four parts.

First, build the dependency map before work starts, during planning, while the schedule can still be adjusted[^35]. CPM is a planning tool first. Once work is underway, it is harder to change the path, so the earlier it is built, the more decisions it can influence.

Second, recalculate the path on a fixed cadence, weekly at minimum, and after any significant change[^36]. The path that was critical at kickoff is not necessarily the path that is critical in week six. Recalculation is cheap in modern tools, which compute it automatically, and it is the only way to keep the answer current[^37].

Third, use the path as the communication tool it is. The critical path is the clearest way to explain a schedule to a stakeholder: these are the tasks that determine the finish date, here is where we have flexibility and where we do not, and if this critical task slips by X days, the project slips by X days[^38]. That is the answer the program director in the opening scene needed, and it is the answer that turns a panicked review into a calm one.

Fourth, integrate it with the risk register. The critical path highlights the highest schedule risks, because a delay on a critical activity is a delay to the project, while a delay on a non-critical one is absorbed[^39]. Mapping risks to critical activities, and keeping contingency on the critical path, is where schedule risk management actually happens. The risk register article on this site covers the discipline of running risk as a weekly habit; the critical path is the map that tells you which risks are worth the attention[^40].

![Run the critical path as a weekly habit: four practices that turn CPM from a kickoff artifact into the operating map of delivery, from building the dependency map before work starts to integrating the path with the risk register](</diagrams/critical-path-dependency-scheduling-2026/4-weekly-habit.png>)

## The path is the plan

The critical path method is sixty-five years old, and it has not been replaced because the problem it solves has not gone away[^41]. Every project still has a longest chain of dependent work, and that chain still decides the finish date. What has changed is that modern tools compute the path automatically, so the barrier to using it is no longer the math. It is the discipline of looking at the answer.

For a consulting team, the payoff is concrete. You stop treating every late task as an emergency and start treating the ones that matter as the only emergencies. You can tell a client, with confidence, which tasks decide the date and which ones have room to move. And when a schedule has to compress, you know exactly where to act and which technique fits. The critical path is not a chart to file at kickoff. It is the operating map of the delivery, and the teams that read it are the ones that hit their dates.
