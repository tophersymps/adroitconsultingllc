Ninety-eight percent of companies have deployed AI agents or plan to. Seventeen percent have visibility into how those agents perform or whether they conform. Forty-eight percent lack clear roles and responsibilities for governing them.

Those numbers come from the SAP LeanIX Agentic AI Survey 2026, which PMI cited in its October announcement [^12]. Read together they describe a gap, and the last two months were mostly about closing it. The industry stopped shipping capability and started shipping constraint.

This edition covers two months, since the last roundup ran on September 4. In that window Salesforce began registering agent identities and metering their calls, Kubernetes started writing a specification for how applications behave on a cluster, Figma made the design system an input an agent can read, and two model makers halved the price of competent work. Different vendors, different layers, one move: give the agent an identity, draw a boundary around it, count what it does, and write a test that says whether it did the job.

## Salesforce: an agent now needs a badge and a meter

Dreamforce on September 15 renamed the company's interface strategy. Headless 360, announced at TDX, shipped as AIforce, with three surfaces: Claudeforce inside Claude, Slackforce inside Slack, and Agentforce Coworker inside Salesforce itself [^1]. The pitch is that the platform is no longer the screen. It is the data, workflows, and permissions that any authorized agent can reach.

Then came the part that changes budgets. Salesforce published an impact note on September 17 saying that in November it will ship agentic registration and a billing model for Headless Toolkit traffic [^2]. The mechanics are specific enough to read twice. Admins register each agent on the Platform, and registration carves out a discrete identity instead of letting the agent act under the credentials of the person it assists. Admins can then attach a narrower permission set to that identity. From there, every successful call a registered agent makes, over MCP or a direct API, is metered as a Headless Platform Interaction and drawn against Flex Credits in Digital Wallet.

Three details decide the work. The multipliers are not announced, so the rate card reads TBA, and Salesforce owes 30 days of notice before metering starts. Sandboxes, scratch orgs, and Developer Edition orgs are exempt, so a team can build and test without drawing credits. And the deadline is administrative rather than technical: customers buying from now on must register every agent that uses Salesforce APIs within three months of the notice, while existing customers migrate at renewal.

![The path from agent to metered call in AIforce. An admin registers the agent on the Platform, which issues a discrete identity carrying a narrower permission set than a human user has. Every successful call over MCP or a direct API is then counted as a Headless Platform Interaction against Flex Credits in Digital Wallet. Sandboxes, scratch orgs, and Developer Edition orgs sit outside the meter, and existing customers move onto the model at renewal.](</diagrams/state-of-the-stack-november-2026/2-agent-to-meter.png>)

The demand side explains the timing. On November 4 Salesforce said Agentforce Coworker usage grew 21 times in three months, past 185,000 users, and put a $50 per user per month price on it [^3]. Agents are no longer a pilot line item. That is precisely why the platform wants them counted, and why an integration that quietly ran under a human's credentials is about to become visible, attributed, and billable.

If you run agents against Salesforce, the work in front of you is inventory rather than engineering: list every agent and every MCP client, decide which identity each one should carry, and size the call volume before someone hands you a rate card. Our earlier piece on the [API request budget](/field-notes/salesforce-api-request-budget-2026) covers the accounting half of the same problem.

## AI: the floor fell out of the middle tier

Two releases changed more architecture decisions than any frontier announcement, because both hit the price of work that runs millions of times.

On October 7 Anthropic shipped Claude Haiku 5.5 at $0.10 per million input tokens and $0.50 per million output, roughly 90 percent below Haiku 4.5 for prompts up to 100,000 tokens, a band Anthropic says covered about 90 percent of requests to its previous small model [^4]. The capability number is the interesting one: 72.4 percent on OSWorld 2.1 against 15.7 percent for Haiku 4.5 and 48.9 percent for GPT-6 Luna, with Terminal-Bench 4.0 at 39.2 percent against 16.4 percent for Luna. A small model that could not drive a browser a year ago now finishes long multi-step computer tasks at a tenth of the price.

On October 26 OpenAI shipped GPT-6.1 Terra at $1 input and $5 output per million tokens, half the standard price of GPT-5.6 Terra and GPT-6.1 Sol, and said it performs on par with GPT-6 Astra at one-tenth its price per token [^5]. Cached input at $0.05 per million makes repeated context nearly free, which is the shape an agent loop needs.

![Two October releases pulling in opposite directions. Claude Haiku 5.5 sits at 10 cents input and 50 cents output per million tokens and scores 72.4 percent on OSWorld 2.1, where GPT-6 Luna scores 48.9 percent at the same price and Haiku 4.5 scored 15.7 percent. On the right, Gemini 4 Argon went to everyone on October 13 at 2 dollars input and 10 dollars output, rising to 4 and 20 on November 30, and Gemini 4 Carbon was announced on October 28 at 88.2 percent on SEC-bench Pro, released first to a small group of cyber defenders through the Fairwind Program.](</diagrams/state-of-the-stack-november-2026/3-price-capability-split.png>)

The top of the market moved the other way, and got more expensive doing it. Gemini 4 Argon went generally available on October 13 at $2 input and $10 output per million tokens for the introductory period, rising to $4 and $20 on November 30 [^6]. Two weeks later Google announced Gemini 4 Carbon, reporting 88.2 percent on SEC-bench Pro, at the same introductory price, with an access list that starts with a small group of cyber defenders through its Fairwind Program rather than with everyone. Frontier capability is being staged behind programs now, not published to a public endpoint on day one.

Put the two directions together and routing becomes the highest-leverage decision in an AI budget. On output tokens the spread between the small tier at 50 cents and the frontier at $10 is 20 times, and it doubles to 40 times when Argon's introductory rate expires at the end of November. The expensive model is for the small share of calls that need it. The cheap tier is enough for summarization, extraction, subagents, and browser work. Teams that route by task instead of by vendor loyalty are cutting spend without touching quality, and teams that pinned a single model last spring are paying twice for the same output.

## Web: the recommended default arrived with a critical inside it

Next.js 16.4 shipped on October 6 and changed the framework's advice, not just its features. Cache Components, the opt-in caching model introduced across the 16.x line, became the recommendation for every Next.js app, the default in create-next-app, and the announced default in Next.js 17 [^7]. Caching turns declarative: you annotate the parts of the tree that may be cached instead of inheriting implicit behavior across the app.

Eight days later the new caching path produced the month's worst vulnerability. On October 14 Vercel shipped an out-of-band release, 16.4.1 on Active LTS and 15.5.28 on Maintenance LTS, fixing two critical unauthenticated remote code executions and one high-severity denial of service [^8]. The first critical sits inside `use cache` functions that return multiple typed arrays, affects 16.0.0 through 16.4.0, and has no workaround. The second arrives through SVG handling in the Image Optimization API and the bundled sharp library, and reaches back to 10.0.0. Vercel rated both code-execution issues 9.5 [^13].

![The October 14 Next.js security release as an exposure table. An unauthenticated remote code execution in use cache functions returning multiple typed arrays affects 16.0.0 through 16.4.0 with no workaround. An unauthenticated remote code execution through SVG image optimization affects 10.0.0 through 15.5.27 and 16.0.0 through 16.4.0. A high severity denial of service in App Router applications with at least one Server Action affects 13.0.0 through 15.5.27 and 16.0.0 through 16.4.0. All three are fixed in 16.4.1 and 15.5.28.](</diagrams/state-of-the-stack-november-2026/4-nextjs-exposure.png>)

The timing is the lesson, and it is not really about Next.js. A caching default changes the blast radius of a bug, because the affected code path now runs everywhere by design instead of where someone opted in. The teams least exposed were the ones that had pinned a patch and could rebuild in a day, which is the discipline the August release was meant to teach.

The other half of the month points the same way. On October 28 Vercel published an agent skill that reads Turbopack's module graph and proposes import boundaries to defer, and reported close to two megabytes of compressed JavaScript removed across its own properties [^9]. The framework now ships tooling written for agents as a first-class consumer. That raises the review bar rather than lowering it, since an agent that can restructure an import graph can restructure it wrongly, and the defense is the bundle diff someone reads before merging. The [import graph walkthrough](/field-notes/nextjs-bundle-import-graph-2026) covers how to read that diff.

For this week, three things matter more than any release note: upgrade to 16.4.1 or 15.5.28, run `npm ls sharp` and confirm 0.35.5 or later if you are staying on 15, and rebuild rather than hot-reload.

## DevOps and design: the same move, one layer down

Kubernetes opened a Workload Conformance Working Group on October 27, and the charter is unusually concrete about what it refuses to accept [^10]. It will produce a specification of how workloads behave at runtime for each minor version, a method for testing each requirement against a running workload, and upstream tooling that runs those tests. Scope spans security, resilience during disruptions, resource use, networking, storage, API usage, and observability. One rule is already fixed: every requirement needs an objective test that a binary can run, so nothing subjective enters the specification.

The gap is easy to underrate. Certified Kubernetes tests the cluster. The AI Conformance program tests whether a cluster can run AI workloads. Nothing tested the applications themselves, which is why operators still checked each workload by hand before a node drain or an upgrade. A stable first specification is two to three release cycles away, so the practical work for the next year is building the same evidence yourself: a named behavior per workload, a check that returns pass or fail, and a record of which of your workloads would survive a drain today.

Figma made a parallel move on October 15, in the layer where design decisions live [^11]. Code layers moved into open beta on all plans. Specs launched in open beta as a native container for context, Markdown that references the canvas instead of floating free of it. More important for anyone running a design system: guidelines can now attach to published libraries so an agent follows established patterns, code sources can be imported from a GitHub repository or an npm package so an agent builds against the real components, and a system map plus system check can trace dependencies and flag gaps such as missing descriptions and interactive states.

That is the Kubernetes idea in a different medium. A design system used to be documentation a person read. It is becoming a specification a machine reads, with a checker that reports where it is incomplete. If your library has no description on its interactive states, an agent will invent them, and the invention will look consistent while being wrong.

## Delivery: 98 percent running, 17 percent watching

PMI used its Global Summit on October 22 to announce three offerings, and the reason behind them is the sharpest statistic of the two months [^12]. Alongside a new Advanced Project Leader certification and an executive course on delivery conditions, PMI launched an e-learning course and build lab for scoping and governing agents. The release cites the same survey: 98 percent deployed or planned to deploy, 17 percent with visibility into performance or conformance, 48 percent without clear ownership.

PMI's own numbers describe the demand from the practitioner side. Seventy-seven percent of project professionals use AI for research and analysis, 69 percent for content and deliverables, 53 percent for risk identification, and only 19 percent are concerned their employer will replace them with AI. Its partner Prosci, in the same announcement, reports that roughly two in three workers now use AI regularly while the share of organizations getting the best results has not grown since 2024, with the friction in verifying output, designing governance, and coordinating adoption across teams.

Read the roundup against that gap and the pattern is hard to miss. Salesforce answered with a register and a meter. Kubernetes answered with a specification and a test. Figma answered with stored guidelines and a system check. Next.js answered with a patch cadence that fired twice in five weeks. None of those is an AI feature. All of them are the unglamorous half of running agents, and the organizations that spent September and October on that half can now say what their agents do, what they cost, and whether they did the job.

Four moves cover most of the ground, and none needs a new platform. Register every agent and give it its own identity and its own permission set. Write one objective check per agent that returns pass or fail, the way the Kubernetes group insists. Put a cost figure beside every agent instead of a seat count. Then review the output the way you review a pull request, because the 17 percent figure is a management problem before it is a technology one.

What we are watching for December: whether the Flex Credit multipliers land with enough notice to budget against, whether the first workload conformance draft names behaviors teams actually test, and whether the cheap tier holds its benchmark lead once agents use it for long runs rather than short ones.
