The integration review runs late into the afternoon. The front-end team demoed its rebuilt checkout page, the back-end team shipped its payments API, the data team moved order history into a warehouse. Every single team hit its sprint goal. The program is still four weeks behind, because the checkout page does not render without the payments API, and the payments API does not become safe to use until the identity team lands its token service, and that token service has been parked for two sprints behind a different product owner's priority list. Nobody lied in a status update. The individual sprint burn-downs are all green. The dependency between them was nobody's job, so it became everyone's problem [^1].

That is the failure mode this article is about. Teams that work together on one integrated product do not slip because their backlogs are too big; they slip because the work in one backlog depends on work in another, and that dependency is only tracked as an assumption, a shared look, or a hopeful word in a stand-up. The [critical path method](/field-notes/critical-path-dependency-scheduling-2026) names the sequence of tasks inside a single schedule that decides the finish date. It says nothing about the seams between teams, where the delivery actually lives in a scaled engagement. This is the cross-team coordination problem, and treating dependencies as real, owned work is the difference between a program that coordinates and one that just meets [^1][^4].

## Why cross-team dependencies are different from task dependencies

A dependency, at its simplest, is a logical relationship between two pieces of work: one task cannot start, or cannot finish, until another one does. The PMBOK Guide does not even define "dependency" directly; it calls it a logical relationship between two activities, or between an activity and a milestone [^3]. Project managers have managed these in sequence diagrams and Gantt charts for decades. Those work fine when two tasks sit inside the same schedule, owned by the same manager, tracked in the same tool. Finish-to-start, start-to-start: the ordering is visible and the owner can see when a predecessor slips [^3].

Cross-team dependencies break that tidy picture because the two ends live in different accountability structures. A cross-team dependency is when one team's task relies on another team's output, the classic front-end team waiting on the back-end team's APIs before it can build the user interface [^3]. The front-end team cannot move it, because the deliverable belongs to a different team, a different backlog, a different sprint, frequently a different product owner and a different manager. There is no single schedule that contains both ends. So the dependency is real, it is load-bearing, and it is mostly invisible to the tools each team uses to plan its own work [^1][^3].

The invisibility is the killer. Cross-team dependencies are exactly the kind that surface only when a task is blocked, discovered as a surprise in the middle of a sprint rather than named at the start [^4]. This is why they compound. When a dependency is undiscovered until work stops, the team upstream is already committed to other priorities, the downstream team is already idle-waiting, and the resolution has to fight an established backlog. The cost is not the delay itself; it is that the delay arrived after the plan had already been committed and communicated [^4][^5].

## The dependency matrix exposes the real structure

The tool that makes cross-team dependencies visible is the dependency structure matrix (DSM), a method formalized in 1981 by Don Steward. A DSM is a square matrix where each row and column represents a team or component, and a cell marks whether the row's work depends on the column's work. The matrix is read the same way a spreadsheet is: scan down a column to see everything that depends on that team, scan across a row to see everything that team depends on [^3].

The DSM earns its place because it surfaces two structures that a Gantt chart hides. The first is the bottleneck team. If five teams all depend on the same platform team, that column has five marks in it, and the matrix shows at a glance that one team is a single point of failure for the whole program. Any delay in that team propagates to five others. The second is the cycle. When work depends on itself through a chain of teams, A waits on B, B waits on C, C waits on A, the matrix shows a loop that no single team can resolve alone, because resolving it requires coordination, not effort [^3].

In agile environments, DSMs support program-level visibility. When teams rely on shared components or must deliver features in sync, mapping those dependencies supports incremental delivery and reduces rework, because the team sees which shared components are the real coordination surface [^3]. The matrix is not documentation; it is the load-bearing map of where this program can break.

## Coordination at scale: Scrum of Scrums and its limits

Once the dependencies are visible, the question is who coordinates them. The oldest answer is the Scrum of Scrums, a scaled agile technique for coordinating multiple teams working toward a common goal. It dates to 1996, when Scrum's creators Jeff Sutherland and Ken Schwaber used it to coordinate eight business units, each with multiple product lines, and synchronize the individual teams [^1]. The shape is a virtual team: each delivery team sends a delegate who can speak for the team and has the authority to make decisions or escalate, and those delegates meet regularly to work dependencies, risks, and integration challenges [^1].

The discipline matters more than the ceremony. Atlassian's guidance for running a Scrum of Scrums is blunt about what makes it work: keep the scaled daily scrum to fifteen minutes, hold it after the last team's stand-up, establish a working agreement and an agenda to keep it focused, track the number of days a team is blocked by impediments, and focus on delivering stories that have dependencies first so they unblock other teams [^1]. Notice what that list has in common. Every item is a way to make coordination observable. The meetings exist to surface the dependency and the blocked-day count is the metric that proves whether coordination is actually happening.

The delegate role is the load-bearing piece. A representative should have enough context to speak for their team and the authority to make decisions, not just report. Teams that send whoever happens to be free get a coordination meeting that produces notes and no decisions, because the person in the room cannot commit their team to anything [^1]. Rotating the role is a way to build broader skill across the team, but the delegate must always carry real decision authority or the whole structure stalls.

Scrum of Scrums is not the only scaling answer, and it has limits worth naming. Scrum.org's Nexus framework exists specifically to address what a set of independently-run Scrum teams cannot: it groups roughly three to nine Scrum teams working from a single product backlog to build an integrated increment at least once per sprint, and it exists to reduce the complexity created by cross-team dependencies [^6]. The difference is worth understanding. A Scrum of Scrums coordinates teams that keep their own backlogs. Nexus consolidates the backlog so the seams are inside one planning structure, not between several. For consulting engagements, the choice is usually driven by how much integration freedom the client will allow: independent backlogs with coordination, or a single backlog with a heavier planning ceremony [^1][^6].

## Treat dependencies as work, not communication

The single biggest shift a delivery lead can make is to stop treating a dependency as a communication concern and start treating it as a work item. A dependency that is only a conversation has no owner, no deadline, no acceptance criteria, and no way to fail visibly. A dependency that is a tracked work item does [^4][^4].

Concretely, that means every dependency gets the same treatment as a user story. It has a named owner on the upstream team who is accountable for the deliverable. It has a target completion date that ties back to the downstream team's need date, not the upstream team's convenience. It has acceptance criteria, the concrete thing the downstream team needs, so "finish the API" becomes "the POST /checkout endpoint returns a 200 with the order ID in under 400 milliseconds on a validated token." And it lives on a shared dependency board where blocked work percentage is visible, because the metric that tells you a program is healthy is not how much each team built, but how much of each team's work is sitting idle waiting on another team [^4][^4].

This is where the dependency matrix becomes an operating tool rather than an artifact. The matrix tells you which team is the bottleneck, and the work-item discipline tells you what that bottleneck team has actually committed to. The combination is how you catch the classic failure: a dependency that was "agreed" in a meeting but never entered any backlog, so six weeks later nobody can point to where it went. When a dependency is work, it cannot quietly evaporate, because it has an owner who gets asked about it, and a blocked count that goes up when it does not land [^4][^4].

The consulting-specific version of this matters. In a consulting engagement there is often no single manager who owns both the client team and the delivery team, and many dependencies are external, the client's approval process, a vendor's release, another consulting firm's integration. Those external dependencies do not follow the agile cadence and they are the ones most likely to slip silently [^4]. The fix is the same as for internal dependencies, only earlier and with more buffer: name the external deliverable, assign an owner who is accountable for chasing it, and build buffer time into the schedule around it, because you cannot renegotiate a vendor's release window the way you can renegotiate an internal sprint [^3][^4].

## Schedule the dependency, or design it away

Two follow-through disciplines decide whether dependency management actually shortens delivery: scheduling unblocking work first, and reducing the number of dependencies structurally.

**Schedule unblocking work first.** When a piece of work is a prerequisite for another team, its sequencing priority comes from the cost of the delay it imposes, not from its own size. This is the logic behind Weighted Shortest Job First (WSJF), the prioritization model used at scale to sequence work for maximum economic benefit by dividing relative cost of delay by relative job duration [^5]. You do not need the formal WSJF score to apply the intuition. You need the rule: a small unblocking task for another team outranks a large self-contained task for your own team, because the small task does not just finish; it unblocks everything waiting downstream [^5]. Programs that schedule unblocking work first see their blocked-work percentage drop, because work stops waiting on other teams and starts flowing [^4].

**Design dependencies away.** The most powerful dependency management is the one that makes the dependency unnecessary. Mature scaled organizations evolve from managing dependencies to designing for independence: modular architecture where teams own end-to-end slices, clear API contracts and shared services that let teams integrate without waiting on each other's implementation, and cross-functional teams aligned to value rather than to function [^4]. A well-defined API contract is a dependency that has been made safe: the front-end team can build against the contract the day it is signed, not the day the back-end implementation lands. Continuous integration and shared development environments do the same job at the code level, shrinking the big-bang dependencies that only appear at release time [^4][^4].

This is not abstract architecture theory under another name; it is delivery planning with the seams taken seriously. The teams that finish on time are not the ones with the most polished ceremonies. They are the ones that looked at their dependency matrix, found the four-dependency bottleneck team, either scheduled its unblocking work first or gave it a stable contract to build against, and then watched their blocked-work percentage fall.

## Build the dependency table before you build the plan

The practical starting point for any multi-team engagement is smaller than a framework. Before the first joint planning session, draw the dependency matrix. For each pair of teams, ask and write down: does team A's committed work depend on team B? When is that deliverable actually needed, not when would it be nice to have it? Who on team B owns it, has it in a backlog, and accepts being accountable for it? What concrete acceptance criteria let team A know it is safe to start? [^3]

Run that one exercise and the change is immediate. Dependencies that were "obvious" turn out to have no owner. Deliverables that everyone assumed were next sprint are scheduled three sprints out on the upstream team. The bottleneck team names the five things it is genuinely accountable for and pushes back on the other four. None of this requires adopting a scaling framework, buying new tooling, or changing how the teams run their sprints. It requires taking the seams seriously enough to make them visible [^3][^1].

Keep the review cadence honest. The dependency matrix goes stale the moment it is not maintained, because new dependencies emerge as the work evolves. Regular scaled syncs and status reviews keep the dependency landscape current, so a change in one team's plan surfaces immediately for the team downstream before it becomes a surprise [^3][^1]. The blocked-work percentage is the number to watch, because it is the one metric that directly measures whether the teams are actually unblocking each other or just meeting politely [^4].

## The coordination is the delivery

The one-line version is that a multi-team engagement is only as fast as its slowest seam. A program with three excellent teams and an unmanaged dependency between two of them is slower than a program with two adequate teams and no seams left unexamined, because the seams are where the idle time spends itself [^1][^4].

The practices are all well-established: the dependency matrix to make the structure visible, the Scrum of Scrums or a single integrated backlog to coordinate it, dependency-as-work to give every seam an owner and a blocked count, unblocking-first scheduling, and API contracts and modular boundaries to shrink the number of seams over time. None of it is new or exotic. What is rare in practice is taking the seams seriously enough to do it, to name the dependency, own it, schedule it, and report it with the same discipline as a story. In scaled delivery, that is the difference between a program that meets and a program that coordinates.
