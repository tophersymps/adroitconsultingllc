The button label that fit perfectly in English is the one that breaks your product somewhere else. "Submit" is six characters, two of them visually narrow. In German it becomes "Absenden", eight characters with a wider footprint. In Portuguese, a small message like this can run three times as long. The fixed-width button you sized for the English string now clips text, or wraps it onto two lines that overflow a row built on the assumption that nothing grows. That one word is where a design system stops being a system and starts being a patchwork of per-language exceptions.

This is not a translation chore that a vendor solves by swapping strings. It is a layout problem baked into every component you have already shipped, and it is the reason many design systems quietly rot the moment a product goes global. The good news is that the fixes are concrete, testable, and mostly already built into the web platform. The bad news is that you almost certainly have not applied them, because your components were designed and tested in a single script, a single direction, and a single set of typographic habits.

This article walks the design-engineering side of localization: what actually happens to a layout when text expands, why right-to-left languages are a mirroring problem not a text problem, which CSS tools do the work for you, and what a genuinely locale-aware design token system looks like. It is the companion to the accessibility testing guide, because many of the same failures, truncated text and broken reading order, have WCAG implications, the same class of failure our [accessibility testing layers article](/field-notes/accessibility-testing-layers-ci-2026) covers. By the end you can audit your own component library for the four failure modes and know exactly what to change.

## Why expansion is the load-bearing fact

Before any design decision, internalize the central number: translated text rarely stays the same length, and small strings expand the most. The W3C Internationalization group publishes the reference set, drawn from IBM's global design guidelines. An English string of up to 10 characters typically expands 200-300% in translation. An 11-20 character string expands 180-200%. A 21-30 character string expands 160-180%. Even long strings over 70 characters still grow, by about 130 percent[^1]. The shorter the label, the larger the relative growth, and the shorter the label, the more likely it is to be squeezed into a fixed-width slot like a form entry, a tab, or a button[^1].

The practical ranges line up across the industry. Most European languages translated from English expand 15-30%, while Dutch and German can go 35% or higher[^2]. German, Finnish, and Hungarian expand significantly, and German text can grow 30-50%[^6]. Finnish and Hungarian also build long compound nouns: the English "Input processing features" becomes the single German word "Eingabeverarbeitungsfunktionen"[^1]. A token that wrapped cleanly across two lines in English can become one unbroken word that refuses to wrap at all in a narrow column.

The catch that surprises designers who only worry about the word count is that character count is not the same as space used. Chinese, Japanese, and Korean text often contracts in character count, sometimes by 10% to 55%, but the horizontal space can hold or even grow, because the characters are dramatically wider than Latin glyphs[^2]. The English "desktop" becomes "デスクトップ" in Japanese: one fewer character, noticeably more horizontal space[^1]. And many non-Latin scripts, including Arabic, Chinese, Devanagari, Japanese, Korean, and Thai, require more vertical space between lines than Latin text does. Thai can consume about 150% of the vertical space of the same English text[^1]. A card whose line height was tuned to an English baseline will feel cramped and clip ascenders and vowel signs in Thai or Arabic.

So the failure is not "longer words". It is that text grows in every dimension in ways that the layout did not reserve for. Width grows, height grows, and the growth is worst exactly where your components are most rigid: small labeled controls.

## The mirroring problem of RTL

Expansion is a quantitative problem. Right-to-left script is a qualitative one, a mirror, and treating it as text alignment is the most common miss. Arabic, Hebrew, Farsi, and Urdu read from right to left, and their entire interface must mirror: the first item in a navigation bar goes to the right, a "back" arrow points right, progress bars fill from the right, and reading order flows right to left[^3]. This is not about right-aligning the text. The whole layout logic flips.

The test-practice guidance frames it sharply: layout mirroring, reading order, focus order, and icon directionality all have to adapt, and a keyboard Tab order that stays left-to-right while the visuals are right-to-left breaks navigation for keyboard and screen-reader users[^3]. Directional icons are the subtle case. A "back" arrow that flips to point the wrong way reads as a forward arrow, which is worse than no icon at all. A progress indicator, a feed, or a carousel that does not reverse direction violates the learner's expectation for that script[^6].

Here is what a lot of design-system teams get backwards. They reach for a one-off fix, a `[dir="rtl"]` override that resets a margin, so the codebase accrues dozens of direction-specific exceptions that have to be maintained and that silently break when a component changes. The correct move is structural: build the layout on CSS logical properties so the mirroring comes free.

## Logical properties are the free RTL layer

CSS has a pair of concepts that make RTL support mostly automatic, and they deserve a precise explanation because their names confuse people. Every inline element has an "inline" axis (the direction text flows) and a "block" axis (the direction lines stack). For English and Arabic, inline is horizontal and block is vertical; for Japanese, inline flips to vertical. Within the inline axis there is a "start" and an "end". In a left-to-right layout, start is left and end is right. In right-to-left, start is right and end is left[^5].

Physical properties, `margin-left`, `padding-right`, `border-left`, describe a fixed side of the screen. Logical properties, `margin-inline-start`, `padding-inline-end`, `border-inline-start`, describe a side relative to the writing direction, so they flip automatically when the document direction does[^5]. Consider the classic card: an avatar on the start side, text to its end. In physical CSS you write a margin on the avatar for LTR, then an `[dir="rtl"]` override to reset it and set the other side, and you repeat that dance for every component[^5]. In logical CSS you write one property, `margin-inline-end`, and the browser reverses it for RTL with zero overrides[^5].

This is a large design-engineering win because Grid and Flexbox are logical by default. A flex row and a grid reflow according to the document direction automatically, so components built on them mirror for free, which is the single biggest reason to build components on Grid and Flexbox rather than floats and physical positioning[^5]. There are still gaps: `background-position` has no logical equivalent, so a mail icon positioned as a background on the end side needs an RTL Android override, and absolute positioning needs `inset-inline-start` and `inset-inline-end` rather than `left` and `right`[^5]. But the bulk of component spacing, which is where mirroring bugs hide, becomes a solved problem.

## Typography wants locale-aware tokens

Text expansion and direction both land on the typographic layer, and that is where a design system either absorbs the difference or hard-codes it. The SAS Institute Filament design system is a public case study in exactly this problem: product designs crafted in English, then shipped to a global customer base, producing overflow, RTL issues, and font inconsistencies that cascaded into the application[^4]. Their fix is instructive because it is token-based.

The Filament team found that CJK (Chinese, Japanese, Korean) fonts often lack a distinct bold weight, so applying a 700 font-weight token to CJK text renders poorly or synthesizes a blurry bold. Their solution was to remap the font-weight token from 700 to 400 for CJK locales. They also adjusted font-family, letter spacing, font-style, and font-variant tokens per language family[^4]. That single decision shows the shape of the problem: typography tokens are locale-dependent values, not universal constants.

Their token pipeline divides languages into families so they do not maintain a file per language-density pair. Western European and Slavic (Polish, English, French, German, Spanish), Chinese (simplified and traditional), Middle Eastern and East Asian (Arabic, Hebrew, Japanese, Korean, Thai, Vietnamese), and Global Diverse (Africa, South Asia, Pacific, Indigenous, Uralic, Turkic) each get their own token file with the values that change across the system[^4]. The locale-specific changes are concrete: font size, icon size, line height, spacing, and border radius[^4]. For example the CJK locales use a 16px base font size at medium and low density and 18px at high density, while Western European and Slavic languages use 14px at medium, 16px at high, and 12px at low density[^4]. Base spacing scales differently for scripts whose glyphs sit tall.

There is a warning buried in that case study worth surfacing: Figma's default line-height multiplier of 1.2 sits below the WCAG minimum of 1.5, so a team that relies on the tool's default ships text that fails the line-height bar for accessibility[^4]. Landing at 1.5 or above is both a readability and a compliance decision, and it buys exactly the vertical room that tall scripts need.

## The shape of a locale-aware system

Pull the pieces together and a concrete system emerges. Components are built on Grid and Flexbox so they mirror with the document direction and grow with their content. Spacing uses logical properties, so LTR and RTL are one codebase. Layout avoids fixed widths on text containers and uses auto-layout and wrapping instead, which is what lets a German word longer than the button fit without clipping[^6][^4]. Typography tokens carry per-language-family overrides for font-family, weight, line height, and spacing, sourced from a small set of locale-family token files rather than per-language exceptions[^4].

The disciplined way to lock this in is to test against the worst case before you have any translation. Pseudo-localization replaces your strings with text that is artificially lengthened, often padded with characters that force the layout to expand, which surfaces every fixed-width container and every overflow early while the string is still cheap to change[^2][^6]. Test at 200% expansion is a recurring benchmark in the accessibility-localization guidance, because it approximates the growth a long translation produces[^3]. And test with real translations for the layouts that matter, because wrapping, RTL mirroring, and font compatibility only become apparent with real content, not with placeholder text[^4].

![Three buttons compared at the same slot: the English Submit fits a fixed 140px width, the longer German Absenden clips in that fixed width, and a flexible auto-layout button grows to show the full label without truncation](</diagrams/localization-layout-design-systems-2026/4-fixed-vs-flexible.png>)

Here is the Adroit-on-the-ground note, framed reader-outward. We hit these exact failure modes inside our own delivery pipeline when a single client artifact moved from an English layout to a set of localized variants. The fix was not more translation work; it was tightening the layout contract up front, logical spacing, flexible growth, pseudolocalized test fixtures, so the localized version stopped breaking the thing the English version had always hidden. It is the same shift any product design system makes: stop patching per language, and build the layout to absorb the difference from the start.

## The audit you can run today

You can find out in an afternoon whether your system has the problem. Run four checks. First, survey your spacing and sizing properties for physical `left`, `right`, `margin-left`, `padding-right`, and `border-left` that should be logical. Every one is a place RTL will break and a maintenance cost you are paying in both directions[^5]. Second, grep your buttons, tabs, and form fields for fixed widths and `white-space: nowrap`, the two properties that clip or overflow on text expansion, and give the text room to grow or wrap[^2][^6]. Third, check your typography tokens: do weight, line height, and font-family hold across CJK and other tall-script locales, or is bold a global value that will render badly in Chinese[^4]? Fourth, run a pseudolocalization pass at 200% expansion and fix every overflow it finds before you spend money on translation[^3].

You do not have to support Arabic tomorrow to make these changes worth it. Logical properties and flexible layouts cost nothing on an English-only product and they are what make the later languages cheap. The design systems that survive going global are the ones that stopped assuming one script, one direction, and one set of typographic habits. The rest pay for that assumption in every new market, a few broken buttons and cramped cards at a time.
