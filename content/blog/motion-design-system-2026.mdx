Open the product you use most and tap a button. Something moves. A panel slides, a row expands, a toast drops in from the top edge. Now ask a simple question: who decided that it would take 300 milliseconds? In most teams the honest answer is a library default, a copied snippet, or a prototype nobody revisited. Motion is the last layer of the interface that ships without an owner.

That matters more than it sounds, because motion carries two obligations that cannot be met by accident. It has to be fast enough that feedback still reads as feedback. Jakob Nielsen's three response limits have held for over thirty years: 0.1 second for the feeling of direct manipulation, 1 second for an uninterrupted flow of thought, 10 seconds for holding attention on the task[^9]. And it has to be escapable, because the same movement that reads as polish to one person triggers dizziness, migraine, or nausea in another[^4].

Both of those are system properties, not page properties. A team that decides duration on a per-screen basis will get both wrong, in different ways, on different screens, forever. This article is about putting motion where color and spacing already live: in tokens, in component contracts, and in a test you can run.

## Motion has four jobs and one of them is not a job

Nielsen Norman Group's research on interface motion is blunt about what animation is for. Motion is useful as perceptible feedback, as a signal that the interface changed state, and as a spatial metaphor that tells users where content came from or went[^1]. It is much less useful as delight. The reason is biological: humans detect movement with the rod cells in peripheral vision, so motion is nearly impossible to ignore once it is on screen[^1]. Any movement that is not carrying information is therefore competing with the movement that is.

That gives you a decision gate worth applying before anything gets animated. Does this motion tell the user something the static interface does not? A cart badge updating silently is easy to miss, which is why the small animation on the badge is doing real work[^1]. A pencil icon morphing into a disk communicates a mode change more clearly than two icons swapping instantly[^1]. Those earn their place. A hero image drifting on scroll earns nothing, and NN/g's testing on parallax found exactly that: users were indifferent to it, some scrolled past animations before they finished loading, and on mobile the effects were often hidden under the user's own hand[^2].

![A decision gate for interface motion: motion that carries no information is cut, motion that starts automatically and runs past five seconds needs a pause control under WCAG 2.2.2, interaction-triggered motion needs a way to disable it under WCAG 2.3.3, and only then does it get a duration and easing token](</diagrams/motion-design-system-2026/1-decision-gate.png>)

The gate has three exits and they are not interchangeable. Cut it, if the motion carries nothing. Control it, if it starts on its own and runs longer than five seconds: that is the trigger for WCAG 2.2.2 Pause, Stop, Hide, which requires a mechanism for the user to pause, stop, or hide moving content that runs in parallel with other content[^3]. Make it disableable, if a user's interaction triggers it: WCAG 2.3.3 Animation from Interactions requires that motion triggered by interaction can be turned off unless the animation is essential to the information being conveyed[^4]. Only after those three pass does the question of timing come up.

## The duration ladder is not a matter of taste

Nielsen's limits describe perception, and they map surprisingly cleanly onto the duration tokens that mature design systems already ship. IBM's Carbon design system publishes six static duration tokens: `duration-fast-01` at 70ms for micro-interactions like buttons and toggles, `duration-fast-02` at 110ms for fades, `duration-moderate-01` at 150ms for small expansion and short travel, `duration-moderate-02` at 240ms for expansion, system communication, and toasts, `duration-slow-01` at 400ms for large expansion and important notifications, and `duration-slow-02` at 700ms for background dimming[^6]. Carbon's own evaluation checklist asks for micro-interactions inside a 90 to 120 millisecond band with ease-out on user input[^6].

Every one of those numbers sits under Nielsen's 0.1 second line or just above it, which is the point. Interface feedback that lands at 70 to 120 milliseconds reads as instant. Feedback that lands at 240 milliseconds is perceptibly a response but still fast enough to keep the user in flow. It is only when you get past a second that the interface has to start explaining itself.

![A duration ladder mapping interface motion bands to Nielsen's three response limits: micro-interaction feedback at 70 to 120 milliseconds inside the 0.1 second instant limit, transitions at 200 to 300 milliseconds inside the 1 second flow limit, and large or entering transitions at 400 to 700 milliseconds that must hand off to progress feedback before the 10 second attention limit](</diagrams/motion-design-system-2026/2-duration-ladder.png>)

Material Design 3 arrives at the same structure from a different direction. Its recommended pairs put an emphasized transition that begins and ends on screen at 500ms, an emphasized decelerate entrance at 400ms, an emphasized accelerate exit at 200ms, and the standard set at 300, 250, and 200ms respectively[^7]. Note the asymmetry in both systems: entrances get the long duration, exits get the short one. Material's reasoning is that exit transitions need less attention because the user's next task is already in front of them, while entrances deserve more time because they are introducing something new[^7]. Carbon makes the same distinction through easing: exit easing accelerates out of view to imply permanence, standard easing implies an element that left but could return, like a side panel[^6].

Distance matters too. Both systems scale duration with the size of the transition area, because a large element that moves at the same speed as a small one looks slower[^6][^7]. That is the argument for a duration scale rather than a single `--duration` value: one number cannot serve a 4px toggle nudge and a full-screen sheet.

One caveat worth stating plainly, because it changes what you should copy. Material 3 Expressive moved its components to a spring-based motion physics system, and the easing-and-duration model is now documented as still valid for transitions but no longer maintained for components[^7]. If you are starting fresh in 2026, spring tokens are at least as defensible a foundation as cubic-bezier pairs. What must not happen is a product mixing both without knowing which is which.

## Enter and exit are not the same animation

The asymmetry is the single most common defect in hand-rolled motion. A team writes one transition, applies it in both directions, and ends up with a slow exit that makes the interface feel sticky. Users dismiss a dialog and then wait for it. Material's guidance is explicit that exits should be shorter than entrances[^7], and Apple's Human Interface Guidelines add a related rule: let people cancel motion, and do not make them wait for an animation to finish before they can do anything, especially if they will see it more than once[^12].

The practical version is a pair of tokens per surface rather than one, with the exit on the fast side of the band and the value chosen from the same scale. A 240ms entrance paired with a 150ms exit is not a design flourish. It is the difference between a panel that feels responsive and one that feels like it is thinking about it.

## Put the animation on the compositor

The third failure mode is invisible in design review and obvious in a performance trace. Browsers render in stages: layout computes geometry, paint fills pixels, and composite assembles layers on the GPU. Animating a property that triggers layout forces the browser to recompute geometry on every frame. Animating `opacity` and `transform` stays on the compositing stage, where the work is cheap[^8].

web.dev's documentation of this is measured rather than theoretical: the same movement animated with `top` and `left` dropped about 50 percent of frames in their example, while the version animated with `transform` dropped about 1 percent[^8]. The visual difference at design review is zero, because they are the same motion. The difference in a user's hand is the whole experience.

![The browser rendering pipeline from layout to paint to composite, showing that animating width, height, top, or left forces layout and paint on every frame while opacity and transform stay on the compositor, which is why the same movement dropped 50 percent of frames in one version and 1 percent in the other](</diagrams/motion-design-system-2026/3-render-pipeline.png>)

Two habits follow. First, motion tokens should name the properties they animate, so a component cannot quietly animate `height: auto`. Second, `will-change` is a debugging tool, not a default: web.dev's guidance is to use it sparingly and only after you have found a real performance problem[^8]. Promoting dozens of layers to fix a stutter usually moves the stutter somewhere worse.

This is also where motion becomes a Core Web Vital problem. Interaction to Next Paint measures how long it takes from a user's click, tap, or key press until the browser paints the next frame, and it is scored at the 75th percentile of page loads: at or under 200 milliseconds is good, above 500 is poor[^10]. A decorative animation that occupies the main thread is not just decoration. It is latency the user can measure.

## The reduced-motion path is a mode, not a switch

The accessibility requirement is often reduced to one media query, which is where teams get it wrong. `prefers-reduced-motion` is well established: Baseline, widely available across browsers since January 2020, from Chrome 74, Firefox 63, and Safari 10.1[^5]. But what the query asks for is not "no motion". It asks for the removal, reduction, or replacement of non-essential motion, and MDN's own example replaces a scaling pulse with a slow opacity dissolve rather than deleting the animation[^5].

That distinction is the difference between an interface that still communicates and one that goes flat. The WCAG techniques for 2.3.3 are C39, using the CSS query to prevent motion, and SCR40, using the same query from JavaScript[^4]. In practice a useful system ships three tiers, and the middle one is where most of the work is.

![Three reduced-motion tiers: full motion with translation, scale, and parallax for users who made no request, reduced motion that keeps opacity and color transitions while removing translation, scale, and parallax, and a static tier where state changes are instantaneous and announced through copy or focus changes](</diagrams/motion-design-system-2026/4-motion-tiers.png>)

Tier one is full motion for users who made no request. Tier two is the reduce path: keep the crossfade, drop the slide, the scale, and anything that moves a large object across the viewport. Big travelling elements are the specific vestibular trigger, which is why MDN names scaling and panning of large objects as the problem[^5], and why Apple separately warns against motion at the edges of the field of view and against sustained oscillation[^12]. Tier three is for interfaces where even a fade is questionable, and it collapses transitions to instant state changes that are announced through copy or focus movement instead.

The important part is that tiers two and three are implemented once, in the component layer, keyed off the token set. A component that reads `--motion-enter` and `--motion-enter-reduced` cannot forget the reduced path. A component that hard-codes `animation: slide-in 300ms` will forget it every time. It is the same argument we make about [component APIs in headless libraries](/field-notes/headless-ui-component-libraries-2026): behavior that lives in the contract survives, and behavior that lives in a page does not.

## View transitions raised the ceiling and lowered the excuse

For years the reason custom page transitions were rare was cost: you had to keep the old DOM around, position the new one, animate both, block stray clicks on the outgoing content, and clean up. The View Transition API does that work for you. It handles both same-document transitions inside a single-page app and cross-document transitions between pages in a multi-page app, with default animations plus ways to customize or skip them[^11].

Cross-document transitions need only an opt-in rule on both documents, `@view-transition { navigation: auto; }`, and elements you want to track separately get a `view-transition-name`[^11]. That is a meaningful reduction in the cost of doing navigation motion properly, and it is worth knowing before you reach for a JavaScript animation library.

It is also, as of MDN's compatibility data, not universal. Cross-document transitions are supported in Chrome 126 and Safari 18.2, and Firefox does not support the `@view-transition` rule at all[^11]. A browser without the rule simply navigates normally, which is the correct failure mode for a progressive enhancement. Treat the transition as polish, never as the thing that carries state, and feature-detect `document.startViewTransition` on the JavaScript side.

![A progressive enhancement path for view transitions: same-document transitions feature-detect on document.startViewTransition, cross-document transitions opt in with the view-transition at-rule supported in Chrome 126 and Safari 18.2, and browsers without support follow the link normally with no functional loss](</diagrams/motion-design-system-2026/5-view-transitions.png>)

## The token set is small

Here is a working shape for the motion layer of a design system. Six durations on a non-linear scale, three or four easing curves, one reduced-motion variant per semantic slot, and a property allowlist. Everything else in the system reads from these and nothing hard-codes a millisecond value.

- `--motion-fast`: 70ms, for state feedback on buttons, toggles, and checkboxes[^6]
- `--motion-quick`: 110ms, for fades and small opacity changes[^6]
- `--motion-moderate`: 150ms, for short travel and small expansion[^6]
- `--motion-standard`: 240ms, for expansion, toasts, and system communication[^6]
- `--motion-slow`: 400ms, for large expansion and important notifications[^6]
- `--motion-backdrop`: 700ms, for background dimming behind a modal[^6]
- `--ease-standard`, `--ease-entrance`, `--ease-exit`: the three curves Carbon publishes, as cubic-bezier values or spring parameters[^6][^7]
- `--motion-distance-scale`: a small multiplier for elements that travel a long way[^6][^7]

The allowlist is the part teams skip and then regret. Write down that motion may animate `transform`, `opacity`, and `color`, and that any component animating a layout property needs an explicit exception with a reason. One line in the component contract prevents the class of bug that no amount of review catches, because the wrong version looks identical in a static frame.

## Audit motion the way you audit color

Motion that is not measured will not stay consistent. The audit is cheap, because almost every failure is visible in a slow-motion recording and a trace.

1. Record the three most-used flows at a quarter speed and watch for transitions that start late, overshoot, or fire twice.
2. Enable `prefers-reduced-motion: reduce` in the browser and repeat the flows. Anything that still slides, scales, or parallaxes has skipped the mode.
3. Open the performance panel and check which rendering stage the animations touch. A paint or layout band per frame means a property that should have been a transform[^8].
4. Grep the codebase for `ms` and `cubic-bezier` outside the token file. Every hit is a decision that escaped the system.
5. Check the long-running or automatic animations against the five-second rule, and confirm something pauses them[^3].

That third step is the one that changes behavior, because it turns a subjective argument about smoothness into a frame chart. It is also the step most likely to surface something the design team never specified, since a library default will show up in the trace even though no one wrote it.

## The honest limits

Two cautions before this reads as a rule that motion is always worth building.

The first is that motion has a poor track record as a persuasion device. NN/g tested parallax across years and found users largely indifferent to it, with real costs in load time and readability and occasional motion sickness[^2]. Apple shipped a Reduce Motion setting in iOS 7 in 2013 specifically because task-switching animations were making people feel vertigo and nausea[^2]. A motion system helps you ship restraint consistently. It does not make decoration valuable.

The second is that reduced motion is not a checkbox. The media query tells you the operating system setting, not the person's reason. Some users want no movement at all, some want no large movement, some are unaffected by fades and badly affected by scale. Three tiers cover more of that spectrum than a binary switch, and none of them covers it completely. Ship a global preference in the product as well as respecting the system one, because the system one is buried and a lot of people never find it.

## What this looks like when it works

A motion system is not a document. It is a small number of decisions made once, in a place where they can be changed once. Six durations that map to perceptual thresholds instead of taste. An entrance and an exit that are deliberately different lengths. A reduced-motion path wired into the component contract rather than bolted on per screen. An allowlist that keeps animation on the compositor. And a decision gate that cuts the motion which was never going to teach anyone anything.

The test of it is unglamorous and immediate. Record a flow at quarter speed, turn on reduce motion, open the performance panel. If all three look right, the motion is specified. If any of them surprises you, something on that screen still belongs to nobody, and it is the same thing that will be wrong on the next screen your team builds.
