There is a specific sentence that shows up in every cross-functional project, and it is the one sentence a consulting engagement cannot afford. Somebody asks, "hold on, didn't we decide this back in February?" Then everybody looks at each other. Then somebody opens Slack and types the same phrase into search. Then they surface half a thread that does not quite say what they remembered it saying. Then the team re-litigates a decision that took a week to make the first time[^1].

The reason is not that people forgot. The decision was never written down anywhere anyone would look. The thinking lived in the meeting, and the meeting ended. Meeting notes captured what was said, not what was decided. Three months later the only record of the choice sits in three or four people's heads, and none of the versions agree[^1].

On a product project that drift is expensive. On a consulting engagement it is corrosive, because consulting does not ship a product so much as a string of decisions. What scope is in. How the data flows. Which tool wins. When sign-off happens. What the acceptance criteria mean. Every one of those is a decision, and the client is carrying their own memory of it just like you are carrying yours. When the two memories diverge, you do not get a quiet disagreement. You get a scope dispute, a reopened design, or a handover where the new person inherits a set of conclusions nobody can defend.

## Why decisions disappear

Call it decision debt. A team can be technically excellent, communicative, and thoughtful and still pile it up, not by making bad decisions but by making decisions that disappear[^2]. Technical debt is code that needs refactoring. Decision debt is reasoning that needs reconstruction, and you cannot refactor a decision you cannot find[^2]. Once the context is gone, the alternatives considered, the constraints at the time, the tradeoffs weighed, the decision becomes an archaeological puzzle and someone has to dig. Digging costs time.

Decisions do not get documented because the moment they are made, everyone feels they will remember. In the short term they do. The problem is the half-life of working memory on a team with turnover, shifting priorities, and engagements that run eighteen months[^2]. Most decisions end up in one of three places: a Slack thread nobody will search, with the full context buried under forty thousand other messages; a meeting that was not recorded, or was recorded in a calendar invite that expired; or a person's head, and then they leave[^2].

The most dangerous variant is the implicit decision. Nobody ever made it explicitly. The team just started doing things a certain way, and now it is load-bearing behavior with no documented rationale[^2]. When someone challenges it, there is nothing to point to, not because the decision was wrong but because the evidence trail does not exist. This is where handoff friction compounds, in the invisible gap between the person who made a call and the person who now has to live with it[^2].

![The three places a decision goes to die: a Slack thread nobody searches, a meeting that was not recorded, and a person's head who then leaves, plus the implicit decision that was never made explicitly at all.](</diagrams/decision-log-consulting-delivery-2026/1-where-decisions-go.png>)

The human reason behind all three is the same memory failure. Psychologists call it hindsight bias. Baruch Fischhoff demonstrated it in 1975 in the first experimental study of what he called creeping determinism: once people know an outcome, they systematically overestimate how predictable it was from the start[^3]. Subjects told that a result had occurred rated it as far more likely than subjects who had not been told, even when they were instructed to ignore the outcome[^3]. Daniel Kahneman described the companion effect, outcome bias, where we judge a decision by its result rather than its reasoning[^4]. Memory is not a recording, it is a reconstruction, and each time you recall a decision your brain rebuilds it using everything you have learned since[^5].

The practical consequence for a delivery team is blunt: if you did not write down what you chose and why at the moment you chose it, you will misremember it, and the lessons you should have extracted will not be extracted[^3]. You cannot learn from a decision you did not record[^3].

![A decision written down at the moment it is made stays stable, while an unrecorded decision gets rewritten by hindsight after the outcome is known, collapsing the original reasoning into whatever makes the outcome look obvious.](</diagrams/decision-log-consulting-delivery-2026/2-hindsight-bias.png>)

## What it actually costs

The macro numbers are staggering and they are worth stating once. Analysis by Iterators finds that up to 50 percent of corporate knowledge cannot be found centrally, which means teams are regularly rediscovering conclusions they already reached, and the average US enterprise loses 4.5 million dollars a year in productivity to inadequate knowledge sharing and preservation[^2]. McKinsey's research on organizational knowledge management found that knowledge workers spend up to 20 percent of their time searching for information or recreating work that already exists somewhere in the organization[^6]. This is a core reason decision-making velocity degrades as teams grow past ten people[^6].

Teams pay for undocumented decisions in three specific ways[^2].

Relitigation. The team debates a choice that was already settled because nobody can find the original decision and its rationale. Sometimes the outcome is the same. Sometimes the team reverses a good decision because the reasoning was lost[^2].

Onboarding drag. New engineers and project managers spend their first months learning how things work here by asking the people who were around. Every "that is just how it is here" answer is an undocumented decision surfacing as a tax on new-hire productivity[^2].

Context-switching overhead. When team members explain the same decision repeatedly in one-on-ones, in Slack, in planning, a single undocumented choice multiplies its cost across every conversation it generates[^2].

Rework's guide to decision logs opens with the cleanest version of the problem. A growth team spent four hours in a meeting trying to reconstruct why they had chosen their current pricing structure. The person who led the decision had left the company eight months earlier. Nobody had written anything down: not the alternatives, not the customer data they used, not the reason they ruled out an option that two people in the room now liked. The team lead said afterward that they had not made a decision that day, they had just admitted they could not make one without context they no longer had. A five-minute log entry eight months earlier would have given them that context[^6].

For a consulting firm, every one of these costs lands inside the commercial relationship. Relitigation is a scope dispute. Onboarding drag is a handover where the incoming specialist re-derives conclusions the engagement already settled. Context-switching overhead is the senior billable person spending their margin re-explaining decisions they made months ago. The client's memory of what was agreed is just as much a reconstruction as yours, and in a fixed-fee engagement the difference between two memories is money[^7] someone did not plan to spend.

## The pattern already exists: architecture decision records

None of this is new, and the software world already built the fix. It is called an Architecture Decision Record, and Michael Nygard popularized it in 2011. The minimal format is a short document per decision with a small set of sections: the status, the context, the decision, and the consequences[^8]. The status field tracks a decision as proposed, accepted, rejected, deprecated, or superseded[^8][^9]. The context says what problem or change motivated the decision. The decision says what was actually chosen. The consequences say what becomes easier or more difficult to do because of the change, and the guidance is explicit that all consequences should be listed, not just the positive ones[^9].

The pattern's power is where it aims the reader. arc42's documentation guidance puts it plainly: with many decisions, teams know about the decision because it is visible in the code, but they miss the motivation behind it[^9]. ADRs exist to preserve the motivation. The guidance calls for documenting only the decisions that are significant, documenting the rejected alternatives as well as the chosen one, and giving every decision a timestamp[^9]. ADRs were adopted by teams at AWS, Microsoft, and GitHub as a low-overhead way to preserve architectural reasoning without adding ceremony, and AWS's own analysis found that teams using ADRs reduced the coordination load that undocumented decisions help create[^2][^10].

But ADRs are tied to a specific home, technical architecture decisions stored as one file per decision in a git repo. That is a strength when the decisions are about systems. It undershoots the full scope of what a consulting team decides in a week. The decision log is the broader pattern, and an ADR is one kind of log[^1]. The same discipline, capture the choice and the reasoning at the moment it lands, applies to scope decisions, sign-off decisions, approach decisions, and every other call that shapes the engagement.

## The consulting decision log: six fields that survive a new hire

The version that works in delivery borrows the ADR's discipline and strips it to a schema a person can fill in while they are still in the room. Quire's six-field entry is the template that survives contact with a cross-functional project[^1].

Title for what was decided, not what was discussed. "Use feature flags for the new dashboard rollout," not "rollout strategy discussion." A reader should know from the title alone what changed.

Date when the decision was finalized, not when the discussion started. A debate can run three meetings. The log only cares about the day it resolved.

Owner, the person accountable for the decision standing. This is not always the person who proposed it. When it needs revisiting or defending, this person is the first stop[^1].

Summary, one short paragraph: what was decided, the most important constraint behind it, and any obvious second-order effect. Do not go longer. The log gets ignored the moment it turns dense[^1].

Alternatives considered, a short list of the other options with a one-line note on why each lost. This is the field teams most often skip and the one that is most valuable three months out, because reopening a decision without knowing the original alternatives is guessing[^1][^6]. Capture the trade-off, not just the outcome.

Trigger to revisit, the specific thing that puts the decision back on the table. New data, a scope change, a date. When nothing would, write "stands until project ends," and treat an inability to name any trigger as a quiet sign the decision was made on vibes[^1]. Rework's parallel five-field format collapses the same idea into Date, Decision, a two-to-four-sentence Context, Alternatives considered, and Who decided[^6].

![A decision log entry has six fields under two hundred words: a title saying what was decided, the date it was finalized, the accountable owner, a one paragraph summary, the alternatives that lost and why, and a trigger to revisit.](</diagrams/decision-log-consulting-delivery-2026/3-six-field-entry.png>)

The whole entry must stay under two hundred words and take about three minutes to write[^1][^6]. Brevity is a feature, not a concession. A three-minute record that actually gets written beats an elaborate template that never gets touched[^2][^6]. If writing the log takes longer than the decision did, the process will die within a month.

## The rules that stop the log from dying

A decision log is easy to design and hard to keep alive, and the failure modes are predictable. The first is retroactive logging. Someone updates the log on Friday from what happened all week, and by Friday nobody remembers the alternatives, so the entries flatten into bland summaries with no decision context, which is meeting notes wearing a different hat, and within a month nobody reads it. The fix is to log at the moment a decision lands, with whoever facilitates typing the entry as it resolves and the team agreeing on the wording before moving on. If the log cannot be written in five minutes, the decision is not actually done yet[^1].

The second failure mode is centralized authoring with no agreement. One person, usually the PM, tries to capture every decision the team makes, and by the time they write it up the decision has already been bent a little by retelling. The team disagrees with the log's version, stops trusting it, and stops updating it. The fix is shared authoring with central ownership: the PM owns the format and the location, but anyone who makes a decision writes its entry, so the words come from the people closest to the decision[^1].

The third failure mode is no agreed home. The log lives in someone's personal Notion and the link breaks when they leave, or it is split across five tools so people give up searching and re-decide instead. The fix is one canonical home attached to the project itself, in the same workspace as the work, so it travels with the project and nothing breaks when someone changes roles[^1][^2]. A searchable home matters almost as much as the recording habit: a decision log nobody can find is only marginally better than no decision log at all[^2][^6].

Anchor the habit to the ceremonies you already run. The asa team's advice is to make the close of any significant meeting include two minutes capturing the decisions made, and to treat the record as part of the decision rather than a downstream task[^2]. In a sprint cadence that means the decision log gets updated in planning (decisions about what to build), in the acceptance conversation (what done means), and in the retro (decisions about how the team works). Add "decision recorded" to the definition of done for any significant choice, and link each record from the PR, ticket, or spec where it takes effect[^2][^6]. When someone asks why, do not answer in Slack, link to the record. That trains the habit and makes the log self-reinforcing[^2].

![A decision moves through a status lifecycle: proposed, then accepted when the stakeholders agree, then superseded or deprecated when circumstances change the specific trigger field says what reopens it, with past records kept rather than deleted.](</diagrams/decision-log-consulting-delivery-2026/4-lifecycle.png>)

Supersede, do not delete. Status is the mechanism that keeps the log honest over an eighteen-month engagement. A decision that was right at month two can be wrong at month nine, and when it is, the record of it does not get erased, it gets marked superseded with a pointer to its replacement[^2]. That is what makes the log a history of reasoning rather than a stack of current choices, and it is exactly the audit trail hindsight bias cannot corrupt[^3].

## Where we hit this, and what fixed it

We found this failure mode in our own delivery chain the way everyone finds it, at a handover. Our delivery runs a chain of specialists, each profile handling a stage of the work, and for a long time the decisions each stage made lived in that specialist's head and in the handoff artifacts that moved with the ticket. The moment a call stopped being one person's job, it became "the way we do it here," with no documented rationale and nothing to point to when the approach was challenged.

What fixed it was not a tool. It was a two-minute record closed in the same conversation where each client-affecting decision landed, written by the person who was making the call, in one canonical log attached to the engagement. It cost almost nothing per decision and it paid for itself exactly where the research says it will: at handover, where the incoming specialist reads the reasoning instead of reconstructing it, and at the first "could we revisit that," where the log answers the question that used to start a dispute. The principle is the same one the ADR community arrived at years ago, applied to every decision a consulting engagement makes, not just the architectural ones.

## Start Monday, not next quarter

The biggest mistake teams make with a decision log is trying to reconstruct history first. Do not. The goal is to stop new debt from accumulating, not to rebuild the old stack[^2]. Start from now.

- Start the log today with one decision from last week, in whatever workspace the team already uses. A blank Notion page, a Confluence space, a Google Doc, a /decisions folder in the repo. The tool matters less than the habit[^2][^6].
- At the next meeting, spend the last five minutes asking what the team decided today that is worth logging. If the answer is nothing, that is a useful data point. If the answer reveals something significant that would have been lost, that is the argument for the habit[^6].
- When someone asks why a decision was made, link to the record instead of answering from memory. That single behavior makes the log self-reinforcing[^2].
- Review the log in the retro and in quarterly review, marking stale decisions as deprecated or superseded rather than pruning them[^2][^3].
- Add "decision recorded" to the definition of done for significant choices[^2].

The one-question filter before you log anything: would a reasonable person ask why we did this in six months? If yes, write it down[^2][^6]. A consulting engagement is a stream of decisions, and the record of those decisions is the only part of the work that survives turnover, memory, and the client's own reconstruction of what was agreed. Make the log the deliverable, and the deliverable stops being negotiable.

## The one-line version

A consulting engagement is a string of decisions, most of which evaporate the moment the meeting ends. Keep a six-field decision log, log at the moment and not on Friday, let whoever decides write the entry, give it one searchable home, and supersede rather than delete.
