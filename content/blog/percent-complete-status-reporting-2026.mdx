Four workstreams dial in. The numbers are 85, 90, 80, 95. Nobody asks what they count, because everyone already knows what they mean, and that is the problem. Twelve weeks later the programme lands four months late and the same four people sit in a room explaining that the reporting was accurate.

They were not lying. Percent complete is the most widely used progress number in project management and it is the one the organisations that run the largest, most expensive projects on earth treat as a last resort. NASA's earned value reference guide lays out the techniques available for measuring accomplishment and tags percent complete as "least desirable" and "somewhat subjective" [^1]. The US Government Accountability Office says performance measured by percentage of work completed "should be based on objective and quantifiable work completion" [^2]. Both organisations still allow the method. The difference is that they define it before the work starts, they require evidence behind the number, and they keep a shorter list of things that count as finished.

A consulting team can adopt the same discipline without importing a defence contracting bureaucracy. It takes four moves: decide what the number is measuring, stop measuring the work that cannot be late, report the milestone ladder instead of the average, and make the colour on the dashboard a request for a decision rather than a verdict on the project manager.

## Three different numbers wear the same label

When a delivery lead says "80 percent", one of three things is true and they are not interchangeable.

The first is **duration elapsed**. Thirty-two weeks into a forty-week plan is 80 percent of the calendar, regardless of what exists. NASA is unusually blunt about this one. Its guide tells project managers that a schedule "percent complete" input is not required and not recommended, because the figure displayed in scheduling software "is actually the percent of duration complete", which "is not necessarily the same as the physical or accomplishment percentage" [^1]. The number in the Gantt chart is a clock, not a measurement of work.

The second is **effort burned**. Hours spent divided by hours budgeted. This is the number consultancies default to, because the timesheet already exists and the client is billed from it. It answers a real question, which is whether the fee is being consumed at the expected rate. It answers nothing about whether the deliverable exists.

The third is **physical accomplishment**, the share of the defined work that is finished and accepted. This is the only one of the three that can be used to forecast a completion date, and it is the only one that requires a definition of finished.

![Three readings of the same 80 percent figure: weeks elapsed, hours burned, and accepted deliverables, diverging on the same 40-week engagement](</diagrams/percent-complete-status-reporting-2026/1-three-readings.png>)

The gap between them is not small. Take a 40-week ERP implementation reporting at week 32. Duration is 80 percent. Effort is 80 percent if the team is on plan. Of the seven named deliverables, four are accepted, one is in test, and two have not started, which puts physical accomplishment at 57 percent. All three figures are honest. Two of them will produce a completion date that is wrong by months.

The practical fix is cheap. Write the definition into the reporting template on day one and put the number that is being reported next to the figure itself. A status line that reads "62 percent complete (accepted deliverables of 7 planned)" cannot be quietly reinterpreted in week 30. A bare "62%" always can.

## Work is back-loaded, and that is structural

Fred Brooks described the mechanism in 1975 and it has not improved. Milestones, he wrote, "must be concrete, specific, measurable events, defined with knife-edge sharpness. Coding, for a counterexample, is '90 percent finished' for half of the total coding time. Debugging is '99 percent complete' most of the time. 'Planning complete' is an event one can proclaim almost at will." His conclusion is the sentence to put on the wall: "Concrete milestones, on the other hand, are 100-percent events" [^3].

The reason the last stretch takes so long is not laziness or optimism. It is that the riskiest work cannot begin until the rest of the work exists. Integration needs two systems. Migration needs a stable data model. User acceptance needs something users can log into. Cutover needs everything. So the work that remains when a project reports 90 percent is not 10 percent of the effort, it is the concentrated, sequence-bound, dependency-heavy part that all the earlier work was preparation for.

![Cumulative accomplishment against elapsed time on a typical delivery, showing the gap that opens when integration and acceptance work is back-loaded](</diagrams/percent-complete-status-reporting-2026/2-back-loaded-curve.png>)

Consulting engagements have a standard tail, and it is remarkably consistent: user acceptance testing, data migration and reconciliation, training, cutover and go-live, and formal acceptance and sign-off. Every item on that list is a 100 percent event. None of them can honestly be reported at 90 percent, because a half-migrated dataset is not a dataset that is nearly migrated, it is a dataset that is not migrated.

That has a reporting consequence. If the tail is where the risk sits, then the tail is what the report should expose earliest, and a percentage average is precisely the instrument that hides it. Five deliverables at 80 percent and two at zero average out to a number that looks like progress and contains none of the information.

## The work that cannot be late

NASA's guide sorts work into categories before it assigns any measurement technique. Discrete effort is work tied to a specific end product that can be planned and measured directly. Level of effort is work that earns value purely with the passage of time, because it has no discrete deliverable: project management, oversight, administration [^1]. Level of effort never shows a schedule variance, for the simple reason that it is measured by the clock, and the guide warns that heavy use of it "has the effect of masking potential problem areas and skewing the EV data reported", with a rule of thumb to keep it near 20 percent of the budget [^1].

Read that against a typical consulting status report and the diagnosis is immediate. "Discovery and stakeholder alignment" reported at 60 percent for three consecutive weeks is level of effort wearing a percentage. "Programme governance" at 45 percent is level of effort. Neither can be late, neither can be wrong, and neither tells the client whether the thing they are paying for exists. They are not dishonest numbers. They are numbers that cannot fail, which is a different and more dangerous property.

![Decision path for choosing a measurement technique per deliverable, from zero-or-one-hundred for short items through weighted milestones for long phases](</diagrams/percent-complete-status-reporting-2026/3-measurement-ladder.png>)

The ladder in the figure is the practical version of NASA's selection guide [^1]. Short deliverables that finish inside one reporting period earn nothing until they are done. Work spanning two periods takes half at start and half at finish. Long phases get split into weighted milestones, each of which is a 100 percent event with a named reviewer, so credit is granted by evidence rather than by the person doing the work. Percentage of completion survives only as the fallback for genuinely continuous work, and only with backup evidence attached.

## What the base rates say about a green dashboard

There is a second reason to distrust an early green. The prior is against you.

The largest study of IT change initiatives assembled 1,471 projects and found an average cost overrun of 27 percent. That average is the reassuring number. The finding that matters is the tail: one project in six was a black swan, averaging a 200 percent cost overrun and a schedule overrun of almost 70 percent [^4]. A later study of 5,392 IT projects completed between 2002 and 2014, worth $56.5 billion, found that cost overruns follow a power-law distribution rather than a normal one, which means extreme overruns occur far more often than a normal-distribution model would suggest and are systematically underestimated by managers who assume the bell curve [^5].

![Overrun distribution under a normal assumption compared with the fat-tailed reality, showing the one-in-six band that the average conceals](</diagrams/percent-complete-status-reporting-2026/4-fat-tail.png>)

Governments that have to budget for this have stopped pretending otherwise. The UK Treasury's supplementary Green Book guidance on optimism bias sets starting adjustments by project type, and for equipment and development work, the category that includes ICT development projects, the upper-bound adjustment at outline business case stage is 200 percent on capital expenditure and 54 percent on works duration [^6]. That is not a consultant's scare tactic. It is the official starting assumption of a government that has been burned repeatedly and decided to price the burn in.

None of this means a project is doomed. It means that "on budget and on schedule" at week four is a statement about the mode of a distribution, not a measurement of your particular project. The correct response is not pessimism, it is range. Lovallo and Kahneman described the mechanism two decades ago: forecasters default to the inside view, an account of this project's specific plan, and consistently ignore the distribution of outcomes for projects of the same class, which is the outside view [^10]. Reference class forecasting, the discipline that follows from it, asks a simpler question than "how long will this take us" and a harder one: "how long did the last twenty of these take, and where in that spread does this one sit".

## RAG is a decision protocol, not a mood

The traffic light is the most abused artefact in delivery governance, and the industry has a name for the failure. A watermelon project is green on the outside and red on the inside, and the Association for Project Management describes exactly how it happens: pressure not to show amber or red because that pulls senior stakeholders in, so the status stays green until the last possible moment and then turns red all at once [^7].

The fix is not a better template. It is a written rule that ties each colour to a decision state rather than to a feeling:

- **Green** means tracking against the approved baseline, no intervention needed.
- **Amber** means a deviation exists or is imminent, and the team has a credible recovery plan inside its own authority.
- **Red** means the baseline will be missed and a decision above the project's authority is required.

The third definition is the one that changes behaviour. Red stops being a confession and becomes a request. A delivery lead who reports red is asking for a scope decision, a budget release, or an escalation to a vendor, and the organisation's response should be to answer the request, not to interrogate the reporter. Where reporting red gets someone cross-examined and reporting green gets them left alone, you will get watermelons every time, regardless of how well the template is designed [^7].

Two supporting rules make the colours mean something. The status must be derived from the milestone ladder rather than negotiated in the meeting, and every report must show the trend beside the snapshot, because a project that has been amber for six consecutive weeks is a different object from one that turned amber this week.

## Earned value, used for the one thing it is good at

Earned value management gets dismissed in consulting circles as defence-industry theatre, which is a loss, because one part of it is genuinely useful: it converts cost and schedule into an index that can be tracked over time.

The mechanics are simple enough to hold in your head. Planned value is the budgeted cost of the work scheduled by now. Earned value is the budgeted cost of the work actually accomplished. Actual cost is what you spent. The cost performance index is earned value divided by actual cost, and dividing the budget at completion by that index gives an estimate at completion [^2]. A CPI of 0.9 at the halfway point means you are buying ninety cents of finished work per dollar, and the arithmetic says the total will land about 11 percent over budget.

What makes it worth the trouble is a finding about stability. The cumulative cost performance index on defence contracts has been shown to stabilise to within 10 percent of its final value by the 20 percent completion point, and in most cases it only got worse from there [^8]. In plain terms: by the time a fifth of the work is done, you usually know how the project ends, and the news rarely improves.

The counter-evidence matters just as much, and honest delivery leads should know it. A re-examination of UK construction data found only two of ten projects stable at the 20 percent mark, and a study of 35 industrial construction projects found that over half did not stabilise until after 80 percent completion [^9]. The stability point is a property of the project class, not a law of nature. Which gives the practical instruction: run the index on your own history before you trust it, and treat the index as a trend with a range rather than a single authoritative number.

For fixed-price consulting engagements the honest version is narrower still. Cost performance is mostly a margin question internal to the firm, and what the client needs is the forecast date and the forecast scope, expressed as a range. Earned value earns its place as the early-warning instrument behind that range, not as the headline.

## The report that cannot be a watermelon

Everything above collapses into one artefact: a weekly report with a fixed shape, five blocks, one page, the same every week so that the reader notices what changed rather than re-learning the format.

![Anatomy of a weekly status report built from accepted artifacts, a milestone ladder, a forecast range, open decisions, and explicit asks](</diagrams/percent-complete-status-reporting-2026/5-report-anatomy.png>)

**Finished this period.** The named artifacts that passed acceptance, who accepted them, and the date. Not tasks completed, not hours consumed. The acceptance evidence is what makes the claim checkable, which is the same principle behind [the acceptance gate we recommend for consulting delivery](/field-notes/acceptance-gate-consulting-delivery-2026).

**The milestone ladder.** Which of the named 100 percent events are done, in progress, or not started, with the dates. No percentage above the milestone level, because a percentage above the milestone level is where the ambiguity gets introduced.

**The forecast.** A completion range with dates, the basis for it, and the delta since the previous report. The delta is the actual news. A forecast that has moved three weeks in a month is a signal, even when the colour has not changed.

**Open decisions and live risks.** Each with a named owner and a date. Decision latency is one of the most reliable early indicators of a stalling engagement, and it is invisible in a percentage. This is why [a decision log beats a status narrative](/field-notes/decision-log-consulting-delivery-2026): it makes the queue measurable.

**The asks.** What the delivery team needs, from whom, by when. A report with no asks either has no problems or is not being read carefully.

## Where AI makes this better and worse at the same time

Status reporting used to be expensive, which was the excuse for doing it badly. That excuse is gone. An agent can assemble a status pack from a tracker, a repository, and a calendar in minutes, and the assembly is no longer the bottleneck.

The risk moves rather than disappears. Generated reports inherit whatever definition of done the tracker carries, and they carry no hesitation. A human writing "80 percent" pauses, because they know it is soft. A generated line reads the same whether the number came from accepted artifacts or from a field someone filled in during a planning meeting three months ago. If your definition of done is vague, automation will scale the vagueness and wrap it in a consistent, confident format.

The structural answer is the one we apply to our own delivery pipeline: a work item moves through explicit states, and a stage counts as complete when its artifact exists and has passed an acceptance gate, not when someone types a percentage into a field. Progress becomes the count of accepted artifacts and the age of the oldest open decision, both of which can be computed without asking anyone how it is going. That is a narrower kind of reporting than a percentage, and it has the property that matters: it cannot be produced without opening the work.

## The test to run on Monday

Ask each workstream lead two questions. Name the last artifact that passed acceptance, and the date. Name the next one, and the date it will pass.

If they can answer both, the percentage in the deck is a summary of something real and you can argue about the forecast. If they cannot, you do not have a measurement, you have an estimate wearing a measurement's clothes, and the honest thing to do is label it as one. The number is not the problem. The problem is that a percentage can be produced from a calendar, a timesheet, or a feeling, and only one of those three is a forecast you can act on.
