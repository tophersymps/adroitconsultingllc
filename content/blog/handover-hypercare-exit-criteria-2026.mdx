The scariest sentence in consulting delivery is not "the build is late." It is the thing a client says six weeks after go-live, when your team has already moved its people to the next account: "How do we restart the batch job? It has been quiet since you left, and nobody here knows where the credentials are."

That sentence is a bill for a billable phase you never priced. The work shipped. The client accepted it at the gate we described in our piece on [acceptance and the Definition of Done](/field-notes/definition-of-done-ai-agents-2026). And still the engagement did not actually close, because the client came away with a functioning system and none of the ability to run it.

This article is about the part of the delivery lifecycle almost nobody writes about: the exit. The handover, the knowledge transfer, the runbooks, the hypercare window, and the exit criteria that let you leave with the client standing. It is the least documented phase in consulting, and the one where engagements quietly stop being profitable referrals and start being support tickets.

## Done is not closed

Acceptance decides whether the client will pay for the work. Handover decides whether the client can live with it after you leave. The two are separated by a gap of knowledge, and that gap is where consulting value evaporates.

![The handover gap sits between the acceptance gate and a closed engagement, and it carries four things no acceptance criteria captures: access, operation, recovery, and reasoning](</diagrams/handover-hypercare-exit-criteria-2026/1-handover-gap.png>)

Think about what a handover actually transfers. A client who has just signed off a delivery needs four things that no acceptance criteria in the world captures:

1. **Access.** Credentials, environments, repositories, dashboards, and the map of who holds each key.
2. **Operation.** How to run the thing day to day, reboot it, patch it, and scale it past the point you staffed.
3. **Recovery.** What to do when it breaks at 2am, and who to call before they call you.
4. **Reasoning.** Why the system is shaped the way it is, and which decisions are load-bearing and which are reversible.

The first category is a document. The other three are knowledge, and knowledge does not transfer by sending a link to a wiki.

NIX United, an engineering firm that builds teams for hundreds of product companies, breaks the knowledge you carry into three types, and each one needs a different handover method[^1]. Explicit knowledge documentation, architecture diagrams, API specs, runbooks transfers through written artifacts. Tacit knowledge debugging instinct, judgment about trade-offs, understanding of why the system misbehaves the way it does lives in heads and transfers only through shadowing, pair time, and live walkthroughs. Implicit knowledge the informal process that everyone follows but nobody wrote down surfaces only when you run structured Q&A workshops and scenario drills long enough that the unwritten rules come out. Hand over only the explicit layer and you have handed the client a box of parts, not a machine they can run.

## The handover is a deliverable, not a meeting

The mistake most teams make is treating the handover as a single afternoon of walkthroughs bolted onto the end of the project. It is a deliverable with the same discipline as the build itself: a scope, an owner, a schedule, and a test that proves it landed.

![Three kinds of knowledge need three different handover methods: explicit knowledge transfers through written artifacts, tacit knowledge only through interaction, and implicit knowledge through structured probing](</diagrams/handover-hypercare-exit-criteria-2026/2-knowledge-types.png>)

A knowledge transfer plan, done properly, is a risk-management and continuity mechanism, not a documentation exercise[^2]. It defines what transfers, who owns sharing it, who receives it, how it happens, and when it is done. Good KT plans run continuously, not at the end: as the project team learns something the client will need, that learning enters the handover backlog on its own sprint lane, so the exit is fifty small transfers instead of one impossible download at the finish line.

The practical shape of the handover is a runbook. A runbook is a step-by-step recipe for a repeated task or incident, written so that someone who did not build the thing can execute it[^3]. PagerDuty, which lives on incident response, frames the runbook as the thing that turns an expert's head-back knowledge into a shared asset: well-built runbooks let a team of normal operators handle what used to require the one person who knew[^4]. The effective runbook is never a product readme. It is a set of operating procedures built from actual incidents and actual recovery, not from how the system was supposed to behave.

A runbook worth handing over covers the seven sections the ex-Google operations author Tom Limoncelli standardizes: a service overview, build information, deploy instructions, common task procedures, a pager playbook mapping every alert to a specific response, disaster recovery plans, and the service level agreement the system owes[^5]. If the runbook for the thing you built does not have a pager playbook that answers "this alert fires, then what," the handover has a hole in it.

## Hypercare: the bridge between go-live and independence

Even a perfect handover leaves the client running a system the day after you built it, and the first days after go-live are when every gap in configuration, training, and documentation surfaces at once. That is the moment you run hypercare: a defined period of elevated support after go-live, with tighter response targets, wider coverage, and named owners, until the system is stable enough for the client to run it on business-as-usual terms[^6].

The term comes out of enterprise system implementations and it has a sharp rule attached: a hypercare window is a period, and it ends when a named set of exit criteria is met, not when the calendar says so[^7]. "Four weeks" is a schedule, not an exit. A time-boxed period either ends while the client is still wobbly, or it drags on because nobody can prove the system is safe to hand fully over. The discipline is to define, before go-live, the conditions that must all hold for a stretch of stable days, and to exit on that evidence.

## Staffing hypercare on the surge, not the average

The most common hypercare failure is running the window with business-as-usual staffing. Help desk teams use a simple formula to plan it, and consulting teams should steal it: agent hours needed per week equals baseline weekly tickets times a surge multiplier times average handle time, divided by a utilization rate counted at 0.7, not 1.0, because nobody is productive every billed hour[^8].

![Step through the staffing formula on the worked example: a 400-seat account pulls 161 agent hours in the first two weeks at a 2.5x surge, tapering to 90 hours, then 45 hours of business-as-usual steady state](</diagrams/handover-hypercare-exit-criteria-2026/3-staffing-surge.png>)

The surge multiplier is the load-bearing number. The first two weeks after go-live run at 2.0 to 3.0 times baseline; weeks three and four taper to about 1.3 to 1.5 times; and the utilization floor at 0.7 means you staff for real, not for the best case[^9]. Run the worked example from that playbook and the point lands. A twelve-agent team brings a 400-seat account live. Baseline demand is 90 tickets a week at half an hour of handle time, about 45 agent hours a week in steady state. Weeks one and two at a 2.5x surge run roughly 161 agent hours, or about four dedicated agents, not the one and a half the BAU math implies. Weeks three and four at 1.4x run about 90 hours, tapering to two and a half[^10]. Staff that surge and you are buying the client a stable landing; staff it at BAU levels and you are borrowing from next quarter's relationship in the form of missed response targets.

## The exit criteria are the contract

The act that makes the exit real is writing the exit criteria before go-live, and using them as a contract that both sides sign off on when it is met. The full arc, from the readiness planning two weeks before go-live through the taper to the client-signed exit, is where the discipline lives.

![Run hypercare as a four-phase taper from readiness before go-live through intensive care, step-down, and exit, ending when six written exit criteria all hold for five consecutive business days](</diagrams/handover-hypercare-exit-criteria-2026/4-exit-criteria.png>)

Supportbench's hypercare playbook specifies six conditions that must all hold for five consecutive business days before the elevated window closes[^11]:

1. Ticket volume at or below 120 percent of the projected business-as-usual baseline.
2. No open critical-severity issues, and the high-severity backlog trending down.
3. No new recurring defect themes in the last five days, meaning the same root cause is not resurfacing.
4. First-contact resolution within 10 percent of the normal rate, so clients are not needing follow-ups to get answers.
5. Every critical workaround documented and owned, so nothing the client is relying on lives only in someone's memory.
6. A client stakeholder signs off on the handover summary.

Condition six is the one that matters most, and it is the one teams skip[^12]. An exit the client explicitly agrees to is a milestone. An exit they discover, because the proactive support quietly stopped and the fast response times quietly became ordinary, reads as a downgrade, and a downgrade is how a reference account turns into a churn risk. The sign-off makes the taper a shared decision instead of a silent withdrawal.

## Taper deliberately, and tell the client you are doing it

Hypercare is not a cliff. It is a taper, and the taper has to be announced. A four-phase structure keeps it honest: readiness in the two weeks before go-live when the support and delivery teams align on known issues and the at-risk accounts; intensive care in weeks one and two with daily standups, a visible tag on every hypercare ticket, and proactive outreach before the client has to ask; step-down in weeks three and four as volume falls, with standups dropping to mid-week and check-ins going weekly while staffing returns toward baseline; and then exit in weeks four to six, measured against the criteria and closed with the client's sign-off on the written summary[^13].

Published to the client, this taper reads as competence. Stage it silently and the same taper reads as abandonment; abandoned accounts escalate to executives instead of to the support queue[^14]. A consulting team that finishes a delivery and then runs a hypercare window it announced, staffed, and ended on agreed criteria is not doing a favor. It is protecting the very thing the whole engagement bought: a client who trusts you enough to refer you.

## Where the exit lives in a consulting P&L

There is a reason the exit phase is the bit consultants most want to skip: it feels unbillable. Go-live is done, the team is high value on the bench or on the next account, and re-staffing the same delivery to answer handover questions looks like giving away margin.

The framing that fixes this is to stop treating the exit as post-sale support and start treating it as part of the deliverable. Agree it at contract time, scope it in the work plan, and put an owner on it the way you put an owner on the build. The handover backlog, the runbook, and the hypercare window then have the same status as the architecture: things you promised to deliver, costed and scheduled, not courtesies you hope to absorb. A fixed-fee engagement that does not scope its exit is a fixed-fee engagement that eats its own margin on unplanned support, which is exactly the failure mode we dissected in our work on [estimation and scope control](/field-notes/estimation-after-ai-2026).

## A self-sufficiency test proves the handover landed

The last and most honest tool in the exit kit is the self-sufficiency test: can the client's own operator run the recovery procedure from the runbook, without phoning anyone on your team? Run it as a rehearsal that mimics a real incident. Hand the operator the runbook, invent an incident, and watch. If they reach the resolution, the handover worked. If they stall because a step references a dashboard they cannot reach, or a credential they do not hold, or a decision nobody explained, the handover has a hole, and you found it while your people are still on contract to fix it instead of three months later as a support ticket with a different team's time attached[^15]. Documentation that has never been executed by the person who has to rely on it is a wish, not a deliverable.

## The work is complete when the client does not need you

The acceptance gate closes the money. The handover closes the work. One measures against the client's willingness to pay for what was built; the other measures against the client's ability to live with it once you are gone. They are different phases, run by different disciplines, against different evidence, and a delivery that executes one and skips the other has shipped a feature and lost a relationship.

The discipline is not hard to adopt. Write the exit criteria before go-live, alongside the acceptance criteria, and treat both as contract. Build the knowledge transfer as a continuous backlog instead of a final download, and let tacit and implicit knowledge ride in on shadowing and scenario drills rather than hoping the wiki captured them. Author the runbook from real incidents with a pager playbook that answers every alert, and rehearse it on your own people before you are gone. Staff the hypercare window on the surge, announce the taper, and exit on agreed evidence with a signed handover summary.

Do that and the handover stops being the scary sentence six weeks after go-live. It becomes the reason the next account on that client's sidebar is referrals instead of tickets, which is where consulting margin actually lives.
