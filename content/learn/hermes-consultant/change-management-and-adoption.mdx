# Lesson 19: Change Management and Adoption: Getting Real People to Actually Use the System

The handover from the last lesson gets the client a system they can run. It does not get them a system they will run. Those are different outcomes, and the gap between them is where most agent engagements quietly die. You install the agent, you document it, you move the credentials into the client's hands, and then you leave. Three months later you check in and find the intake team still typing every claim into the old spreadsheet by hand, while the agent you built sits idle in a channel nobody opens. The system worked. Nobody used it. That is a failed engagement.

This lesson is about closing that gap. It teaches the transferable skill of getting real people to change their habits enough to adopt the thing you built. It is not a marketing course and it is not a pep talk. It is a discipline with named concepts, concrete moves, and a worked example, and it is the discipline that separates the consultant who delivers software from the consultant who delivers change. Hermes Agent is the vehicle because it exposes the adoption problem clearly: you can watch a perfectly good agent go unused, and you can watch specific design choices turn it into something people actually reach for.

## Define the terms: change, adoption, resistance, and the owner

Start by naming the ideas, because the single biggest failure in this work is that consultants talk about "change" when they mean two different things.

**Change** is the alteration in how a system works: a new agent takes over the intake queue, the weekly summary is automated, the lookup is now a question instead of a search. Change is technical. It is what you ship.

**Adoption** is the alteration in how people behave: the intake team routes claims to the agent, the manager reads the summary instead of the spreadsheet, the analyst asks the bot the lookup instead of remembering the query. Adoption is behavioral. It is what determines whether the change sticks.

The distinction matters because you can have change without adoption, and a consultant who delivers change but not adoption has not delivered value. This is the trap the framing from lesson 4 (value and ROI) warned about. A discounted ROI calculated against a system nobody uses is not an ROI at all. It is a cost.

**Resistance** is what adoption looks like when you have not done the work. It is almost never laziness or stubbornness. It is almost always a rational response to an unanswered question: Why should I change my workflow? What happens if the agent is wrong? Is this going to cost me my job? Is this thing going to make me look bad? Resistance is information. It tells you which of those questions is unanswered, and which person is carrying it.

**The owner** is the named human whose job it is to make adoption happen and who has the authority to remove the obstacles. This is not the person who approved the budget. It is the person who runs the team the agent touches, who can say "we use this now," and who will be held accountable if they do not. Without an owner, adoption is nobody's job, and nobody's job does not get done.

Adoption work, done right, is the practice of answering those questions for the people who matter and making the new behavior the path of least resistance. Everything else in this lesson is a method for doing exactly that.

## The adoption problem is not a training problem

Here is the mistake almost every technically strong consultant makes on a first adoption effort. They build the agent, and when nobody uses it they run a training session. Then they run another training session, and write better documentation, and build a nicer dashboard. None of it works, and they conclude the users are resistant, when in fact the users were never the problem.

Training and documentation treat the obstacle as ignorance. Most adoption failures are not ignorance. The users know how the agent works. They have a different problem: the agent asks them to do something that does not fit how they already operate, or it asks them to trust something they have no reason to trust, or it competes with a habit that is already working well enough.

Think about it from the intake team's seat. Their job has a rhythm. They type a claim into a form, they move to the next one, and when the shift ends they are done. You hand them an agent that can route claims faster, but using it means changing where they look, changing how they enter data, and trusting that a black box got it right. That is not ignorance. That is a genuine tradeoff, and until you make the new way better in the specific way that matters to them, they will correctly keep the old way. The spreadsheet is not resistance. The spreadsheet is the incumbent, and the incumbent always wins on default.

The consultant's job is to make adoption easier than the incumbent, not to make it available. That is the reframe. You are not adding a tool. You are competing with a habit, and you win the way any competitor wins, by being better where the user feels the difference.

## Design around the work, not around the agent

The most powerful adoption lever is not training or communication. It is design, specifically designing the agent to live where the people already work and to match the way they already think. This is why the agent platform matters as much as the agent.

A practical version of this in Hermes is the messaging gateway. An agent that requires people to open a terminal, or to go to a separate dashboard, or to learn a new interface is asking for adoption. An agent that sits in the same chat channel the team already uses for their work, whether that is Slack, Teams, Discord, or Telegram, is not asking for anything. It is showing up in a place people already look. The messaging gateway lets a single agent run across all those platforms, so the same automation appears in the channel the team actually lives in, rather than forcing the team to come to it. [^1]

The same principle applies to how the work is delivered. A scheduled automation that pushes a summary into a channel at a set time does not require anyone to remember to ask for it. It arrives. Hermes cron runs jobs on a schedule and delivers the output to the configured platform, so the weekly report the manager needs shows up on Monday morning without anyone having to trigger it. [^2] That is adoption by removal of effort. The manager does not need to form a new habit of "check the dashboard." The work comes to them.

The second design lever is visibility. People trust what they can see. An agent whose activity is opaque is an agent people suspect. Hermes kanban makes the agent's work legible: every task, every handoff, and every comment is a row anyone can read, so a team can watch the agent work the same way they would watch a colleague, and see when it is stuck or wrong instead of guessing. [^3] That visible record converts trust from a leap of faith into something a skeptical user can verify for themselves.

The third lever is consistency. People adopt a system they can predict. Skills in Hermes are the mechanism for that: a named, reusable procedure the agent follows, which means the same kind of request produces the same kind of result every time. [^4] The intake analyst who knows that asking for a claim status always produces the same reliable lookup can build a habit around it. The analyst who gets a slightly different answer each time cannot, and will go back to the thing that never surprises them.

## Find the person whose problem it is

Adoption is not a mass-market problem. It is a one-person problem repeated. Before you design anything or schedule any training, find the person whose problem the agent most directly solves, and make that person your first and loudest user.

This is the champion move, and it is often the difference between a tool that lingers and a tool that spreads. You are not looking for the most senior person, and you are not looking for the most enthusiastic person. You are looking for the person with the most painful, most frequent, most visible version of the problem the agent solves. For the intake agent, that is the analyst who processes the most claims and who complains loudest about the lookup. That person has real skin in the game. They will try the agent not because you told them to but because it makes their own afternoon shorter, and when it does, they will tell their teammates in a way no slide deck ever could.

Your job is to make that champion succeed and to make their success visible. Give them the agent first, work out the rough edges on their real work (not a demo), and then when it works, the champion does the selling for you. The adoption lesson from the AI at Work track applies here directly: people adopt tools their respected peers are already using and can vouch for, far more than they adopt tools their managers mandate. [^5]

The owner and the champion are different people and you need both. The owner has authority and can remove obstacles, but authority does not spread a tool. The champion has credibility with the people who will use it, but credibility does not unblock a budget or a security review. You need the owner to say "we use this now" and the champion to make it true.

## Worked example: getting the claims-intake team to actually use the agent

Bring it together on the engagement you have carried through the series. In lesson 13 you secured the claims-intake agent and in lesson 18 you handed it over. Now face the hard part: the intake team has not adopted it. They still type claims into the old form and the agent sits unused. You have already shipped change. You have not shipped adoption.

Start by finding the person whose problem it is. You do not run a training session yet. You watch the intake team for a day, or you ask the owner, and you identify the analyst who processes the most claims and who most visibly struggles with the lookup. That is your champion. You sit with them and you ask what actually happens when they route a claim, not what the process doc says. That conversation is where resistance becomes information. The champion tells you the truth the approval committee never would: the agent works but they have to open a separate channel to use it, the lookup takes three steps when the old search took one, and nobody trusts it because they cannot see what it did with last week's claims.

Then you design around the work. You move the agent into the channel the team already uses, so routing a claim is a message where they already are, not a trip to a new interface. [^1] You schedule the weekly summary to push into the channel on Monday morning so the manager does not have to remember to ask for it. [^2] You make the agent's work visible by pointing the team at the kanban record, where they can watch the routing and see the status of each claim instead of taking the agent's word for it. [^3] You lock the lookup into a skill so every analyst gets the same reliable answer every time, which is what turns a one-off test into a repeatable habit. [^4]

Then you make the champion's success concrete and visible. You work out the remaining rough edges on their real claims, not on a demo set. You let the champion run it for a week and you fix what breaks. And you do not announce it in a memo. You let the champion's next routing conversation show a teammate how much faster it is, and you let that peer see it work on real work. That is the adoption engine: one person whose afternoon is shorter, seen by someone who trusts them. [^5]

You do not declare success when the agent is installed. You declare success when you can count the routing happening in the channel and the spreadsheet going quiet. That is the measure, and it is the one the last lesson's handover actually depended on all along.

## The pitfalls to avoid

**Treating adoption as a training problem.** When nobody uses the agent, the instinct is to train harder. Training fixes ignorance. Most adoption failures are not ignorance, they are a fit problem or a trust problem. Diagnose which one you actually have before you schedule a session.

**Adding a tool instead of beating an incumbent.** The old spreadsheet always wins on default. Until using the agent is easier in the specific way the user feels, they will correctly keep the old way. You are competing with a habit, so design to beat it, not to be available alongside it.

**Forcing users to come to the agent.** Every new interface, every separate dashboard, every terminal command is an adoption tax. Put the agent where people already work and deliver the output where they already look, and you remove the tax instead of charging it.

**Trusting mandate over credibility.** A manager saying "we use this now" does not spread a tool. People adopt what respected peers use and vouch for. Find the champion with the painful problem and make their success visible. Authority unblocks, credibility spreads, and you need both.

**Measuring delivery instead of usage.** "The agent shipped" is not adoption. "The team routes in the channel and the spreadsheet went quiet" is. Pick the usage measure before you start, and report against it, or you will call a failure a success.

## Try it

Take the engagement you have been carrying and design the adoption plan, on paper, before you touch the agent:

1. Name the change and the adoption separately. What technically ships, and what must people start doing differently for it to matter?
2. Identify the incumbent. What do the users do today instead, and why is it working well enough for them to keep it?
3. Name your champion. Who has the most painful, most frequent version of the problem the agent solves, and how will you make their afternoon measurably shorter?
4. Name your owner. Which human can say "we use this now" and has the authority to remove an obstacle, and how is that different from the person who approved the budget?
5. List the adoption taxes in your current design. Where must a user come to the agent, remember to ask, or trust something they cannot see, and how would you remove each one?
6. Pick the usage measure. What observable behavior will you count, and what does the old behavior look like when adoption has happened?

If you can answer all six, you have an adoption plan. If your instinct is to skip straight to "schedule training and send a memo," that is the builder reflex again, the one this whole series has been training you out of. A system nobody uses is not a system, and training does not make people change. Making the new way easier than the old way does.

## What's next

You can now get real people to actually use the system you built: you separate change from adoption, design the agent into the way people already work, find the champion whose problem it is, and measure success in usage rather than shipped features. But adoption brings a new responsibility with it. Once the whole team is routing real claims through the agent, the stakes of a mistake go up, because a tool people use has far more reach than a tool they ignore. The next lesson is about governance and guardrails: acceptable use, protecting personally identifiable information, and responsible automation. Where this lesson made the system stick, the next one makes sure that when it is used, it is used safely and within the rules the client actually cares about.
