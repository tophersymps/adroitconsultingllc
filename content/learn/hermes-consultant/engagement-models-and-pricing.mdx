# Lesson 25: Engagement Models and Pricing: Fixed-Fee vs Hourly vs Retainer, and When Each Fits

Lesson 24 made you calm in a live incident: preserve the evidence, change one variable at a time, keep a rollback you can use, keep the client informed through the worst of it. That calm is a skill, and there is a business question underneath it that you have handled so far on instinct. If an incident runs long, are you paid for the diagnosis or only for the fix? If a client asks for "just a small change," does the price change with it? If a team wants you around for four months, are you selling hours or availability?

This lesson answers those questions. It teaches you the three standard ways to price consulting work, what each one really buys and sells, and how to pick the one that matches the risk and the delivery style of an engagement. The mechanical skill from the last few lessons, the scoping, the verification, the deployment, is what you deliver. The model you price it under decides whether you are fairly paid for it and whether the incentives line up for both sides.

## Why the model matters more than the rate

New consultants default to hourly because it is the easiest number to produce. You pick a rate, you count hours, you bill. That instinct misses the real decision. The rate is the multiplier. The engagement model is the contract over who bears the risk of being wrong about scope, and that risk is where the money, and the trust, actually lives.

Think about it from the client's side. When a client buys from you, they are not really buying hours. They are buying an outcome: a working agent, a configured deployment, a system that stops breaking. The question every pricing model answers is how you and the client split the risk that the work turns out to be bigger, harder, or different than anyone expected. Whoever takes that risk needs to be compensated for it. Whoever does not take it should not be asked to pay for it.

That is the lens the rest of this lesson uses. For each model, ask three things: what does it price, what risk moves to which side, and when does it fit.

## Fixed-fee: you take the uncertainty, you keep the upside

A fixed-fee engagement sells a defined outcome for a defined price. You agree up front that a working agent implementation, a deployed dashboard, a configured deployment pipeline, costs a set amount, and you deliver it for that amount no matter how many hours it takes.

The fixed-fee model puts scope risk on you. If the work is exactly what you scoped, you profit from the efficiency you built in lessons 14 through 20, the 30 to 45 minute tasks, the reuse, the verification that catches mistakes early. If the work turns out bigger than you scoped, you eat the overrun. That is the deal, and it is the reason fixed-fee projects are priced with room built in for the uncertainty.

When does fixed-fee fit? When the outcome is well defined and the scope is something you have done before. A client who needs a standard Hermes deployment, a Kanban pipeline stood up, a set of skills packaged, can get a fixed price because you know roughly what that work costs. The client likes it because the budget is predictable. You should like it because a well-scoped fixed-fee job rewards the efficiency you are good at.

The danger is pricing a fixed-fee job whose scope you cannot see. If the requirements are vague, if the integration is novel, if you have never done this kind of work before, a fixed price is a bet against yourself. You will either overprice it and lose the client, or underprice it and work for free. The fix is to price fixed-fee only when you can define the outcome tightly, and to scope the boundary legally, which is the subject of lesson 26.

## Hourly: you sell time, the client takes the uncertainty

An hourly engagement sells time at a rate. You bill for the hours you work, the client pays for the hours you work, and if the work turns out bigger than expected, the client pays more. Scope risk sits on the client's side.

When it fits: when the scope cannot be known in advance and the work is genuinely open-ended. Exploratory work fits hourly, because the whole point is that you do not yet know what the work is. Short bursts of well-defined effort fit hourly, because the overhead of a fixed-fee contract is not worth it for a weekend's worth of work. And work where the client wants flexibility, the ability to steer day by day, fits hourly, because the client pays for exactly the effort they use.

Hourly has a reputation problem, and you should know the shape of it. A client who hears "hourly" hears two fears: an incentive to stretch the work, and an unpredictable total. You defuse the first by being fast and by showing your work; the hour meter is honest only when the client can see what each hour bought. You defuse the second by pairing an hourly rate with a good scope and a rough total forward estimate, so the client is not buying a blank check.

Hourly also changes your incentives in a way worth naming. When you are hourly, speed costs you money in a narrow sense. The temptation is to slow down. Resist it. The consultants who win repeat work are the ones who are fast and honest about what their hours bought, because a fast hourly consultant is the cheapest, easiest-to-trust option a client will ever find. Slowness is a tax on your own reputation.

## Retainer: you sell availability, the client buys attention

A retainer exchanges a recurring fee for a standing amount of your time and attention over a period. The client pays monthly, and in return you are available: you monitor the system, you handle incidents, you field the "quick question," you do the small ongoing work that would otherwise be a parade of tiny hourly charges.

A retainer is not a discount on hours. It is the answer to a different need. The client who wants a retainer does not want to re-buy you every week. They want continuity: someone who knows their setup, who watches their logs, who does not have to re-learn the project on each new incident. Lesson 24's incident work is exactly the kind of thing a retainer covers, because that work cannot be priced per incident without being either overpriced or bankrupting the client, and because the value of the response depends on knowing the system before the fire starts.

Retainers shift the incentive picture again. Under a retainer, your incentive is to keep the system boring, to prevent incidents rather than bill for fighting them, because a system that runs smoothly is a retainer that renews. That alignment is the genuine selling point of the model. You are paid to make yourself less urgently needed, and a client who sees that will keep you around.

When does a retainer fit? When there is ongoing operational need: monitoring, maintenance, incident response, a stream of small changes. It fits when the relationship is meant to last months, not by the task. It does not fit a one-shot implementation, because a one-shot implementation has no ongoing need, and paying a monthly retainer for no work is a waste of the client's money that will breed resentment.

## A worked example: capturing value across all three

The three models are not rivals. A healthy consulting relationship often uses all three at once for different parts of the same engagement, and the skill is choosing which for which.

Take a client who needs Hermes Agent deployed for their team. The implementation is well understood, you have done it a dozen times, so you quote it fixed-fee: a defined system, installed, configured, documented, tested, for a defined price. Good. That becomes the reference point.

Along the way the client asks for a few exploratory things, a workflow you have never seen, a corner of the product you are not sure about. You do those on an hourly basis, because you cannot price them up front and their cost is genuinely uncertain. The client understands, because the hours are for the unknown part and the fixed fee was for the known part.

After launch, the arrangement shifts again. The client wants you available to monitor, to respond to incidents, to handle the small stream of change requests. That is a retainer. It is not more fixed-fee work, because the work is not predictable task by task. It is not hourly, because the client does not want to approve time every time the dashboard throws an alert. It is a monthly fee for your attention, and it covers the reality of running a system day to day.

Notice what that does. The client gets a predictable price for the predictable work, pays only for the genuinely uncertain exploration, and buys continuity for the ongoing operations. You get a defined profit on the part you are good at, fair compensation for the parts you cannot predict, and a recurring relationship instead of a one-off check. Each part is priced under the model that matches its risk, and nobody resents the arrangement because nobody was asked to pay for a risk they were not really taking.

![One Hermes deployment split across three models: the implementation priced fixed-fee, the during-delivery unknowns priced hourly, and the ongoing operations as a retainer, each chosen to match the risk of that part.](</diagrams/engagement-models-and-pricing/2-one-engagement-three-models.png>)

This is the pattern to remember for lesson 26, because the match between a price model and a scope boundary is where almost all pricing pain comes from. A fixed-fee price without a scope boundary is a timer running against your own money. A retainer without a defined set of included detail is an argument waiting to happen. The model does not remove the need to define the boundary; it decides who is hurt when the boundary turns out to have been drawn in the wrong place.

## A live example in Hermes Agent terms

To make this concrete, think about the actual work you do as a Hermes consultant. The tools are the same in every model. What changes is how you describe them and what the client is paying for.

A fixed-fee implementation, in Hermes terms, is: "I will stand up your agent, wire in the connectors from lessons 8 and 9, load the security model from lesson 13, set up the cron jobs and the monitoring from lessons 12 and 20, and prove it all works with the verification discipline from lesson 15." That is an outcome with a defined end. You price it as a unit, and you carry the risk that one of those pieces is harder than expected, because you know the shape of the work well enough to price the risk.

An hourly exploratory session, in Hermes terms, is: "You want to see whether agent deployment makes sense for your team at all. Let's run a discovery, check what your workflows look like, and measure what an implementation would actually move. I bill for the hours, because we are both still finding out what the work is." That is the lesson 1 and lesson 2 discovery work, priced honestly as exploration.

A retainer, in Hermes terms, is: "I stay connected to your deployment. I watch the monitoring dashboard from lesson 24, I respond when a cron job breaks, I run the playbooks from lesson 20 when something needs attention, and I keep your skills index current as you add use cases. One monthly fee covers all of it." That is continuity, and it is priced under the model that rewards preventing fires rather than fighting them.

## Pricing traps to name out loud

The pros and cons of each model point at a set of mistakes that come from picking a model without checking the risk match. Name them so you can spot them in your own quotes and in other consultants' advice.

![The five pricing traps consultants should name out loud, from treating a fixed-fee quote as a guess to pricing the unknown the same as the known.](</diagrams/engagement-models-and-pricing/3-five-pricing-traps.png>)

**Treating a fixed-fee quote as a guess.** A fixed price is not a guess at the hours times a rate. It is a decision that you can see the scope well enough to own the overrun. If you are guessing, you are not ready for fixed-fee, and the honest move is to say so and quote the work differently.

**Billing hourly without a scope when the scope exists.** If the work has a defined, known outcome, selling it hourly is leaving money on the table and inviting the client to distrust your incentives. A client who can be given a real fixed price should usually get one on the part of the work that is predictable.

**Selling a retainer as if it were hours in bulk.** A retainer priced as "20 hours for a discount" is really bulk hourly, and it inherits all of hourly's incentive problems without a retainer's alignment. A real retainer names the set of included work, not just the hours it might cover. [^1]

**Undercounting the overhead of setup.** The first meeting, the scoping call, the discovery, the documentation of your own environment, all of it is real work with real cost. Whatever model you price, make sure the setup is either inside the quote or explicitly hours, never silently free.

**Pricing the unknown the same as the known.** If one part of a job is routine and another is a research question, do not fold them into a single number. Separate them, price the known part fixed and the unknown part hourly, and let each carry its own risk.

## Try it

You are quoting a piece of work. The client wants you to deploy Hermes Agent for their team, and they want you around afterward to keep it running. Work through the pricing decision on paper:

1. Split the work into its three natural parts: the implementation, the during-delivery unknowns, and the ongoing operations. Which is the known outcome, which is the exploration, and which is the continuity?
2. For each part, name the model that matches its risk. What does the client's risk look like under your choice, and what does your risk look like? Are you asking the client to pay for a risk that is really yours, or vice versa?
3. Price the fixed part. What is the defined outcome, and what room have you built in for the overrun you would be owning?
4. Define the retainer part. What concrete set of work is included in the monthly fee, and what is explicitly outside it? What would you do for a client who asks for something on the outside of that boundary?
5. Write the honest one-liner you would say to the client explaining why the work is split the way it is, not as a negotiation trick but as a real description of who is carrying which risk.

If you can split the work and name the risk match for each part, you can quote an engagement that neither side resents. If you catch yourself reaching for "just bill it hourly and see" as the answer to step two, that is the missing decision this lesson exists to force. Pick a model on purpose, for each part, and name the risk you are asking the client to take alongside the risk you are taking yourself.

## What's next

You can now choose an engagement model that matches the delivery style and risk of the work: fixed-fee where you own a defined outcome, hourly where the scope is genuinely unknown, retainer where the client needs continuity. But choosing a model is not the same as protecting yourself inside it. The next lesson is about contracts and scoping: statements of work, boundaries, and avoiding scope creep. It covers how to write a scope boundary that protects both sides, what a statement of work is for, and how to keep a well-meaning client's evolving requests from silently turning a fixed-fee project into an unbounded one. Where this lesson made sure you are paid fairly for the model you choose, the next one makes sure the scope stays exactly where the price assumed it was.
