# Lesson 24: Troubleshooting in Production: Diagnosing a Broken Agent Without Breaking It More

By lesson 21 you could tell when an agent was quietly failing. This lesson is about the moment you find out and everything that happens next, under pressure, in a system the client depends on. The report did not fire. The intake agent stopped replying, or worse, started replying with the wrong thing. The client is asking what happened, and the workspace is right there, open, editable, waiting for you to fix it. The single most common cause of a bad production incident is not the original failure. It is what the consultant did after, trying to fix it without first understanding it.

The craft you need is diagnostic discipline: the ability to reach a root cause without making the system worse, and to repair it in a way you can undo if the repair does not hold. It transfers to any agent platform, because it is really about a few habits that are independent of the tool. You preserve the evidence before you touch anything. You change one thing at a time and prove each change before the next. You keep a snapshot you can roll back to. And you communicate with the client throughout, instead of going quiet while you thrash. This lesson teaches those habits with Hermes Agent as the working example, because it gives you the concrete pieces: full session records, a cron health check, an event log, and checkpoints that snapshot your work before destructive operations. Your job is to assemble them into a reflex that holds when the pressure is on.

## Define the terms: symptom, root cause, and blast radius

Start by naming the distinction that keeps you out of trouble.

A symptom is what you observed: the job failed, the reply never came, the output had the wrong value. A symptom is the way a problem surfaces, and it is almost never the cause. The cause, or root cause, is the underlying break that produced the symptom: a config key that changed, a source system that renamed its table, a skill that stopped loading because its required credential was revoked. You cannot fix a symptom, because the symptom will come back the moment the surface condition returns. You can only fix the root cause, and to find it you have to investigate, which is a different activity from repairing.

Blast radius is the scope of a change's effect, and it is the concept that makes "without breaking it more" precise. Every change you make has a blast radius: the set of sessions and jobs that could be affected if the change is wrong. Editing one cron job's prompt touches that job. Editing a provider config you share across the whole fleet touches every job and every interactive session. Restarting the gateway touches everything that runs through it. The discipline is to shrink the blast radius of your diagnostic changes until it covers only what you understand, so that if your fix is wrong, the damage is bounded and reversible.

Which brings you to the framing that decides whether you fix or make it worse. You are always allowed to change the system. You are never allowed to change it blindly and then hope. Every change, even a small one, needs three answers first: what did I observe, what do I believe the root cause is, and what will I do if this change does not work. If you cannot answer all three, you are not ready to change anything yet, and the correct move is more investigation, not more action.

## The first reflex: preserve the evidence, then diagnose

The most destructive instinct in a live incident is to start repairing immediately, because the first thing you do to fix is often the same thing that destroys the evidence you needed to understand the failure. You delete the corrupt file and regenerate it, then realize you no longer have the corrupted original to inspect. You restart the gateway, and the session history that would have shown the last steps is now in a state you cannot compare with anything. You edit the prompt, and you have lost track of what it said before, which is the exact piece of information you needed.

So the first move is always the same: before you change anything, you secure the evidence. In Hermes, every conversation is recorded as a session in a SQLite database with the full message history, the tool calls and their results, the model that ran, the token counts, and the start and end timestamps, and you can search across sessions to find the exact conversation that went wrong. [^1] That record is the ground truth of what the agent actually did, not what you assume it did, and it is the first place you look. When the intake agent stopped replying, the session record tells you whether it crashed, hit a tool error, or produced output that was never delivered, and those are three different problems with three different fixes.

The same instinct applies at the infrastructure layer. The cron health check surfaces every job with a failed run, a failed delivery, or a schedule that silently stopped firing, and it reports run success and delivery success separately, so you can distinguish a job that failed to run from a job that ran and could not reach its target. [^2] And before the failure even happened, Hermes runs pre-dispatch configuration validation that blocks a misconfigured job as blocked_config, delivers one alert, and makes no LLM call at all, so a broken job fails loudly and never spends tokens. [^2] All of that evidence exists precisely so you can diagnose without thrashing.

Here is the habit in its most concrete form. When you suspect a problem in the underlying data store, the canonical transcript lives in state.db, and the discipline is to copy the whole SQLite image before you do anything to it. The documentation is blunt about how to do this safely: state.db, state.db-wal, and state.db-shm are one live SQLite image, and you must not copy them independently, because you can turn a damaged-but-readable database into one that no longer opens at all. [^5] You stop the process that can write to it, you copy the three files together, and only then do you consider repair. That is the entire lesson in one example: the fastest way to make an incident permanently worse is to touch the one piece of state you do not understand, without a copy of it, while it is still live.

## Change one variable at a time, and prove each change

Once the evidence is preserved, the diagnostic loop is boring on purpose, and that is why it works. Each cycle changes exactly one thing, then checks the one thing it was meant to change, and only then moves on. The agent that is broken because a skill stopped loading requires one change, loading the skill, and one check, that the skill resolves. The job that is broken because a provider key rotated requires one change, correcting the key, and one check, that the provider authenticates. If you fix three things at once and the job still fails, you have to undo all three to learn anything, and you have spent the recovery on trial and error instead of diagnosis.

The opposite of this is the seductive move that ruins incidents: changing multiple things at random until the symptom goes away. It sometimes works, which is why it is so tempting, and it always leaves you with a working system you do not understand and cannot reproduce. A week later the same latent bug fires again, only now you have no idea which of your many changes was holding it off. The one-variable discipline costs a little more time up front and saves you from that cycle permanently, because when it works, you know the single change that fixed it, and that knowledge is the deliverable that keeps the incident from recurring.

Hermes gives you the safety structure that makes one-variable changes cheap to reverse. Checkpoints are automatic snapshots taken before destructive operations such as file edits and certain terminal commands, stored in a shared shadow git repository that never touches your real project, and you can restore from them with a single command or preview the diff of what you are about to undo. [^4] Checkpoints are opt-in, so you turn them on as a matter of professional habit, and then every change you make is one command away from undone. When your fix does not hold, you restore, change the next single variable, and check again, without ever having to remember what the previous state was.

## Know when a restart is the answer, and when it is a gamble

There is a category of failure that tempts the heaviest shortcut: the whole-gateway restart. The agent is frozen, hung, or spinning, and the panic move is to restart the process and see if it comes back healthy. Sometimes that is genuinely the right call, and you need to be able to tell the difference, because a restart is the change with the largest blast radius there is, and it is almost never the first thing you try.

The pattern is straightforward. A restart is a valid repair when the failure is a live-process problem: the agent is stuck in a loop, holding a poisoned connection, or wedged on a bad in-memory state, and the fix is to clear that process state. It is a gamble when the failure lives in persistent state, because restarting does not touch the thing that is actually broken. If a job fails on every run because its provider key is invalid or its skill is misconfigured, restarting the gateway will give you the same failure, now with less evidence, because the pre-run logs are gone. The gateway's dashboard warnings exist to help you judge this: it surfaces warnings when the host is low on memory or disk, or when the agent restarted unexpectedly under memory pressure, and that tells you a struggling process is likely the cause of a freeze. [^3] A restart to clear memory pressure is one thing. A restart to clear a config error is a prayer.

The same judgment extends to the data recovery ladder. When the state database itself is corrupt, the disciplined recovery is a specific, staged sequence: stop every process that can open it, run an inspection, repair through the backup-and-promotion path, verify the canonical row counts did not decrease, and only then restart the gateway. [^5] You never delete canonical rows to make a derived-index error disappear, and you never run the repair while the gateway is still up, because a running writer can corrupt the very repair you are trying to make. [^5] Every step there is the opposite of a panic restart, and every step exists because someone once skipped it and made a recoverable problem unrecoverable. That is the pattern: the more broken the system, the more careful the moves, not less.

## The client contract during an incident

The technical discipline matters, but the part that protects your reputation as a consultant is the communication, and it starts before the incident ever happens. You establish the reporting contract up front: what the client can expect when something breaks, how fast they will hear from you, and what you will and will not do to a live system. When the incident does hit, the contract turns into a simple, repeatable rhythm. You tell the client what you observed as soon as you see it. You tell them what you are checking, in plain terms, and roughly how long the diagnosis will take. And you tell them the moment you decide to change something live, with the specific change and its blast radius, before you make it, so a surprise is replaced by a plan.

The failure mode to avoid is silence. Going dark while you investigate is the single worst communication move, because the client fills the gap with the worst possible story, and because an hour of quiet thrashing reads as incompetence even when your diagnosis was careful the whole time. A short update that says "I found the failure, the weekly job could not reach its delivery target, I am checking what changed in the routing config now, this does not affect the other jobs, I will confirm the fix within the hour" is worth more than a perfect technical fix delivered in silence. Notice what that message does: it names the observed failure, it names what is being investigated, it states the blast radius explicitly, and it commits to a verification target. That is the consulting version of the evidence-first discipline, and it earns trust the same way, by showing work rather than claiming it.

This is also where the pre-incident work pays for itself. The rolling safety net of checkpoints, the documented recovery procedure, and the habit of reading the session log before changing anything are not things you can build in the middle of a fire. They are built in the calm, and they are the reason a careful consultant can tell a client "I will change one thing, verify it, and I can roll it back if it does not hold," and mean it. When you can say that with proof, you have turned the worst part of the job, the live incident, into the part where you look the most in control.

## Worked example: the claims-intake agent goes quiet

Put it together on the engagement you have carried through the series. One Monday morning the client writes that the claims-intake agent stopped replying to claims since the weekend, and several new claims have no confirmation. Your symptoms are two: no confirmations going out, and you do not yet know why.

You do not touch anything yet. First you preserve the evidence. You check the session log for the intake sessions around the time it went quiet, and you see the agent took a call from the claims system, attempted a tool call that wrote the claim record, and the call failed with a database connection error. [^1] That changes the diagnosis immediately: the agent did not stop replying, it stopped being able to write, and the reason is in that failed tool call, not in the agent's config. You check the cron health check to confirm no scheduled job was collateral damage, and you see the other jobs are healthy, which tells you the blast radius is isolated to the intake path. [^2] Two minutes of reading before one minute of changing, and you already know the shape of the problem.

Then you isolate the variable. The connection error points you at the shared database, and you check whether it is a credentials problem, a reachability problem, or a full disk by looking at the dashboard warnings, which right now show nothing about memory or disk pressure. [^3] You decide the most likely single cause is a rotated credential on the intake service account, so you change exactly that one credential, and you prove it with a single write against the same database from a one-off session. It succeeds. You have made one change and verified it, and you have a working claims-intake agent again within the hour.

Now the part that separates a fix from a repair. You turn the evidence into a rollback-safe state before you declare victory, because the intake writes hit a database you did not change, and you want the writes themselves protected. You confirm checkpoints are enabled so the file-level change you made is one command from undone if the fix does not hold overnight. [^4] You write the client a short update: symptom, root cause, the single change, the blast radius, and the verification you ran. And you note in the handover that the underlying trigger, a credential that rotated and was not caught, is the thing that actually needs watching, so it can be prevented instead of diagnosed from scratch next time.

## The pitfalls to avoid

**Changing things until it heals.** The system sometimes recovers, and you have learned nothing and cannot reproduce the fix. Change one variable at a time and prove each one, so the fix is a real root cause, not an accident.

**Restarting to fix a config error.** A restart clears process state, not persistent misconfiguration. If the same job fails every run for the same reason, restarting buys you the same failure with less evidence. Restart for process problems, diagnose the rest.

**Touching state you do not understand without a copy.** Copy the whole SQLite image, the sidecars together, before you touch a live store. Stop the writer first. The fastest way to make an incident permanently worse is to edit the one file you need to understand, live, with no backup.

**Deleting rows to clear a warning.** Never destroy canonical data to make a derived index error disappear. You cannot recover what you delete, and the "fix" erases the evidence of what was actually wrong.

**Going silent on the client.** Quiet thrashing reads as failure even when the diagnosis is careful. Send short updates that name the symptom, the investigation, the blast radius, and the verification target. Show the work.

**Fixing three things at once.** If it does not work you have to undo all of them to learn anything. One change, one check, then the next.

## Try it

Take the engagement you have been carrying and write its incident-response plan on paper:

1. Name the evidence. What records will you preserve before you change anything, and where do they live? Write down the exact command or tool call you would use to secure a copy of the state before touching it.
2. Name the symptom and the likely root cause. Pick the claim outcome from lesson 23 and describe what you would observe, what you believe actually broke, and what evidence would confirm or refute it.
3. Name the smallest change. What is the single variable you would change first, and what is the one check that proves it worked?
4. Name the rollback. How will you undo that change if it does not hold? Which snapshot or checkpoint protects you?
5. Name the restart test. Under what conditions would a whole-gateway restart be the right repair, and why would it not help a config error?
6. Name the update. Draft the short message you would send the client at the moment you find the failure, including the symptom, the investigation, the blast radius, and the verification target.

If you can answer all six, you can walk into a live incident and come out with the system fixed and the client's trust intact. If you find yourself writing "I would restart it and see" as the answer to step three, that is the gamble this lesson exists to remove. Go back and name the smallest change and the one check before you touch a live system.
