# Lesson 4: Framing Value and ROI: How to Talk to a Buyer, Not a Builder

Lesson 3 taught you how to find the real problem and write a problem statement the client agrees with. That earns you the right to propose a solution. This lesson is about the part that comes right after: justifying the price of fixing it. You found the value in discovery, but value you cannot put a number on is value the buyer cannot defend. This lesson teaches you how to measure an agent's value honestly, how to build an ROI case that survives a skeptical finance person, and how to talk about it in the language of the person who signs the check instead of the language of the person who builds the thing.

## Why framing value is the consultant's real job

Here is a truth that separates consultants from contractors. A contractor builds what you asked for. A consultant makes sure you understood the value before you paid for it. The build is only the visible fraction of the work. The framing, the measurement, and the honest math are what turn a demo into a decision.

Buyers are not skeptical of agents because they dislike technology. They are skeptical because every vendor in front of them is selling a promise, and promises are cheap. Your job is to make the promise auditable. When you show a buyer a number they can check next quarter, you have done something no sales deck does. You have given them a way to verify that the money was well spent. That is the single most effective thing a consultant can do to close a deal and keep the relationship.

The counterintuitive part is that honest framing helps your price, not just your credibility. A buyer who understands exactly what they are buying, and how they will know it worked, stops haggling over the fee and starts asking when you can start. Vagueness is what triggers price resistance. Clarity dissolves it.

## The builder's instinct, and why it fails with a buyer

The builder's instinct is to lead with what the thing can do. Look at how the tool actually works. An agent built on Hermes can run unattended on a schedule, load reusable knowledge on demand, and run as many separate profiles as the work needs. [^1] [^2] [^3] A builder hears that and thinks, that is a capable system, let me tell them about it.

The buyer does not care. The buyer's question is never what it can do. It is what it does for me. "It runs unattended on a schedule" is a feature. "Your support queue clears itself overnight, so nobody starts the day behind" is a value. The difference is the buyer in the second sentence. When you frame value, every capability has to answer one question: so what, in the buyer's business, in the buyer's units.

The units matter. A builder measures in tokens, jobs, and response times. A buyer measures in hours, errors, and money. The consultant's whole job in this phase is translation. You take what the agent does and convert it into the buyer's units. If you cannot do that conversion, you have not finished the work. You have just built something and left the value unclaimed.

## The three value layers

Value is not one number. It is three, and each one appeals to a different kind of buyer. Learn all three and you can lead with whichever one the person across the table will care about.

**Time saved is the honest baseline.** This is the easiest to measure and the easiest to defend, because it comes straight from the numbers you gathered in discovery. If the team spends forty hours a week on status checks, and the agent handles the checks that never need judgment, you can compute the hours recovered. Lead with time when the buyer is an operations person, because they live in hours.

**Error and rework cost is the one nobody budgets.** In discovery you asked what happens when this goes wrong. That answer is gold for value framing. Every wrong output has a cost: the time to find it, the time to fix it, the customer who got the wrong answer, the compliance near miss. This cost is real but invisible, because nobody writes a line item for it. When you put a number on it, you are showing the buyer money they are already losing. That is a much stronger argument than showing them money they might save, because it is not hypothetical. It is already happening.

**Capacity and quality are the upside.** The hours you recover are not just cost saved. They are people freed to do work that is harder to automate. The agent takes the repetitive third of the work, and the human keeps the part that needs judgment. Framed this way, the agent is not a headcount cut. It is a force multiplier. This matters because most buyers are not trying to fire people. They are trying to do more with the team they have. Show them that, and you align the automation with what they actually want.

## ROI math that survives scrutiny

Now you build the number. ROI framing fails in predictable ways, and almost all of them come from math that is too tidy to be true. A finance person will poke at three things, and your case has to survive all three.

**The baseline has to be real.** Use the numbers you pulled from the data in discovery, not the numbers someone remembered. If you quote a baseline the buyer can check and the real number is different, the whole case collapses. One verified number is worth ten estimates.

**The delta has to be honest about what the agent actually changes.** The agent does not do the whole job. It does the part that is high volume, low judgment, and governable. Your ROI has to claim only that part. If the agent can handle the routine checks but a human still reviews anything uncertain, then the hours you claim are the routine ones, and the review time is a cost, not a saving. Overclaiming the delta is how consultants destroy their own credibility.

**The recurring cost has to be in the math.** An agent is not free to run. There is the platform cost, the cost of the model calls, and, most importantly, the human time spent supervising and reviewing the agent's output. [^1] An unattended agent on a schedule runs by itself, but somebody still has to own it, watch the alerts, and handle the cases it flags. Put that cost in the equation on the same line as the saving. A buyer who discovers a hidden cost later will feel misled. A buyer who sees the honest cost up front and the net is still positive will trust you.

The shape of the math matters too. Show the number per month and per year, not just per year. Show the payback, the point where the savings have covered the build and the running cost. And show the running number separately from the one-time number, because the buyer needs to know what keeps paying after the project ends. The value of an agent is recurring. Your framing has to make that recurrence visible, because it is the reason the automation is worth more than the one-time build that produced it.

## The buyer's three questions

Every buyer, regardless of industry, is really asking three questions. Answer all three explicitly and you will not be chasing objections later.

**What does it cost?** Be specific and complete. Build, running cost, review time, the cost of the humans who supervise it. Give a number, not a range so wide it is meaningless.

**What do I get for it?** Name the outcome in the buyer's units. Hours recovered, errors avoided, capacity freed. Not capabilities. Outcomes.

**How do I know it is real?** This is the question most consultants forget, and it is the one that closes the deal. Promise a specific, measurable result by a specific date, with the metric you will report. When you name the acceptance cases in the proposal, as lesson 3 taught, you are also naming the evidence. That evidence is your answer to question three. The buyer who knows how they will verify success stops weighing risk and starts weighing timeline.

## Worked example

Return to the logistics company from lesson 3, because the discovery there did the hard part and now you get to see the payoff. The problem statement: customer service throughput is capped by unreliable delivery-status data that forces manual checking, not by slow reply writing. The proposed build: an agent that drafts reply templates and assembles status information it can trust, flags anything it cannot verify, and never sends a customer-facing message without a human review.

Now frame the value for the buyer, the operations director who signs the check. Do not lead with what the agent can do. Lead with the hours.

The team handles four hundred tickets a week. In discovery you learned a third of them are the same three query types, and that roughly a third of all tickets cannot be resolved without a human making a judgment call because the underlying data is unreliable. The honest baseline: the agent reliably handles the queries that never need judgment, and it assembles the trustworthy status information so the human's check is faster. That is a defensible delta. It is not "the whole queue is automated." It is a specific slice.

Put numbers on it. Say the agent clears the routine third of the queue without human involvement, recovering, on the reliable estimate from the ticket log, about a hundred tickets a week of hands-on time, roughly eight hours across the four agents. That is the time baseline. The error cost: every wrong promise to a customer is a liability, and the compliance rule requires human review of anything uncertain, so the agent's discipline of flagging instead of inventing directly reduces the rework and risk that discovery surfaced. That is the second layer, and it is the one that resonates with the buyer because it is money already at risk. The capacity layer: the four agents now spend their day on the judgment calls and the customers that actually need a human, which is better work and better service, not less work for the team.

Then the honest cost, on the same page. The platform and model calls for an unattended scheduled agent are modest, but the real cost is the review time, because the agent never sends without a human. You put that in the math. The net is still clearly positive, and the payback is a handful of months, because the saving is recurring. You show the buyer the recurring number separately from the one-time build, and you name the metric you will report at the end of the first quarter: tickets cleared by the agent, review time per ticket, and the error rate in what ships to customers. [^1]

The buyer does not need to know about cron expressions or skills or profiles to say yes. [^1] [^2] [^3] The buyer needs to see the hours, the risk, and the verification, all in numbers they can defend to their own leadership. That is the whole craft. You translated what the agent does into the operations director's language, and the decision became obvious.

## The value narrative, not the feature list

When you present, you will be tempted to open with the system. Resist it. A one-page value narrative beats a feature walkthrough every time. Its shape: the problem you both agree on, the outcome in the buyer's units, the honest cost, and the verification. That is the whole story. Every feature you are proud of earns its way in only if it makes the story stronger, and usually it does not need to be named at all.

This is the difference between selling a tool and selling a result. A tool buyer has to become an expert to justify the purchase to their boss. A result buyer walks into the next meeting with a number and a plan to check it. You want the second kind. They are easier to close, and they are the clients who renew.

## The pitfalls to avoid

**Leading with the feature list.** Nobody buys a capability. The buyer's eyes glaze over at "it runs unattended on a schedule." They lean forward at "your queue clears itself overnight." Lead with the outcome, always.

**Fabricating precision.** A fake baseline is a bomb with a timer on it. When the buyer checks your number and it is wrong, the deal and your reputation die at the same moment. Use the real numbers from discovery, or do not quote a number.

**Hiding the recurring cost.** The buyer will find the running cost eventually, and finding it without you destroys trust. Put it in the math up front. Honest cost plus a clearly positive net is a stronger pitch than a hidden cost and a suspicious buyer.

**Overclaiming the delta.** The agent does the routine slice, not the whole job. Claim only what the agent actually changes, and count the human review as a cost. Overclaiming is how a good case becomes a bad one the moment it is tested.

**Forgetting the verification.** A value case without a way to check it is a promise. Name the metric and the date. The buyer who knows how they will verify success stops negotiating.

## Try it

Take the problem statement you wrote in lesson 3 and frame the value for the buyer who signs the check:

1. Write the outcome in the buyer's units: hours, errors, or capacity, and pick the one that buyer cares about most.
2. Put the baseline number down, and note where it came from so it is checkable.
3. Name the honest delta, the slice the agent actually changes.
4. Put the recurring cost in the math, including review time.
5. Compute the payback, and separate the one-time number from the recurring one.
6. Write the verification: the metric you will report, and when.

If the one-page value narrative fits on a single page and the buyer could defend every number to their own boss, you have framed value like a consultant. If you caught yourself writing a list of features, stop, and translate each one into a so-what for the buyer.
