# Lesson 26: Contracts and Scoping: Statements of Work, Boundaries, and Avoiding Scope Creep

Lesson 25 made sure you get paid fairly for the engagement model you choose. This lesson makes sure the scope stays exactly where the price assumed it was. The two are the same battle fought in different places: pricing decides who carries the risk of being wrong, and scoping decides what, precisely, the price covers. A fixed fee is only fair if the work is defined, and a definition is only a definition if it is written down and both sides agree to it. That written agreement is the contract, and it is not bureaucracy. It is the only thing protecting you, and the client, from the slow drift that turns a clean engagement into an unbounded one.

## Why a handshake is not a contract

Most small engagements start with a friendly call. The client describes a problem, you describe what you can do, everyone nods, and the work begins. That feels warm and it feels fast, and it produces one durable thing: a shared memory of a conversation that each side heard slightly differently.

You remember the agent that monitors their support inbox. The client, six weeks later, remembers the agent that also drafts responses, and flags spam, and summarizes weekly trends, and they are not being unreasonable. Each addition felt small and implied at the time. The problem is that nothing was ever written down, so "implied" did a lot of invisible work, and when you finally push back, you are not correcting scope. You are telling a client that something they genuinely believed was part of the deal is not. Now you are the bad guy, and you have nothing to point to.

The contract exists to replace that fragile shared memory with something both sides can inspect. Its job is not legal posturing. Its job is to make the agreement explicit enough that neither side can drift from it quietly, because drift that is visible early is cheap and drift that is discovered late is a betrayal. A written scope is a kindness to the client, not a weapon against them. It tells them exactly what you will do, so they can hold you to it, and it tells them exactly what you will not do, so they know where their own responsibility for the rest begins.

## What a statement of work is for

A statement of work, often abbreviated SOW, is the document that turns a handshake into a deliverable you can verify. It is the written definition of the job. In one place it should answer five questions, and if you cannot answer all five, you have not defined the work yet.

What you will build. The deliverables, named as concrete things a client could point at and accept, not vague aspirations. An enumerated, verified function with its tests, not an improved codebase. A deployed agent that answers the intake queue, not better operations.

What you will not do. The boundary, named as explicitly as the deliverables. This is the half of the SOW that most people skip, and it is the half that determines whether the engagement stays shaped. Exclusions are not a list of insults. They are the definition of what this price assumed, and they matter exactly as much as the inclusions.

How you will know it is done. The acceptance criteria, specific enough to be checked. The same discipline from lesson 6 and lesson 16, at contract scale. If done is measurable, you both know when the work is complete. If it is not, the project never ends because neither side can name the ending.

What it will cost, and how. The price and the model, the fixed fee or the hourly rate or the retainer's included work, carried over from the engagement model you chose in lesson 25.

What happens when things change. The change process, written in advance. New work is not a fight, it is a named path: the client requests it, you scope it, you price it, and it becomes a small separate piece of agreed work with its own boundary. Writing this in the SOW is what keeps new requests from silently folding into the existing scope.

A statement of work answers five questions in one place: what you will build, what you will not do, how you know it is done, what it costs, and what happens when things change.

## The contract as an artifact you can verify

The instinct that produced lessons 14, 15, and 16 applies to the contract itself. You verify code against a live system, you verify completion against a written task contract, and you should treat the SOW the same way: as an artifact with a clear definition of done that gets checked, not a piece of prose everyone skimmed.

That is exactly the shape an agent's work takes in a system like Hermes. A worker does not start from a vague instruction and improvise for an afternoon. It receives a card that names the task contract, and the completion checkpoint that lets it claim done can only be called after the contract is verified against the actual state of the work. [^1] The tool refuses to let you declare victory on a guess. The discipline this lesson is asking you to apply to a whole engagement is the same discipline the tool applies to a single task: write the boundary down before you start, verify against it before you claim done, and route a change through an explicit path instead of absorbing it silently. [^2]

The consultant's mistake is to treat the contract as something for lawyers and therefore as not-my-job. That is wrong twice. It is wrong because you, not a lawyer, know what the work actually is, and no lawyer can define deliverables you have not named. And it is wrong because the contract is continuous with the work you already do. Scoping a statement of work is scoping a task you can carry, from lesson 16, scaled up to the whole engagement, and verifying acceptance criteria is testing and verification, from lesson 14, applied to the outcome instead of the code. You already have these skills.

## Drawing a boundary that protects both sides

A good scope boundary is specific, written in the client's vocabulary, and obviously fair. Three properties, each worth checking.

Specific beats general. Do not write an agent that handles customer support. Write an agent that classifies incoming support messages into the five existing categories, surfaces urgent ones to the on-call channel, and refuses to make account changes. The second version is testable. The first is a wish. Specificity is what lets both sides agree on done, so the SOW should borrow the exact project-specific terms the client uses, and if the client uses terms so loose they cannot be tested, pushing them to define the terms is itself a service.

Exclusions are named in the client's terms, not yours. Saying we do not handle your legacy CRM migration is clear only if the client and you both know what that phrase points at. Name the actual systems and the actual boundary in words the client uses every day, so there is no room for a later well, that was always in scope.

Fair reads as fair. A client who reads a boundary and thinks it is tiny knows they will be nickel-and-dimed to death, and a client who reads a boundary and thinks it is enormous knows they overpaid. The test of a good boundary is that a reasonable client reads it, nods, and says that is the job I asked for. That fairness is what makes the boundary stickable, because a client who feels scoped fairly will not fight every subsequent change, and a client who feels scoped unfairly will.

## Where scope creep comes from

Scope creep gets blamed on clients, and that is mostly wrong. Scope creep is a failure of the handshake, not of the client's character. It comes from named sources, and once you can see the sources you can stop most of it before it starts.

Ambiguity is the biggest source. When the work is not defined, no change is out of scope, because there is no scope to be out of. The agent that handles customer support invites the natural question of whether it also responds to customers, and nobody wrote down the answer. Almost every creep story traces back here.

The every-request-is-friendly problem. A client asks for a small addition. You say yes, because it is small, and you say yes again next week, and small yeses pile up into an unbounded project without any single decision crossing a line. Individually reasonable, collectively catastrophic. This is the exact pattern of lesson 16's decomposition warning, applied to scope instead of task size: many small undefined additions become a large undefined disaster.

The work changes the definition of the work. The first deliverable reveals something the client wants, which reveals another thing, which makes the original plan obsolete. This is not bad faith. It is learning, and it is supposed to happen. The job of the contract is not to stop the learning. It is to make the learning expensive and explicit enough that both sides re-scope deliberately instead of re-scoping silently.

The fixed-fee trap, from lesson 25, is the amplifier. Under a fixed fee, every un-priced change is free work that eats your margin, and the client has the right mindset because nothing they asked for was ever priced. Hourly at least bills the drift, though it can encourage the opposite problem of scope deliberately exploding. The retainer, with a vague scope, is uniquely vulnerable because the relationship is ongoing and expectations compound quietly, which is exactly why a retainer must name its included work. [^3]

Scope creep comes from named, visible sources: ambiguity, a stream of individually friendly small requests, the work redefining the work as it reveals new wants, and the fixed-fee trap that turns every un-priced change into free labor.

## The change process

Because some scope creep is inevitable, the contract should not pretend otherwise. It should name the path for change, and the discipline is to make changes visible at the moment they happen rather than at the moment you invoice.

A change request is a small event with four steps. The client names the change they want. You scope it: what it is, what it touches, what it leaves alone. You price it, according to the engagement model, as a separate piece of work with its own boundary. You both agree, and only then it joins the work. The point of the process is not to punish the client for changing their mind. It is to make sure every change is a deliberate, priced, agreed decision, recorded where both sides can see it, instead of a silent accumulation that both sides discover at the end.

![A four-step change process flow: the client names the change, you scope it, you price it as a separate piece of work, and only then it joins the work, with a worked example of an intake agent that grows](</diagrams/contracts-and-scoping-sow-boundaries-scope-creep/3-change-process.png>)

The change process is the same move the tool uses for a growing task, from lesson 16: a task that has grown past its window is not a reason to make a bigger window, it is a reason to decompose into separate bounded pieces. A scope that has grown past its boundary is not a reason to absorb it. It is a reason to open a new, separately scoped and priced piece of work. The boundary is load-bearing precisely because it is where you refuse to quietly expand.

## Worked example: the intake agent that grows

Walk through a concrete case, the kind that makes or breaks a consultant. A logistics company asks you to build an agent that monitors their customer support inbox round the clock and alerts the on-call team when a shipment issue is urgent.

The SOW you write names the deliverables: an agent installed and configured on the client's infrastructure, wired to their support mailbox, classifying incoming issues into the urgent and the routine, and alerting the on-call channel for urgent ones. It names the boundary just as specifically: the agent classifies and alerts, it does not draft customer replies, it does not touch billing, and it does not modify the warehouse system. Done is: a test urgent issue lands in the on-call channel within two minutes, verified against the live mailbox, and the routine queue is left untouched. The price covers exactly that.

Two weeks in, the client asks for a small addition. Can the agent also summarize the week's urgent issues every Friday and post the summary to the operations channel? It is a natural request, it is genuinely useful, and it would be the easiest yes in the world. Under the change process you do not say yes. You say: sure, here is what that looks like, it is a separate small piece of work, here is what it costs, and here is a written ten-line SOW for it alone. [^1] The summary becomes its own bounded deliverable with its own acceptance test, the Friday post is scheduled as its own recurring job, [^2] and nobody on either side was surprised. The friendly ask became a clean two-minute exchange instead of the first thread in a growing tangle, and the original engagement's boundary did not move an inch.

The client is not annoyed by this. They are relieved. They asked for something, and you gave them a clear yes with a clear boundary, which is exactly what a professional does. The only client who is annoyed by a change process is one who was hoping to get the work for free, and that is not a client you want to train. [^3]

## The two-part muse: the message and the deliverable

A consultant's discipline on a growing engagement is to hold a single two-part habit: always name the change out loud the moment it happens, and always produce a written artifact that captures the agreed scope.

The message part is the discipline that keeps a scope honest in real time. When the client proposes an addition, you do not absorb it and you do not say a flat no. You name it: that is new scope, here is what it would take, here is what it would cost, and here is how we would add it. Naming it forces the visible decision that stops silent accumulation.

The artifact part is what makes the honesty durable. The handshake becomes the contract, the SOW becomes the written boundary, and every agreed change gets appended so the written record always describes the real job. [^2] A client who can open the file and see exactly what is in scope and what is not is a client you never have to argue with, because the argument was already had, in writing, in the calm before the work.

## Try it

Take the engagement you carried through this series, the one from lesson 25 you priced, and now write its contract:

1. Write the deliverables as concrete things a client could point at and accept. No vague aspirations.
2. Write the exclusions just as specifically, in the client's own vocabulary. What is explicitly not in scope?
3. Write done as measurable acceptance criteria. How will you both know the work is finished?
4. Carry over the price and the model from lesson 25. What does the scope assume the price covers?
5. Write the change process. When the client asks for something new, what exactly happens?
6. Re-read the exclusions and check the fairness test: would a reasonable client read this and say that is the job I asked for?

If you can write a scope boundary specific enough to verify and fair enough to hold, you have turned the handshake into a contract. If you catch yourself writing the deliverables and skipping the exclusions, that is the exact gap this lesson exists to close, because the boundary you do not write is the boundary you will fight about in six weeks.
