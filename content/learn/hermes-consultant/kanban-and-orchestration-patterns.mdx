# Lesson 12: Kanban and Orchestration Patterns: Scoping Work, Dispatching, and Finishing the Job

Lesson 11 taught you to turn a proven one-off win into recurring value: a job that fires on a schedule, delivers to the right place, and tells someone loudly when it breaks. But a schedule raises a new problem the moment your engagement grows. You stop having one job and start having several, each doing a piece of a larger goal, and now you have to coordinate them. This lesson is about that coordination. It teaches you the three moves that keep a whole portfolio of agent work moving without you holding every thread: scoping work so it fits a real work window, dispatching each piece to the worker best equipped to finish it, and knowing when a job is actually done rather than merely started.

The discipline here transfers to any agent platform, because it is not really about a specific tool. It is about a shift in your role. When you have one task, you do it. When you have a portfolio, you design the system that carries the work, and then you stop doing it yourself. The consultant who makes this shift correctly stops being the bottleneck on everything and becomes the person who made the whole thing possible.

## Define the terms: scoping, dispatching, finishing

Before going further, name the three verbs, because they are the whole lesson and they are easy to blur.

**Scoping** is turning a goal into one or more defined pieces of work, each small enough that a single capable worker can finish and verify it in one sitting, and each written down with a clear boundary. Scope is the boundary being explicit, not the work being big or small. It answers, before any worker starts, what gets built, what is verified, and what is explicitly out of scope.

**Dispatching** is sending each scoped piece to the right worker at the right time. Dispatch depends on two facts that have to be true before a card moves: someone is assigned to the work, and anything the card depends on has already finished.

**Finishing** is closing the work with proof that it is done, or blocking it with a clear reason why it is not. Finishing is the decision the whole system turns on, because it is the difference between work that moved forward and work that merely occupied a worker for a while.

In Hermes these three verbs have concrete homes. A **task** is a row on the kanban board with a title, a body, one **assignee** (a profile name, not a group), and a status that moves through a defined lifecycle: triage, to do, ready, running, blocked, review, done, archived. Every row lives in a shared database that any profile or human can read and write. That shared, durable record is what makes coordination possible at all: the work is not hiding inside one agent's memory, it is visible to everyone who needs to see it.[^1]

## Why coordination deserves its own lesson

Lesson 11 made a single win reliable. This lesson makes a portfolio work, and the two skills are not the same. A single recurring job is a closed loop: you write a self-contained prompt, you schedule it, you watch it fire. Several jobs, with several workers and dependencies between them, are an open system. The risk is no longer "will this one job behave," it is "do the pieces fit together, does each worker get the right work at the right time, and does anything disappear into the gaps."

There is a reason consultants who can build one agent sometimes fall apart on an engagement with ten. Building the third and fourth agent is not twice as hard as building the first, it is a different kind of hard. The failure mode is no longer writing a working prompt. It is coordination: scope creep on a card nobody bounded, two workers because nobody picked one, a task marked done that was only started, a dependency that never resolved. Every one of those is a consulting failure, and every one of them is preventable with the three disciplines in this lesson.

There is also a client-facing argument, and it is the one that earns you the word "platform" instead of "demo." A single agent is a tool the client holds. A coordinated portfolio is a delivery system the client can hand to their team and grow. When you can demonstrate scoped, dispatched, finished work at portfolio scale, you are no longer selling the client an agent. You are handing them something their own people can be trained on, extended, and trusted. That is a fundamentally different product.

## Scope the work to fit a real work window

The first discipline is scoping, and the core rule is that a card must fit a real work window. Every worker has a context window and a run budget. It can only hold so much in front of it and it can only take so many turns before something has to give. If you create a card that is really five features stitched into one, the worker will run out of room partway through, and you will get a half-finished thing that is worse than a finished small thing because it is harder to see what is missing.

The antidote is decomposition, consciously chosen. When a goal is bigger than one worker can finish, split it into a small graph of child tasks, each scoped to be done on its own, and each linked to its dependencies. This is the same judgment lesson 3 taught you about scoping a proposal, now applied at the level of a single card. You are drawing a boundary around each piece of work, naming what it produces, and naming what it leaves alone.

There are two failure directions and both waste money. **Over-decomposition** creates a card for every micro-step, and you spend more on overhead, on moving work between cards, than the work itself is worth. **Under-decomposition** creates one card too big to finish, and the worker exhausts its budget mid-task. The right size sits between the two: large enough that the overhead of handing off is a fraction of the work, small enough that a single focused worker can finish it and prove it. You calibrate by asking, in writing, what "done" would look like for the card, and whether that is achievable in one sitting by the worker you have in mind.

The second scoping rule is that the acceptance criteria live **on the card**, not in your head and not in a shared document someone has to remember to read. The worker reads only the card, plus the attachments and the comment thread on it. If the definition of done is not written there, the worker cannot restate it, a reviewer cannot check against it, and you have no objective basis to call the work done or not.[^2] So a well-scoped card carries a block that says what must be true to ship, in verifiable terms, and a list of non-goals that says what is deliberately out of scope. The non-goals matter as much as the goals, because they are what stop scope creep and what stop a worker from "helping" by doing work you did not ask for.

There is a discipline that follows from the first rule and it is worth naming plainly. A worker that reads the card and restates the acceptance criteria in its own words before touching the work catches a bad scope at the cheapest possible moment. If the worker looks at the card and genuinely cannot say what done means, that card was scoped wrong. The fix is to improve the card, not to hope the worker guesses.

## Dispatch to the right worker

The second discipline is dispatch, and the rule is that every card has exactly one assignee who is a worker with the right skills and the right model for the job, and that a card does not start until everything it depends on has finished.

Why one assignee and not a team? Because a running task is owned by whichever worker claims it. The board is not a shared inbox where several people grab whichever piece looks interesting. A task is claimed atomically by its assigned profile, and that profile runs it to a terminal state. Ownership has to be unambiguous, or two workers duplicate work and collide on shared state. A single assignee is the boundary that makes ownership clean.[^3]

The assignee is a named profile with persistent memory, not an anonymous subagent that exists for one call and disappears. That is a meaningful consulting distinction. An anonymous subagent answers a question and is gone, taking its context with it. A named worker is a durable identity: it accumulates memory across tasks, it can be given a set of skills, it can be tuned to a model that fits its role. If you want a specialist that knows the client's systems and gets better with every task, you assign the work to that specialist, and you get continuity you cannot get from a throwaway.[^4]

Dispatch is also how dependencies resolve. You link child tasks to the parents they depend on, and the board holds the child at ready until every parent is done, then promotes it so the right worker picks it up. This is the mechanism that keeps a pipeline sequential without a human standing over it. The dependency is written once, as a link, and the system honors it. You do not have to watch and remember to start the next step; the board does it for you.

The orchestrator's own rule is the one that makes all of this work, and it is counterintuitive enough to restate. A well-behaved orchestrator does not do the work itself. The orchestrator decomposes the goal into cards, links them, assigns each to the worker best built to finish it, and steps back. The moment you catch yourself doing the work that belongs on a card, you have stopped orchestrating and started bottlenecking. This is a difficult habit for anyone who became a consultant by being good at doing the work, and it is the habit that scales.

There is one more orchestration rule that saves a whole class of failures. Before you fan work out into parallel cards, decide the shared things once, up front, and stamp the decision into every card body that depends on it. If two workers are each building half of a system, and the two halves have to round-trip, they may pick different file formats, different naming, different schemas, and the two halves will never meet. The orchestrator's job is to pick the shared convention, decide it exactly once, and write it into both cards ahead of time. The workers cannot see each other's cards, so they cannot coordinate on their own; the coordination has to arrive pre-baked in the scope.

## Finish the job: done is proven, not stated

The third discipline is finishing, and it is the one that most separates professionals, because it is the one where "good enough" habits betray you. The rule is brutal and simple: a running worker must close its task with one of two terminal calls, and it may not exit without either one. It either completes the work, delivering a structured handoff that proves what it changed and how it verified it, or it blocks, with a clear reason why the work is not done and what it needs. Exiting without closing the loop is a protocol violation, and the system treats it as a crash, not as a finish.[^5]

The reason this rule matters is that a scheduled, dispatched portfolio runs without you standing over it. Nobody is watching each worker to confirm it actually finished. The terminal call IS the record. If a worker can stop working and have the task look done without saying so, then a task that was merely started can be mistaken for a task that finished, and you discover the gap when the client does, which is the worst time to discover it. The terminal call is how the system refuses to let "started" pass for "done."

The handoff itself should answer four questions for the next reader: what changed, how it was verified, what could unblock or retry it if it fails, and what risk is deliberately left open. Those four answers are the whole closeout, and they should be machine-readable enough that a downstream worker, a reviewer, or a dashboard can act on them without scraping your prose. The discipline is that every handoff is written for the person who has to act next, who may know none of what you knew. This is the same rule lesson 11 taught for scheduled jobs, now applied to handoffs, and it is how work survives a change of worker.[^6]

Finishing also means being honest about the two ways a task can end that are not completion. A worker can block a task because it needs information, or because the work turned out to be a different problem than the card described. That is not a failure, it is a correct signal, and the honest block is worth more than a hollow completion. Blocking with a clear reason is how the portfolio surfaces a problem to a human at the moment it matters, rather than burying it in a completed task that was quietly half-done. The cheap-sounding move, complete everything and smooth it over, is the expensive one, because it ships unknowns to the client. The discipline is to differentiate sharply: complete only what is actually proven, and block loudly when something is not.

## Worked example: from one report to a coordinated fleet

Put it together on the engagement you have carried through this series. In lesson 11 you automated the claims-intake weekly summary. It fires every Friday, pulls the week's intake, flags what it could not verify, and lands in the client's team channel. That was one job, and it ran on its own. Now the client wants more. They want the anomaly flag to run every morning. They want a monthly trend review. And they want a new dashboard endpoint built that will eventually power the trend review. That is three recurring loops and one build, and if you try to run them by hand the way you ran the first one, you will drown.

So you orchestrate instead. You do not build any of it. You decompose the request into cards, you assign each, you link the dependencies, and you make the scope decisions once.

The anomaly flag is the most interesting card, so follow it all the way through. First, scope. What counts as an anomaly, precisely, so a worker with no knowledge of your conversation can produce it? This is the decision to make once, up front: an anomaly is a claim flagged as unverifiable by the intake system, or a volume spike above a threshold you set. You write that definition into the card. You write the acceptance criteria: the job runs each morning, it produces a digest of anomalies with counts, and it includes the same residual-risk note the weekly report does. You write the non-goals: it does not recommend fixes, and it does not message customers. That is the scope.

Dispatch. You assign the anomaly flag to the worker who owns the client's data service, the one from lesson 10, because that is the profile that already has the skills to read the live systems and the memory of how they behave. You link it so it only runs after the underlying data hook is in place. You do not add a second assignee and you do not watch it yourself. The dispatcher claims it, the worker runs, the board records it.

Finish. When the worker closes the card, you do not accept "done" on trust. You read the handoff, and it answers the four questions. It changed these files. It verified by running against the live intake data on the staging hook and confirmed the digest matched what you expected. It can be retried by re-running this command. It leaves open one risk: the threshold is provisional and worth revisiting after a month of data. Every word of that is proof the work is real, and you have it because the card asked for it and the worker delivered it.

The monthly trend review and the dashboard build need the same treatment, with one extra move. The trend review and the dashboard endpoint share a decision: what the trend data shape looks like. If the worker building the endpoint and the worker building the review each invent their own shape, the two halves will not fit. So you decide the shape once, now, and you stamp it into both card bodies before anyone starts. That is the pre-baked coordination that keeps parallel work from diverging.

The build gets its own chain: a scoped build card, then a QA card linked as its child so it only starts when the build finishes. You do not hold the chain. The board promotes the QA card the moment the build closes, the QA worker checks it against the acceptance criteria on the card, and it finishes. At the end you have three recurring jobs and one verified build, coordinated without you running any of them. That is the difference between a consultant who built the client three things and a consultant who handed the client a system that grows.

## The pitfalls to avoid

**Doing the work yourself.** This is the orchestrator's most seductive failure. You are good at the work, and it is faster to just do it than to write a card. But the moment you do the work that belongs on a card, you have returned to being the bottleneck on everything. Decompose, assign, and step back.

**Writing cards that assume your memory.** A card is read by a worker who has never met the client. "Handle the anomaly" means nothing to it. The workload and the standards from this lesson are identical to lesson 11's self-contained prompt rule: a card a stranger can execute to a consistent result is the one you can dispatch and trust.

**Over-decomposing and under-decomposing.** Too many tiny cards and you burn more on overhead than the work is worth. One card too big to finish and the worker exhausts its budget mid-task. Calibrate so each card is done, and provable, in one sitting by the worker you assign.

**Leaving shared decisions to the workers.** Two workers building halves of the same system cannot see each other's cards. If you do not pick the shared convention once and write it into both, the halves will not fit. Decide before you fan out, and stamp the decision into every card that depends on it.

**Accepting started for done.** A worker that stops working without closing the task has not finished. Insist on the terminal call, insist on the handoff that answers the four questions, and differentiate sharply between work that is proven and work that is merely in progress.

**Claiming done without evidence.** Lesson 8 and 9 drilled this into you at the level of a single tool. It holds at the level of a portfolio. A handoff that says what changed but not how it was verified is not a finish. The verification is the proof the card earned the word done.

## Try it

Take the schedule you built in lesson 11 and expand it, on paper, to a portfolio of three recurring jobs and one build, the way the worked example did:

1. Scope. For each of the three jobs and the build, write one card: what it produces, its acceptance criteria in verifiable terms, and its non-goals.
2. Fit. Ask of every card: can one focused worker finish and prove this in one sitting? If not, decompose it into a small graph of child tasks.
3. Dispatch. Name the assignee for each card, and justify it: why is this profile's skills and memory the right match?
4. Link. Write the dependencies. Which cards cannot start until which others finish?
5. Decide once. What shared convention do two or more cards depend on, and have you written the decision into all of them before anyone starts?
6. Finish. For each card, write the handoff evidence you would require: what changed, how it was verified, what would unblock or retry it, and what risk you would accept leaving open.

If you can answer all six, you can move a portfolio of work without holding every thread. If your instinct is to build the first thing you understand and call the rest coordination overhead, that is the builder reflex again. Stop, and scope, dispatch, and define done first.

You can now scope a portfolio into cards that fit a work window, dispatch each card to the worker best built to finish it, and define done so a job is proven rather than merely started. But a coordinated system that touches a client's live systems raises a question none of the threads you have built so far answer directly: how do you keep that system safe?
