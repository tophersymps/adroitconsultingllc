# Lesson 5: Stakeholder Mapping: Who Has to Say Yes, and Who Has to Live With It

Lesson 4 taught you how to turn what an agent does into a number a buyer can defend. That gets you the meeting. This lesson is about what happens after you have a sponsor who wants the value: the far less glamorous work of mapping every person who can stop the project, and every person who has to live with the result. A consultant who can frame value but cannot navigate stakeholders will watch deal after deal die in an approval meeting they never saw coming. This lesson teaches you how to map the full set of people around an agent implementation, what each one actually cares about, and how to design a rollout that the approvers can say yes to and the users can actually live with.

## The two questions that define stakeholder mapping

There is a short way to say what stakeholder mapping is, and it comes down to two questions you ask about every person involved in an implementation.

**Who has to say yes?** These are the approvers. The people whose sign-off the deployment literally cannot ship without. They might be the budget owner, the security officer, the compliance lead, the IT director, the legal counsel, or the manager who owns the process the agent touches. They do not necessarily use the agent. What they hold is the power to block it, and that power is absolute. If any one of them says no hard enough, the project stops.

**Who has to live with it?** These are the people the agent runs for and among. The operator who works beside it, the customer who receives its output, the support person who cleans up its mistakes, the coworker whose workflow it changes. They do not necessarily sign anything. What they hold is the power to make the deployment succeed or fail quietly, day after day, in ways that never show up in a steering committee.

Here is the trap that kills agent projects. The two groups are rarely the same people, and a consultant who treats them as one audience designs for the wrong person. If you design for the approver and ignore the user, you ship a tool that is safe, approved, and unused. If you design for the user and ignore the approver, you build something delightful that never gets past the gate. Stakeholder mapping is the discipline of keeping both groups in view from the first day, because the moment you start building for one and forgetting the other, the project has already picked a side, and the other side will eventually win.

## The four stakeholder groups in an agent implementation

Most agent implementations, whatever the organization, resolve into four recurring stakeholder groups. They are not the only stakeholders, but they are the four you can count on showing up, and if you have not identified who plays each role in your specific client, you are not done mapping.

**Users are who the agent is for.** They are the people who actually interact with the tool day to day, or whose work the tool changes even if they never open it. Their concern is practical and immediate: does this make my day better, is it fast enough, is it predictable, does it make me look good or look replaceable. The user does not care about your architecture. They care about whether the thing works when they need it and does not humiliate them in front of a customer. For an agent specifically, users care about trust. An agent that is usually right but unpredictably wrong is harder to live with than one that is transparent about its limits, because nobody can plan around a failure they cannot see coming.

**Owners are who the value belongs to.** They are the sponsor who wants the outcome, the executive whose budget it comes out of, the department head whose pain it solves. Their concern is the number you built in lesson 4: does this deliver the value we agreed on, on the timeline we agreed, without blowing the budget. The owner is usually the person who brought you in, which makes them your natural ally. That is exactly why you cannot stop at the owner. The owner wants it to happen, but the owner is rarely the one who can approve it, and never the only one who has to live with it.

**IT is who the deployment runs through.** They own the infrastructure, the credentials, the servers, the deployment pipeline, the network, the monitoring. Their concern is operational: can we run this safely, does it talk to our systems correctly, does it create a maintenance burden, what happens when it breaks at 2am, who is on call. IT is where the phrase "it works on my machine" dies. You can have an owner who wants it and users who love it, and IT can still stop it because the agent needs a credential class that violates their access model, or because they have no way to update it without a manual intervention. [^1]

**Compliance is who governs the rules.** They are security, legal, data protection, and the people who own the policies the organization has to follow. Their concern is not whether the agent works but whether it is allowed to exist in this form: what data it touches, where that data goes, who can see its outputs, how it handles personal information, what a human reviews. For an agent that uses a large language model, compliance carries a heavier load than for almost any other tool, because the core mechanism is unpredictable. An agent does not run the same deterministic code path twice. That makes compliance want guardrails, and their guardrails are not suggestions. They are conditions of approval.

Here is the pattern across all four. The user and the owner care about whether the agent is worth it. IT and compliance care about whether it is safe. You need both answers to be yes before anything ships. A deployment that only passes the worth test will be blocked by the safety crowd. A deployment that only passes the safety test will be abandoned by the users. Stakeholder mapping exists to make you design for both tests at once instead of discovering the second test halfway through the build.

## Draw the map before you design

The practical move is to write the map down before you design anything. Take a page, put the four groups on it, and for each one fill in three things: who plays that role in this client, what they need to be able to say yes to, and what happens if you do not win them.

The map will not be symmetrical. Some groups have one clear person. Others have several, and they disagree with each other. In a larger client, IT security and the IT operations team might have different rules, and compliance might have a separate data protection officer on top of the general counsel. Each one is a node on your map, not a blur in a category. The point of writing it down is that you cannot satisfy a stakeholder you have not named, and you cannot name one from memory when the approval meeting is three weeks out and you are under pressure.

The output is not a theory. It is a working document you update as the engagement moves, because people change roles, requirements change, and a stakeholder who was a rubber stamp in month one can become a blocker in month three. The consultant who keeps the map current is never surprised in a steering committee. The one who sketches it once and files it is.

## Who has to say yes, and what they approve

Let me be specific about the approval side, because this is where consultants lose more deals than anywhere else. Every approver signs off on something different, and you need to know what it is for each one.

**The owner approves the value.** They sign the budget and the timeline. What you owe them is the honest case from lesson 4, and then delivery against it. The owner is usually the easiest yes, because they already want the outcome.

**IT approves the operations.** They sign that the agent can be run, maintained, and updated inside their environment without becoming a permanent headache. What you owe IT is a deployment story: where it runs, how it gets updated, how it is monitored, what it touches, and what happens on failure. [^1] IT is frequently the approver who feels least visible to a consultant who spent all their energy on the owner, and the resentment is fair. You asked them to own the thing without telling them what owning it costs.

**Compliance approves the risk.** They sign that the deployment stays inside policy. What you owe compliance is the data story: what the agent reads, what it writes, where its requests go, how outputs are reviewed, and what guardrails stop it from crossing a line. This is the approval that an LLM-powered agent makes genuinely harder than a traditional tool, and it is the one where a consultant's honesty is tested. If you have to hide how the agent works to get it approved, you have built the wrong thing, and you will pay for it in the first incident. [^2]

Here is the practical consequence. Each approver needs a different artifact, and none of them is the demo. The owner wants the value case. IT wants the operations handoff. Compliance wants the data and guardrail description. If you walk into the approval process with only a demo and a price, you have not prepared for the meeting. You have prepared for one person in a meeting that is really about three different yeses, and the two yeses you did not prepare for are the ones most likely to kill you.

## Who has to live with it, and what they need

The approval side gets the meetings. The user side gets the day. And the user side is where agent implementations actually succeed or fail, which is why the map has to treat them as seriously as the approvers.

**Users need predictability.** An agent that is 95% correct and randomly wrong on the other 5% is a trap for the person who relies on it, because they cannot tell which output to double-check. The design answer is not to promise perfection you cannot deliver. It is to make the agent honest about confidence, so the user knows when to verify, and to give the user a fast path to correct or override the agent when it misses. A user who can see the agent's reasoning, catch a mistake, and fix it in seconds will trust the tool. A user who gets a wrong answer with no explanation and no recourse will quietly route around the whole deployment.

**Users need control, or the illusion of it.** People do not mind a tool that does part of their job. They mind a tool that does part of their job and leaves them holding the consequences with no say in the decisions. Design for a human in the loop at the moments that matter, and the user becomes an ally instead of a saboteur. [^2]

**Users need a reason, not a mandate.** This is the one consultants routinely get wrong. An owner can mandate adoption, but a mandate does not produce trust, and trust is what a tool that produces text needs. The user who was told to use the agent will use it just enough to prove it does not work. The user who was shown why it helps, and who had a hand in shaping how it behaves, will make it work. Part of stakeholder mapping is recognizing that winning the user is an ongoing act of demonstration and listening, not a one-time approval.

The same logic applies to the people downstream of the agent, who never touch it at all. The customer who receives an agent-drafted email is a stakeholder even though they will never sign anything and may not know the agent exists. Their experience is decided by your guardrails: whether the agent is allowed to make a promise it cannot keep, whether a human reviews what goes out, whether the tone sounds like a person. If you forget the end user who never appears in a meeting, the agent can still fail, in the quiet, expensive way of a tool that makes the client's own customers unhappy.

## The two-sided design principle

Stakeholder mapping leads to a single design principle that you can apply to any agent you build. The approvers and the users are not competing priorities to balance. They are two sets of constraints that a good design satisfies at once, and the design is only done when it passes both.

Here is what that looks like in practice. The user wants speed and usefulness, so the agent should do as much as it safely can without asking. Compliance wants a human to review anything that matters, so the agent should stop at the line where the risk starts. These two pull in opposite directions, and the resolution is not a compromise that makes neither happy. It is a design where the agent does the safe, high-volume part automatically, and flags the boundary cases for a human, and the human approval is fast and easy enough that the user does not resent it. [^2]

That is the shape of a stakeholder-mapped agent. The users get a tool that saves them real effort. The approvers get a tool that respects every boundary they are accountable for. And the consultant gets a deployment that clears the gate and survives contact with the real work, which is the only definition of success that matters.

## Worked example: a real agent rollout

Put the map on a concrete case. A mid-sized financial services firm brings you in to deploy Hermes Agent as an assistant for their client operations team. The sponsor, a director, wants the team to handle more client requests without growing headcount. That is your owner, and the value case is straightforward. The work is the mapping.

**Users.** The operations team of six people who answer client queries. Their real concern is that the assistant not hand a client a wrong answer that they then have to defend. You learn this in the discovery interview from lesson 3, and it shapes the design. The agent can draft responses and assemble information from trusted sources, but it has to be able to flag anything it cannot verify, and the team needs a fast way to review and send. Because Hermes Agent can be configured with profiles, the assistant can be set up so that its drafting work and its access boundaries are separated cleanly, and the team interacts with it through a gateway they already understand instead of a new tool to learn. [^3][^4]

**Owner.** The director wants the volume number and the timeline. You owe them the honest value case and delivery against it. Easy yes, but only if you keep them in the loop and do not let the approvals run long enough to drain their patience.

**IT.** They own the machine the agent runs on, the credentials, and the network. Their concern is operational. The assistant needs access to client records, which means it touches sensitive data, and IT has to know how that access is scoped, how the agent is updated, and what happens when it fails. You design the deployment to run inside their environment with the access limited to what the agent actually needs, rather than asking for broad permissions that IT will never grant. You write down the operations story before you meet them, not after they ask. [^1]

**Compliance.** This is the hard one, because the client is in financial services and the data is personal client information. Compliance needs to know what the agent reads, where its model requests go, how it handles personal data, and what a human reviews before anything reaches a client. Because the assistant is an LLM, compliance also needs the guardrail story: the agent cannot promise something the firm cannot honor, its uncertain outputs get flagged for human review, and nothing client-facing ships without a person signing off. You bring compliance the data and guardrail description as a first-class artifact, because in this client they are the approver who can stop everything. [^2]

Now run the two questions against every person on the map. Who has to say yes? The director on value, IT on operations, compliance on risk. Who has to live with it? The six-person operations team on a daily basis, and every client who receives an agent-assisted reply. The design only ships if it satisfies all of them at once, and the map makes you see the whole set before you build a single prompt. A consultant who talked only to the director would build a tool the team refuses to trust and compliance refuses to let out the door, and would discover both failures after the cost was already sunk.

## The pitfalls to avoid

**Stopping at the sponsor.** The owner is your ally, and it is tempting to treat their enthusiasm as the whole approval. It is not. The sponsor can say yes and the deployment still dies in IT or compliance. Treat the sponsor as one node on the map, never as the map.

**Designing for the approver and forgetting the user.** A tool that is perfectly compliant and unused has failed. Approval is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one. The tool has to survive the day-to-day, and only the users can tell you whether it does.

**Designing for the user and forgetting the approver.** The inverse failure. A tool that is delightful and unapproved has also failed, because it never ships. You need both.

**Discovering the second yes late.** The worst failure mode, because it is the most expensive. You build the whole thing to the owner's requirements, then present it to compliance, and compliance tells you it cannot touch client data that way. The cost of redesigning after the build is an order of magnitude worse than designing for the constraint up front. The map is how you surface the second yes in week one instead of week eight.

**Treating stakeholders as a static list.** People change roles. Requirements change. The compliance officer who approved month one can be replaced by someone stricter in month three. Keep the map live, and update it every time the engagement changes.

## Try it

Pick the client you have been using through this series, or an imagined one, and draw the stakeholder map in writing:

1. List everyone who has to say yes, and name exactly what each one approves.
2. List everyone who has to live with it, including the people downstream who never appear in a meeting.
3. For each approver, write the artifact they need to say yes: value case, operations handoff, or data and guardrail description.
4. For each user, write the one thing they most need from the agent to trust it.
5. Check your design against both sides: does it satisfy every approver, and does it give every user a reason to keep using it?
6. Name the stakeholder who is most likely to surprise you late, and write what you would learn about them in week one.

If your design passes every row on both sides of the map, you have mapped like a consultant. If you caught yourself writing only about the sponsor and the feature set, you have skipped the map, and you are one approval meeting away from learning that the hard way.
