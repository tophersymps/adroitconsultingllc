# Lesson 1: What an Agent-Implementation Consultant Actually Does

Every few years a new technology creates a role that did not exist before. In the mid-2010s it was the Salesforce admin, the person who turned a CRM into something a company could actually run on. Right now it is the agent-implementation consultant: the person a company brings in to turn a general-purpose AI agent into a working piece of their operations. This whole series teaches that job, and it uses Hermes Agent as the concrete tool you will be implementing for clients. This first lesson is the map: what the job is, what you hand over, and where it differs from being a builder.

## First, define the thing being implemented

Before you can implement an agent, you need a precise idea of what an agent is. The term gets thrown around, so pin it down now. Hermes Agent, the tool this course is built on, describes itself as "the self-improving AI agent built by Nous Research," and it is careful to say what that is not: it is not a coding copilot tethered to an IDE, and it is not a chatbot wrapper around a single API. [^1] It is an autonomous agent. That distinction is the whole job.

An agent, in the sense this course uses the word, is a program that takes a goal, breaks it into steps, calls tools to do real work (searching, reading files, running commands, editing code), and keeps going until the goal is done, with a human able to watch and approve along the way. Hermes Agent makes this concrete with features that turn "an assistant" into "an employee you can hand work to": scheduled automations via built-in cron, a messaging gateway that reaches twenty-plus platforms from one process, isolated subagents it can spawn for parallel workstreams, a persistent memory that grows across sessions, and a skills system it creates and reuses. [^2] You do not need to memorize that list yet. You need to absorb the shape of it: an agent-implementation consultant sells outcomes, and the agent is the machine that produces them.

## The consultant vs the builder

Here is the core distinction, and if you only take one thing from this lesson, take this. A **builder** makes agents for themselves or their own product. They pick the stack, they decide what counts as done, they own the tooling, and they are the only user who has to be satisfied. A **consultant** implements agents for a client who is not them. That changes everything about how the work is done.

The difference shows up in four concrete places.

**The consultant works to a contract, not to taste.** The builder can redesign whenever they feel like it. The consultant agreed, on paper, to a scope, a timeline, and a definition of done, and the client is going to hold them to it.

**The consultant hands over ownership.** The builder keeps maintaining their agent forever. The consultant builds it, documents it, trains the client's people, and leaves. If the client cannot run it after you go, you did not deliver.

**The consultant inherits an environment they did not choose.** The builder controls their whole setup. The consultant lands in the client's world: their security rules, their existing tools, their network, their risk tolerance, their budget. Hermes Agent is designed for exactly this, because it "runs anywhere, not just your laptop," with six terminal backends from local to Docker to SSH to serverless, and it can be reached from the platforms the client already uses. [^3] That flexibility is not a nice-to-have for a consultant. It is the reason the job is possible.

**The consultant is accountable for adoption, not just function.** A builder's agent working once is a win. A consultant's agent working once is a start. The deliverable is a team actually using the thing, which means training, documentation, and change management are part of the job, not an afterthought.

If you come from a building background, this is the hard transition. The skills that made you good at building (curiosity, willingness to tinker, tolerance for "let me just try it") are the same skills that will get you into trouble as a consultant if you do not pair them with discipline.

## The engagement lifecycle

Every agent-implementation engagement runs through the same four phases. You will live in this loop for the rest of the course, so get the skeleton now.

**1. Discovery and scoping.** You find out what the client actually needs and whether an agent is the right answer. Half the job here is saying no: an agent is wrong for a task that needs human judgment every step, or that happens once a year, or that the client's security policy forbids automating. You also size the work so you can price it honestly. The deliverable is a scoped proposal, not a codebase.

**2. Design and setup.** You decide what the agent should do, what tools it needs, what its guardrails are, and how it fits the client's existing systems. With Hermes Agent this is where you make choices like which model provider to use, which platform the client talks to it through, what skills to install, and what the approval and security posture should be. Setup is fast, and that is a feature: "install in 60 seconds" is literally on the tool's homepage. The time goes into the design decisions, not the plumbing.

**3. Build and validate.** You implement the agent against the agreed scope and prove it does what the contract says. This means running it on real tasks, checking that outputs are correct, that it does not invent facts, and that its failures are safe and visible. Hermes Agent's security model matters here: command approval, authorization, and container isolation are part of the platform, which gives you an honest story to tell a nervous client. [^4]

**4. Handoff and support.** You document what you built, train the client's people, and set up whatever ongoing cadence the contract covers. With an agent platform this includes things like making sure the client's team knows how to review the agent's work, how to adjust its instructions, and how to keep it updated. Then you leave.

## Deliverables, concretely

A consultant hands over more than an agent. For an agent-implementation engagement, plan on these as the standard deliverables:

- **A written scope and definition of done**, agreed before any building starts.
- **The configured agent itself**, running in the client's environment against their data and their rules.
- **Documentation** the client's team can follow without you: how it works, what it can and cannot do, how to change it.
- **Training** for the people who will use and maintain it.
- **A validation record**, proof that the agent did what the scope promised, ideally with examples and edge cases tested.

Notice what is not on that list: clever architecture no one else can read, or a demo that works once on your laptop. Those are builder artifacts. They do not survive contact with a real client.

## Worked example

Put the whole frame together on one small case. A mid-sized agency wants to stop its account managers from manually drafting weekly status updates. They already use Slack. They have a Notion where project updates live. They do not have an IT team and they are nervous about "letting an AI loose."

Here is how the consultant's mindset changes what happens.

**A builder** might immediately stand up an agent, wire it to Notion, and celebrate. The agency would be no better off, because nobody asked the hard questions and nobody trained anyone.

**A consultant** starts in discovery. They learn that the account managers trust the numbers in the status updates, so the agent's output has to come from real data, not a confident guess. They learn the clients are sensitive about which project details go into a machine. They learn the team wants the drafts to land in Slack where they already work.

So the consultant scopes it: a Hermes Agent that reads approved project notes, drafts a status summary, flags anything it is unsure about instead of inventing it, and posts a draft into a Slack channel for an account manager to approve before anything ships. The approval step is not a compromise. It is the feature that makes the client willing to run it. Hermes Agent fits here precisely because it reaches Slack from one gateway, can be configured with security and approval in mind, and can be told to produce output that a human reviews. [^5] The agent does the boring drafting. The human keeps the judgment and the client relationship.

Then the consultant documents it, walks the account managers through approving and editing the drafts, and hands the keys over. That is the whole engagement: scoped, built, validated, handed off. That is what the job looks like.

## The pitfall to avoid

The trap this lesson exists to warn you about is **building before scoping**. It feels productive and it is how most builders naturally operate. But in a consulting engagement it is how you lose money and trust. You build the wrong thing, or you build the right thing with no agreement on what "right" means, and the client is under no obligation to pay for your guess.

A second, subtler trap: **confusing "the agent can do it" with "the client should run it."** A general-purpose agent like Hermes Agent can do a great deal, and "can" is not the bar. The bar is whether the specific task fits the client's risk, budget, security, and adoption reality. Your value as a consultant is partly that you say no, and that you decide what not to automate.

## Try it

Take a task you do repeatedly in your own work, something with a clear input and output. Answer these four questions in writing:

1. What is the exact input, and where does it come from?
2. What is the output, and who needs to check it before it counts?
3. What could go wrong, and which of those failures would be unacceptable?
4. If you handed this to an agent that reached you over Slack, what would the approval step look like?

That last question is the seed of your first engagement. When you have written the four answers, you have effectively done phase one (discovery and scoping) on yourself. That is the discipline this whole series is training.
