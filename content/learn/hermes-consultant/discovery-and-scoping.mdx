# Lesson 3: Discovery and Scoping: Listening Before Proposing

Lesson 2 gave you the lifecycle and told you that discovery is where the whole engagement is won or lost. This lesson does the actual craft. It teaches you how to listen, what to listen for, which questions pull the real problem out of the noise, and how to write a problem statement the client agrees with before you propose anything. Discovery is not a warm-up before the real work. It is the phase where you earn the right to propose a solution at all, and it is the phase where most engagements quietly fail. When you finish this lesson you will be able to walk into any client and run discovery so that you understand the problem better than the client does, and so that they trust you enough to let you.

## Why discovery is not a formality

Here is the uncomfortable truth that defines the consultant's version of this job. The client almost never describes their real problem on the first pass. They describe a symptom, or a solution they have already half-decided on, or a version of the problem that flatters their own theory about it. Your job in discovery is to get underneath what they say to what is actually going on, and to do it politely enough that they keep talking.

The cost of skipping this is not just a wasted build. It is worse. You can build a technically perfect agent for the problem the client described and still fail, because the problem they described was never the problem they had. When that happens, nobody is wrong on purpose. The client described what they could see, and you built to their description instead of their situation. Discovery exists to close that gap, and the consultant who runs it well has done more than gather information. They have built the trust that the rest of the engagement runs on.

There is a second reason discovery matters, and it is financial. Discovery is how you size the work, and sizing the work is how you price it honestly. If you skip discovery and quote blind, you either underprice and eat the overrun, or you overprice and lose the deal. Discovery is the phase that makes your estimate an estimate instead of a guess.

## Who you talk to and what you watch

Discovery is not one interview. It is a small field program. You are trying to learn four things: the problem, the people, the numbers, and the constraints. Each one comes from a different source.

**The problem comes from the people who do the work, not the people who pay for it.** The executive who signs the contract has a view, and you should hear it, but the person who actually does the repetitive task every day is the one who knows where it breaks. Talk to the operator. Watch them do the work. Ask what they hate about it and what they would automate if they could. They will usually tell you more truth in ten minutes than the sponsor did in an hour, because they are not selling you anything.

**The numbers come from the data, not from memory.** Nobody remembers volume accurately. If someone tells you they handle "a ton" of something, find out what "a ton" actually is. Look at the logs, the queue, the spreadsheet, the ticket system. Get a real count. The volume, the frequency, and the error rate are the raw material your problem statement and your pricing both depend on.

**The constraints come from the people who say no.** Every engagement has at least one stakeholder whose approval is required for anything to ship. Usually it is security, compliance, or legal. Find that person early and learn their rules before you design anything. The constraints they carry are not obstacles to your design. They are the design's boundary conditions, and discovering them early is the difference between a design that gets approved and a design that gets rejected after you built it.

So discovery means three or four conversations, at minimum, with different people, plus a look at the real numbers. If you find yourself proposing a solution after a single meeting with one sponsor, you have not done discovery. You have done a sales call.

## The questions that surface the real problem

The questions you ask matter more than the order you ask them, but a few stand out as the ones that break engagements open. These are the questions that separate discovery from a pleasant chat.

**"What does done look like?"** Ask this early. The client's answer tells you what they actually want, and it is often not what you assumed. "Done" might mean the work stops, or it might mean the work gets faster, or it might mean the errors stop. Those are three different problems with three different solutions, and only one of them may need an agent at all.

**"What happens today when this goes wrong?"** This question surfaces the failure modes and the stakes. It tells you what the client fears, and the answer shapes your guardrails. If a wrong output today means a compliance violation, the agent's design has to include a human approval point that a good demo would have skipped.

**"What are you not trying to do?"** The non-goal is as important as the goal. Maybe the client does not want to cut headcount, they want to free people for better work. Maybe they do not want to replace a system, they want to stop manual data entry into it. Naming what is out of scope in discovery saves you from building the wrong thing and saves the client from paying for it.

**"Who has to approve this, and who has to live with it?"** The approver and the end user are often not the same person. The end user is who the agent must be pleasant to work with. The approver is who it must be safe enough to get past. Discovery fails when a consultant designs for the approver and ignores the user, or delights the user and ignores the approver.

**"What is this costing you today?"** Ask this even when it feels awkward. The answer is your honest pricing basis. If the client cannot or will not put a number on the pain, you cannot price the cure, and you cannot later show value. The number does not have to be precise. It just has to exist.

**"Why do you think an agent is the answer?"** This is the question that exposes the solution-in-search-of-a-problem. Sometimes the client has a real problem and correctly senses an agent could help. Sometimes they read a headline and want an AI because it is shiny. Asking this directly forces the honest answer, and it gives you the opening to redirect toward the actual problem, or to say no.

## Define the problem before you propose the solution

Discovery converges on a single deliverable: a written problem statement that the client agrees with. The test of a good problem statement is that it is one sentence, it names the affected party, it names the pain in concrete terms, and the client reads it and nods. If the client hesitates, you are not done discovering.

Here is the discipline that makes this hard. You are a technical person, and your instinct will be to reach for a solution the moment you understand the problem. Resist that. In discovery, you hold the solution back. You write the problem, you get the client to agree to it, and only then do you move to the question of whether an agent is the right answer at all.

That last step is the one that earns you the word "consultant." An agent is the right answer for a task that is high volume, low judgment, repetitive, and that can be governed with clear rules and human approval points. An agent is the wrong answer for a task that needs human judgment every step, or that happens once a year, or that the client's security policy forbids automating, or that is so broken the real fix is a process change, not a tool. [^1] Saying no to the wrong work, and saying it early and clearly, is not losing a deal. It is protecting your reputation and the client's money. The client who hears a good no will come back to you for the work that is right.

## Worked example

Put discovery to work on a case where the client's first description is not the real problem.

A logistics company reaches out. Their request, as the sponsor states it in the first call, is crisp: they want an agent to write customer service emails. They have a team of four people answering customer queries about deliveries, and they want an AI to draft the replies so the team can handle more volume. That is the request. That is what they think they are buying.

The consultant does not start standing up an agent. They start discovery.

First conversation, the sponsor. Done looks like: the four-person team clears more tickets per day with the same headcount, and the replies keep the company's tone. Not trying to do: cut the team. The number the sponsor offers for cost is vague, "they are always behind," so the consultant flags that the real figure has to come from the data.

Second conversation, the operator. The consultant sits with one of the four agents who answer queries. What they learn changes everything. The queries are not the problem. The team can type fast and the replies are mostly boilerplate. The real bottleneck is that every ticket has to be checked against the delivery system, and the delivery system's tracking status is frequently wrong or missing. So half the agent's day is not writing, it is hunting for information that the company's own systems do not reliably have. The thing that actually eats the time, and the thing that makes customers angry, is not reply writing. It is the bad data underneath it.

Third conversation, the compliance and IT person. The rules: any outbound customer communication has to be reviewed by a human, because a wrong promise to a customer is a real liability. And the delivery system is an old third-party API that nobody fully owns.

Fourth, the numbers. The consultant pulls the ticket log. Four hundred tickets a week. About a third are the same three query types. But the error rate in the tracking data that the team depends on is high enough that a meaningful share of tickets cannot be resolved without a human making a judgment call about what to tell the customer.

Now the problem statement writes itself, and it is not what the client asked for. One sentence: the customer service team's throughput is capped by unreliable delivery-status data that forces manual checking, not by slow reply writing. The client reads it and nods. That moment, the nod, is the exit test of discovery, and the nod only comes because the consultant talked to the operator, looked at the numbers, and found the real problem under the stated one.

Then the scope question, honestly. Is an agent the answer? Partly. An agent can draft the reply templates and assemble the status information it can trust. But the constraint that shapes everything is that the tracking data is unreliable, so the agent has to be built to flag anything it cannot verify instead of inventing it, and every customer-facing message still needs a human review. [^2] That is not a compromise. It is the design that the discovery surfaced, and it is exactly why discovery mattered. A consultant who built reply-writing agents to the client's original request would have automated the part that was not the bottleneck and left the real cost untouched.

The scoped proposal reflects all of this: the problem statement, the boundary that the agent never sends without review, the three cases it will be tested on, and an estimate sized to the work the data revealed, not to the work the first call described. The client buys it, because it matches the reality they now recognize. That is discovery. You understood their problem better than they did, you wrote it down so they agreed with it, and you earned the right to propose the actual solution.

## Scoping: turning discovery into a proposal

Discovery hands off to scoping, which is the act of turning what you learned into a defined piece of work you can price. Scoping answers three questions in writing.

**What exactly will be built?** A precise boundary. The agent does these inputs, produces these outputs, and explicitly does not do the other things. The boundary is as important as the capability, because it is what protects you from scope creep and what lets the client know what they are buying.

**What will you test to prove it works?** The acceptance cases. You name, up front, the three or so real situations the agent will be validated against, including at least one failure case. This is the seed of the validation record you will need in the implement phase, and naming it in the proposal means you and the client agree on what counts as done before you build.

**What does it cost, and why?** Your estimate, tied to the scope you just wrote. It is honest only if it came from the real numbers you gathered in discovery. If you priced from the client's first description instead of the data, your estimate is a guess and you will be the one who eats it.

The deliverable of scoping is a written proposal, not a codebase. If you have written a problem statement the client agrees with, a boundary they accept, acceptance cases they signed off on, and an honest price, you have done your job. The agent does not get built until the client says yes to all four. That is the discipline that makes every later phase easier, because from this point forward you are executing an agreement instead of running on a guess.

## The pitfalls to avoid

**Solving the symptom.** The client said "write faster emails," and the real problem was bad data. The consultant who builds to the symptom delivers a tool that does not move the metric, and the client quietly concludes the whole exercise was a waste. Go for the root cause.

**Designing before the problem statement is agreed.** Your design is only worth what the agreed problem is worth. If you skip the written, agreed problem statement and move straight to proposing, you are back to building on a guess. The problem statement is the foundation. Do not build on sand.

**Talking more than listening.** You already know what you think a good agent looks like. The client does not need to hear your opinion yet. They need to feel understood. Discovery is a listening exercise, and the consultant who fills the room with their own expertise learns nothing.

**Falling for the single-sponsor trap.** One enthusiastic sponsor is not discovery. If you have not talked to the operator, seen the numbers, and found the person who can say no, you are missing information that will hurt you later. Discovery is incomplete until the no-sayer has told you their rules.

**Never making the honest no.** If the work is wrong for an agent, say so. Taking it anyway to book the fee costs you more in reputation than the fee is worth, and it fails the client. The honest no is a consulting asset, not a lost sale.

## Try it

Pick a real or imagined task a colleague or a client does repeatedly. Run discovery on it with these six steps, in writing:

1. Name who you talked to: the sponsor, the operator, and the person who can say no.
2. Write the numbers: real volume, frequency, error rate, not the remembered version.
3. Answer "What does done look like?" in one sentence.
4. Answer "What are you not trying to do?" in one sentence.
5. Write the problem statement in one sentence, and ask whether the client would nod at it.
6. Make the honest call: is an agent the right answer, or is the real fix something else?

If you can write all six before you reach for any tool, you are running discovery like a consultant. If the first thing you want to do is open the agent and try something, that is the builder reflex talking. Stop, and go back to step one.
