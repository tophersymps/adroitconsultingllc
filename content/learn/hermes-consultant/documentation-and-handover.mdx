# Lesson 18: Documentation and Handover: Making the Client Self-Sufficient After You Leave

Lesson 17 taught you to package what you build so you can reuse it. This lesson is the other half of leaving a client better off than you found them, and it is where engagements either pay off or come undone. You can deliver a flawless system, verify every screen, prove the weekly report lands on time, and still fail the engagement if nobody on the client's side can do a single thing with it after you are gone. A handover that leaves the client calling you for every change is not a handover. It is a dependency wearing a completion certificate.

## Define the terms: what a handover actually is

A handover is the transfer of ownership and operating knowledge of the delivered work from you to the client, so their team can run it, maintain it, and change it without you. The word that carries the meaning is ownership, not knowledge. Handover is the moment the system stops being yours and starts being theirs, and that shift has to happen in the client's heads as much as in your documents.

Documentation is the written knowledge that makes the transfer stick. It is rarely a single document. It is a small set, and the three jobs those documents do are the backbone of this lesson, because most handovers do one job well and skip the other two. More on that in a moment.

Self-sufficiency is the test. A client is self-sufficient when their team can keep the system running and make routine changes using the documentation and their own judgment, without needing to ask you a question. It is a measurable property, not a feeling. You will want to prove it the same way you prove any other claim, by watching it happen.

One more term, and it is the one that frames the whole lesson. Lesson 17 closed on the difference between a dependency and a deliverable. A deliverable is something the client can run without you. A dependency is something that keeps pulling you back in. The entire art of handover is making sure you leave a deliverable, and the whole reason handover fails is that people treat completion of the work and completion of the handover as the same milestone. They are not. The work being done is the middle of the story. The handover being done is the end.

## Name the owner before you write a word

Self-sufficiency is an ownership claim, not a document count. The client is not self-sufficient because you wrote a thorough wiki. They are self-sufficient because some real person on their team is accountable for the system. If nobody owns it, it does not matter how good your documentation is, because there is no one with a reason to read it and keep it true.

So the first move in a handover is not writing. It is getting the client to name the owner. For each system you deliver, who is accountable if it breaks at two in the morning? Who approves a change to the way it works? Who is the person you hand the keys to? If the answer is "everyone" or "you for now", the handover has not started, no matter how many pages you produce. Everyone owns a thing the way nobody owns it.

Name one owner per system, and put that name in the documentation. The owner is your reader. You write the runbook for them, at the level of detail they need, in the language they speak. A runbook aimed at a coordinator who has never touched a terminal reads differently from one aimed at their engineer, and you need to know which one you are serving before you write a line. The owner is also your first self-sufficiency test subject, which you will need shortly.

## The three jobs of documentation

A handover document set has three jobs, and they answer three different questions. Reproduce answers "how do I run this on a fresh machine". Operate answers "how do I keep the running thing alive". Evolve answers "how do I change it safely after you leave". Most handovers over-produce the first and starve the other two, and that is backwards.

The reproduce doc is the easiest to write and the least used. It covers installation, configuration, and the accounts the system needs. It is the natural thing a consultant writes first, because it mirrors what they just did. But by the time you actually hand over, the system is already running. The reproduce doc becomes a history note, a record of how the thing got here rather than a tool for what happens next. Keep it, because someday the client will move machines or rebuild the environment, but do not mistake it for the handover. It is the least-emergency document in the set.

The operate doc is the runbook, and it is the one that actually gets opened, usually at two in the morning when the weekly report did not land. It is strictly operational: what fires, when it fires, where the output goes, how to check the thing is healthy, and what to do when it breaks. It is written as procedures, not as an explanation of the architecture. "Run this command and look for this" beats "the scheduler invokes the job which posts to the channel". The runbook is the document that earns you your nights back, because every question it answers is a call you will not take.

The evolve doc is how the system is built and why the decisions were made, plus what it deliberately does not do. This is the document that makes the client genuinely self-sufficient, because self-sufficiency is not just running the thing. It is changing it. If the client cannot safely modify the system after you leave, you have not made them independent, you have made them dependent on you for every change, which is a different flavor of the same dependency. The evolve doc records the shape of the system, the alternatives you considered and rejected and why, and a short list of known limits.

Write all three. But know going in which one the client will reach for, and write that one first and best.

## Build the documents through the engagement, not on the last day

The single most common way handover fails is timing. If you write the documentation on the last day of the engagement, you write it from memory, and what comes out is the happy path with the pain removed. This is the same failure Lesson 17 described for skills, and it is worth saying again in this context because it is even more corrosive here. The runbook step that took you two hours to figure out gets compressed into a single sentence that no longer tells the next person the mechanism. They read it, hit the wall anyway, and call you, and the dependency has nothing to do with your notes. It has to do with your notes not being true.

The fix is to stop treating documentation as an end-of-engagement task and start treating it as a build task. The runbook gets its first entry the day the weekly report first fires on its own, while you still remember the delivery target, the check you ran to confirm it landed, and the path to recover when it does not. The decision record gets an entry the day you make the call, while you still remember the alternative you rejected and the reason. The known-limits list grows as you hit edges and decide not to solve them, not when you try to reconstruct them from a clean desk.

When you build documents through the engagement, they write themselves mostly, because you are capturing a decision at the moment you make it. The work of handover is substantially done before the last week. The last week is for testing the documents and fixing the gaps they expose, not for authoring them from scratch. If you catch yourself on the final Wednesday writing the runbook for the first time, that is the alert that you left it too late.

## Run it, do not narrate it: the self-sufficiency test

Here is the discipline that separates handover professionals from everyone else, and it reuses the finishing discipline from Lesson 15 in a new place. The documents are not done when you have written them. They are done when a competent member of the client's team runs the system using only the documents and does not need to ask you a question. That is the verification standard, and it is the only one that means anything.

This is why the handover ceremony cannot be a presentation. A presentation checks your ability to explain, not their ability to operate. You can deliver a flawless walkthrough and still leave them helpless the moment you are out of the room, because you did all the running. So do the opposite. Give the client the documentation, hand them the keys, and sit back to watch them perform the full cycle themselves while you take notes.

The cycle you watch them run is the complete operating loop. They check that the report ran, and they confirm it landed where it was supposed to, using the health-check command from the runbook. Then you introduce a simulated failure, for instance you make the ticketing platform unreachable, and you watch whether the runbook tells them how to recognize that the report failed loudly instead of pretending to succeed, and what to do about it. Then they restart what broke and change one routine setting. Throughout, you do not help unless they are truly stuck, because every time you reach in, you are hiding a gap in the documentation from yourself.

Treat what you learn the way you would treat a failed test. If a step forces them to ask you something the runbook should have covered, that is a documentation bug, not a user error. You fix the document, then you run the loop again until they do not need you. A handover is verified the same way a build is verified: once it survives a stranger using it without your help, and not before. "I showed them how to run it" is intent. "They ran it without me" is proof.

## Secrets out, ownership in

A handover that requires the client to keep running on your accounts is not a handover. It is a hostage situation, and it is the fastest way to convert a clean engagement into an ongoing obligation you did not price. This is the handover face of Lesson 13's least-privilege rule. Never build a system that runs on your personal API key, your personal email account, or your personal token, because the moment you leave, one of two things happens. The system breaks, because your credential expires or you revoke it, or the system quietly keeps running on your account, which means you are carrying the security risk and the dependency for a system you no longer get paid to maintain. Either way you lose.

So part of the build work, not the handover work, is creating the accounts and credentials the system needs in the client's name. The APIs, tokens, and keys live in their stores from the start, and the transfer of ownership happens as you build, not on the last day in a panic. By the time you leave, there is nothing running that needs you to keep existing.

Separate what you hand over from what you must never hand over. The non-secret material is the documentation: the procedures, the architecture, the settings, the decision record. Share those freely, because they are what makes the client self-sufficient. The secrets stay out of the documents entirely. Documentation references a secret by name, the key the team vault holds, or the account the runbook says to use, and the value lives in the client's own secret store. A document with a real credential sitting in it is not documentation. It is a leak waiting to happen, and worse, it poisons the shareability of everything around it, because you can no longer hand the file to anyone without exposing the key. Clean documents are shareable; clean documents are how the knowledge survives you.

## Let the running system carry part of the handover

A system you build on something like Hermes Agent does not exist only in the documents you write. It keeps its own record while it works, and that record is often the most truthful documentation you have, because the system produced it by doing the work rather than by you remembering it. A smart handover points the client at that live record instead of trying to duplicate it in prose.

The operational schedule is a concrete example. When you hand over recurring work, the schedule is not just a prompt you typed. It is stored as plain, machine-readable data on disk, and the job definitions survive updates, restarts, and reboots. A new owner can inspect that record directly. Better still, the runbook should point at a real health-check command that lists every scheduled job and its last status, rather than at a paragraph you wrote describing what you think the health is. Point at the tool and let the client read the live state themselves. That is the verification reflex from Lesson 15 applied to documentation: a document that shows the client how to check the truth beats a document that asserts a truth you have not re-verified since writing it.

The engagement's decision history is another piece of the live record. On a platform with a durable task board, every task is a stored row and every handoff and comment is a row anyone can read, and that history survives the session that produced it. The "why did we build it this way" questions, which are exactly what the evolve document exists to answer, are partly answered already in that durable trail. Point at it. The reusable knowledge you packaged in Lesson 17, the skills, can be checked into the client's own repository so their team keeps loading it after you leave, which is the concrete way the knowledge outlives the engagement.

The pattern behind all of it is the same. Handwritten documentation decays, because the system changes and the words do not catch up. The live record is always current. So the job is not to write documents that restate the live record, it is to write documents that teach the reader how to read the live record and what to do about what they find.

## Cut the cord deliberately

A handover is not the absence of you. It is a deliberate end to the dependency, and it deserves the same planning as the work itself. Decide, in advance and in writing, what happens after you leave.

A short, paid, time-boxed support window is an honest and useful structure. The client knows you are reachable for the first week or two of live operation, you know exactly when that window ends, and the window gives you a controlled place to catch the edge cases that only surface when real users and real data run the thing. Every question that arrives during the window gets sorted into one of two buckets by you. If it is a gap the documentation should have covered, you close the gap in the documentation, so the next time the question does not need you. If it is a genuine change request, it is new work, and you scope it as such. Sorting every incoming item into one of those two buckets is what lets the window actually end, because it means the window is not a perpetual to-do list, it is a bounded period that resolves to either a better document or a new project.

The cord you cut is the dependence on you, not the relationship. The documentation should record who at the client to escalate to, what the standing expectations are, and what counts as an emergency versus a routine question. Self-sufficiency does not mean the client is alone. It means they can operate without you, and they know whom to contact when something is genuinely beyond the runbook. Leaving that path intact is not leaving a dependency. It is leaving a professional relationship that no longer requires you for every keystroke.

## Worked example: handing over the claims-intake engagement

This is the engagement you have carried since Lesson 10: an agent wired to the client's ticketing platform that drafts replies to intake, with a weekly report that fires every Friday at five on its own. Lesson 15 made you verify how the report fails loudly when the platform is unreachable. Lesson 17 made you package the reusable pieces. Now you close it out so the client can run it without you.

Step one, name the owner. You get the client's operations lead, call her Lila, to take accountability for the system. Her name goes at the top of the runbook. She is your reader, so you write at the level she needs, and she is your test subject, so her being able to run it becomes the proof.

Step two, assemble the three documents. The reproduce doc covers installing the per-engagement profile and its skills from the client's repository onto a fresh machine and pointing it at the ticketing configuration. The operate doc, the runbook, is specific and procedural: the report fires Friday at five, it lands in the team channel, here is the health-check command that lists the job and its last status, here is how you confirm it delivered, here is what "unreachable" looks like and how the report fails loud, and here is the recovery path. The evolve doc records the shape of the system, the decision to keep a human in the loop on reply drafts rather than auto-sending, the alternative you rejected, and the list of edges you deliberately did not solve.

Step three, move the credentials into Lila's name. The ticketing credentials and the channel token live in the client's own secret store from early in the build, and the runbook refers to them by name, never with values inline. There is nothing running that depends on a key you own.

Step four, run the self-sufficiency test. You hand her the runbook and the keys, and you watch. She confirms the report ran and landed, you inject the unreachable-platform failure and watch her recognize the loud failure and follow the recovery path, she restarts what broke, she changes a routine setting. Every time she hesitates and looks at you, you note the gap, fix the runbook, and run the loop again. You are done when she does it without looking up.

Step five, cut the cord. You agree on a two-week, time-boxed window at a rate. Questions in the window get sorted into doc gaps and change requests, and the doc gaps get closed so they do not come back. The runbook records who at the client escalates when something genuinely exceeds it, and what counts as an emergency versus a routine ask. When the window closes, the system runs on Lila's team's judgment, the client's credentials, and your documentation, and you walked away from a dependency and into a relationship.

## The pitfalls to avoid

**Documenting from memory on the last day.** You will write the happy path with the pain removed, and the runbook will stop being true exactly where it needs to help. Build documents through the engagement, not at the end.

**Confusing a presentation with a handover.** Narrating your work proves your ability to explain, not their ability to operate. The test is watching them run it without you, so run the ceremony that way.

**Leaving the client on your credentials.** A system that runs on your accounts is a hostage situation. Create the client-side accounts during the build and transfer ownership before you leave.

**Writing only the reproduce doc.** The install history is the least-used document. The runbook is the one that gets opened at two in the morning, and the evolve doc is the one that makes the client actually independent. Neglect those two and you have a history note, not a handover.

**Handing over a system with no named owner.** Self-sufficiency is an ownership claim. If nobody is accountable, everyone can assume someone else is, and nobody keeps the documentation true.

**Never testing the documentation.** A doc you have not watched a stranger follow is an untested artifact with the same status as untested code. Run it once and fix what breaks.

**Leaving the end vague.** "You can always reach out" is a dependency with no closing date. Plan the support window explicitly, and sort every incoming question into a doc fix or a change request so the window genuinely ends.

## Try it

Take the engagement you have been carrying through this series and draft its handover before you ship anything:

1. Name the owner. Which person on the client's team is accountable for the system, and is their name written into the documentation?
2. Separate the three documents. What goes in the reproduce doc, what in the operate runbook, and what in the evolve doc? Which one will the client actually reach for first, and have you started it?
3. Audit a procedure. Pick the most error-prone operational step and write it as a runbook entry. Does a stranger who reads only that entry know what to run and what "it worked" looks like?
4. Test your documentation. Hand the entry to a colleague who has never seen the system and watch them follow it. Where do they stop or ask for help, and what does that tell you is missing?
5. Check the credentials. Does anything the system runs on still depend on something only you own? If so, what would need to change for the client to run it without you?
6. Cut the cord. Write the shape of the support window: how long, cost, and the rule for sorting every incoming question into a documentation gap or a change request.

If you can answer all six with specific artifacts and a run you have watched someone else perform, you have a handover. If most of your answers are plans for the final week, that is the tell: you are about to write the docs from memory. Start the documentation now, while the decisions are still attached to their reasons.

## What's next

You can now leave a client who can run the system without you: you have named the owner, built the reproduce, operate, and evolve documents, tested them by watching the client run the work, moved the credentials into their hands, and closed the dependency deliberately. That is a clean handover on paper. But there is a second question that determines whether the system survives contact with the real organization, and it is not a technical one. A system the team does not actually use is a failed engagement no matter how well it was handed over, and getting real people to change their habits is rarely a documentation problem. The next lesson is about change management and adoption: getting the people whose work the system touches to actually use it, and what to do when a well-built, well-documented system meets an organization that had no reason to change. Where this lesson made the handover clean, the next one makes it stick.
