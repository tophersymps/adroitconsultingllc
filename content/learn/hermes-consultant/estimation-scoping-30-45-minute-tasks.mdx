# Lesson 16: Estimation: Scoping a 30-45 Minute Task, and Why Small Tasks Ship Reliably

Lesson 15 gave you the finishing-the-job discipline: deliver verified artifacts, report blockers honestly, and capture what you learn. That discipline only pays off if you can actually finish, and what decides whether you finish is rarely effort or talent. It is whether you sized the task right before you started. A task you can carry from start to finish in one sitting is a task you can verify, and a task you can verify is a task you can truthfully report as done. This lesson teaches you how to scope work into a 30 to 45 minute window, why tasks that small ship reliably, and how to catch a task that has grown too big before it swallows your day.

## Estimation is a sizing decision, not a prediction

Most people treat estimation as a numbers game. They predict how many hours a project will take, add padding, and defend the number in a meeting. For agent work that framing is close to useless, because the future it predicts is not yours to control. Deadlines move, requirements drift, and a number you produced on day one tells you little about day three.

Estimation done well is a decision about how you are going to work right now, not a prediction of the future. The question it answers is concrete: can I carry this piece of work from start to finish in a single focused window, and can I prove it worked when I reach the end? A good estimate produces a task you can start, complete, and verify without losing your place. A bad estimate produces a task that sprawls, one you start optimistically and leave half-done when your window runs out.

That shift matters because it changes what you optimize for. When you treat estimation as prediction, you optimize for a number that matches reality, which you cannot control. When you treat it as sizing, you optimize for a shape of work you can actually complete, which you control completely. You stop asking how long something will take and start asking how small you can make it. That is the question this lesson is about.

## Why small tasks ship reliably

The claim in the lesson title is not a preference. It is a mechanical fact with four parts.

A small task is finishable. There is a limit to how much focused work a person, or an agent, can hold in context before attention fragments and quality drops. A task sized to fit that limit is a task you can realistically reach the end of in one go. A task sized past it is a task you will set down and come back to, and every restart costs context you have to rebuild.

A small task is verifiable. You can only prove a piece of work is correct when you can see the whole of it at once. When the scope is small, you know exactly what done means and you can check it. When the scope is large, the definition of done blurs, and the verification you ran on part one says nothing about part four. The finishing discipline from lesson 15 only works at a size where the whole result fits in view.

A small task is reportable. Honesty, the hard rule from lesson 15, depends on knowing what actually happened. You can truthfully say a task is done only when you saw it finish, and you can truthfully say a task is blocked only when you can name the exact thing that stopped it. Both are easier at small scale. A large task hides its status in a fog of partial progress, and a foggy status is where fabricated progress reports come from.

A small task has bounded failure. When something goes wrong, the cost is contained. You lose half an hour, you diagnose the specific failure, and you retry with more information. A large task that fails late costs days and often produces no usable evidence about what went wrong, because the failure is buried under too much work to inspect. Small failures are cheap lessons. Large failures are expensive mysteries.

## Where the 30 to 45 minute window comes from

The specific number matters less than the reasoning behind it, but the reasoning is worth naming so you can reproduce it for your own context.

A 30 to 45 minute window fits inside a human attention span. It is short enough that you stay sharp from first step to last, and long enough to do real work, not just housekeeping. A task that fits in that window can be carried through the whole cycle in one context: you decide what to do, you do it, you verify it, and you report it, all while you still remember what you were trying to do and why. That continuity is what makes verification honest, because you can connect the output back to the intent.

The same reasoning applies to an agent. Hermes runs a task in a session with a finite budget of tool calls and a context window that holds what the agent has seen so far. A task that fits in that budget completes, verifies, and reports cleanly. A task that exceeds it hits the agent's hard iteration cap mid-work, leaves partial output, and forces a restart where the new session must reconstruct what the old one was doing. The tool is engineered around the same insight: work that fits in one window ships, and work that does not stalls at a boundary. [^1]

Use 30 to 45 minutes as the default. Adjust it down when the work is unfamiliar or risky, and treat any task that clearly needs more than one window as a sign to decompose, not a sign to make a bigger window. [^1]

## Decomposing a big thing into small tasks

The skill of estimation is really the skill of decomposition, so this is where the work lives.

Start with the outcome and the acceptance test. Write down what done looks like and how you will know it is done. Everything else flows from that, because you cannot size a piece of work you have not defined. This is the same discipline from lesson 6, a clear success criterion, applied at task level.

Then split by deliverable, not by activity. A common mistake is to slice by what you will be doing: write the tests, then the code, then the docs. That produces tasks that depend on each other and cannot ship alone. Slice by what you will produce instead: a verified function with its tests, a working endpoint, a documented configuration. Each slice is a thing a client could point at and accept, and each slice is small enough to verify on its own.

For each slice, apply the window test. Could you carry this from start to finish in 30 to 45 minutes, and can you prove it at the end? If the answer is no, slice again. If the answer is yes, the slice is a task.

Order the slices by risk, not by convenience. Do the uncertain part first, while you still have the budget and attention to absorb a surprise. A risky slice that fails at the end of a sequence invalidates everything before it. Put it at the front so its failure is cheap and its success de-risks the rest. This is the same instinct that tells you to prototype the hard part before polishing the easy part.

## Signs a task has grown too big

Estimation also means recognizing when a task you have already scoped is wrong, before it wastes your window. Learn the signals.

The task has no single done state. If you cannot state, in one sentence, what finished looks like, the task is really two or three tasks wearing one coat. Split it until each piece has a clean acceptance test.

The task needs research and building. Work that requires you to discover something you do not know, and then build on it, is two tasks. Discovery changes the plan, and you should not commit to a build before you have absorbed what discovery teaches you. Separate them.

The task would take a reviewer to trust. If a colleague would reasonably want to see intermediate output before you continue, you have crossed a boundary where a human should be in the loop. Lesson 5's map applies. Respect it by stopping at the boundary and handing over, not by powering through without review. Hermes enforces this shape with a completion checkpoint: a worker may call the completion tool only after verifying the task contract, and it uses the block tool, not fake completion, when human action is required. [^3]

## The rhythm of many small shippings

There is a compound effect worth seeing. One large task produces one moment of completion at the very end, after a long stretch of uncertain status. Ten small tasks produce ten completions, each verified, each reportable, each a small win that tells you whether the approach is working. Momentum is not a motivational luxury. It is information, because every early completion is a check that your plan matches reality, and a plan that is wrong surfaces fast instead of failing late.

This is also why the small-task discipline composes with the rest of the series. Each small task is a bounded, reviewable unit, which is the shape lesson 6 said an agent should operate in. Each one gets a clear success criterion, which is the discipline from lesson 6 again. Each one produces a verified artifact you can honestly report, which is lesson 15's finishing discipline working as intended. And each one is a slice of work you actually execute and verify against the live system, not a description of work, which is what the tooling in lesson 15 makes possible. [^4] The estimation habit is the link that lets all of the earlier lessons actually happen.

## Worked example: scoping an agent setup

Put it together on a concrete case, the kind a client actually hands you. A small company asks you to stand up an agent that monitors their inbox for urgent messages and alerts the team in their chat tool. It sounds like one job. It is several, and this is where estimation earns its keep.

Split by deliverable. First task: install and configure Hermes on a clean machine and verify it runs, the deployment discipline from lesson 7, sized as its own slice. Second task: create the profile and the skill that define how the agent behaves on this specific client's mail, isolated per engagement as lesson 7 taught. Third task: wire the chat integration and confirm a test alert lands, which is the messaging work from the platform setup. Fourth task: schedule the monitoring as a recurring job with delivery back to the team, the cron pattern from lesson 11. [^2] Fifth task: write the one-page handover so the client knows what runs, what it checks, and who owns it.

Each of these is a 30 to 45 minute slice with a clean acceptance test. The agent runs, the profile is isolated, the alert lands, the schedule fires, the handover exists. Any one of them can be verified and reported independently, and a failure in any one of them is contained and diagnosable. The whole engagement is five small shippings instead of one large promise, and the client sees progress at every step, which is the best status update there is. [^1]

## Try it

Take a task from the client you have carried through this series, ideally one you were tempted to size as a single afternoon of work, and decompose it:

1. Write the outcome and the acceptance test in one sentence.
2. Split it by deliverable so each slice is a thing a client could accept.
3. Apply the window test to each slice: can you carry it start to finish in 30 to 45 minutes and prove it?
4. Split any slice that fails, and order the slices risk-first.
5. Name the boundary where a human should review before you continue, and plan to stop there.

A task you can finish in one sitting is a task you can verify, and a task you can verify is a task you can truthfully report as done. That is the whole of the estimation skill, and it is what lets every other lesson in this series hold up under real deadlines.
