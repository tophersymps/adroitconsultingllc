# Lesson 2: The Consulting Engagement Lifecycle

Lesson 1 gave you the map: what an agent-implementation consultant does, and how that differs from being a builder. It ended with the lifecycle in four parts and told you to treat it as the container for everything else. This lesson does the real work. It takes the lifecycle apart into its five phases, defines what each one produces, gives you the exit test that says you are allowed to move on, and shows you where engagements quietly die. When you finish, you will be able to look at any engagement, at any moment, and say exactly which phase you are in and what has to happen before you leave it. That ability is the core consulting skill, and it transfers to any tool you ever implement.

## Why a lifecycle at all

You could skip straight to building. Most people who are good with agents want to. Here is why the lifecycle is not bureaucracy.

The lifecycle exists to force decisions in the right order, and to protect both sides. The client is protected because nothing gets built until they have agreed, in writing, what will be built. You are protected because you never build on a guess, and you never hand over a system the client cannot run. When a consultant says "the scope changed," it usually means one of the phases was skipped and the surprise surfaced at the wrong time. When a client says "this is not what I asked for," it means discovery did not happen. The phases are the rails that keep the engagement on track.

There is a second reason the lifecycle matters, and it is the one that separates consultants from technicians. A technician is judged on whether the thing works. A consultant is judged on whether the client can live with it after you leave. The phases are ordered exactly so that the last thing you do is the thing that makes you unnecessary. Keep that in mind and every phase makes sense.

## The five phases

The lifecycle runs discover, design, implement, handover, support. Each phase has a job and a product. Do not confuse the two. The job is the work you do in the room. The product is the thing you hand to the next phase. Here is each phase with its product and its exit test.

**Discover.** Your job is to understand the client, the problem, the people, and the constraints before you propose anything. You interview, you watch the work happen, you learn what the numbers actually say, and you find out who has to approve changes and who has to live with them. The product of discovery is a written statement of the problem that the client agrees with, plus a go or no-go decision on whether an agent is even the right answer. The exit test: you can state the problem in one sentence, the client nods, and you have made an honest call about whether to proceed. If you cannot state the problem, you are not done discovering.

**Design.** Your job is to decide the shape of the solution before any code exists. What the agent will do, what it will refuse to do, what tools and data it touches, what its guardrails are, how it fits the systems the client already runs, and what the human approval points look like. The product is a written design that the client signs off on. The exit test: the client has agreed, on paper, to what you will build, including the boundaries. Never start implementing on a design nobody approved.

**Implement.** Your job is to build against the design, and to prove the build does what the design promised. The product is a working agent plus a validation record: real cases it handled, edge cases it refused, failures that were caught and safe. The exit test: the agent demonstrably does what the agreed scope says, and you can show it. If you cannot prove it, you are not done.

**Handover.** Your job is to make the client self-sufficient. You document how it works, you train the people who will use and maintain it, and you transfer ownership. The product is documentation plus a trained team plus the keys. The exit test: the client can run and adjust the system without you. If your departure would break it, you have not delivered.

**Support.** Your job is to make the system survive its first real contact with production. You monitor, you fix the edge cases that only show up at volume, and you do it within whatever the contract covers. The product is an agreed maintenance cadence with a clear end. The exit test: the support window closes cleanly, with a record of what changed and a clear decision on whether it extends.

Notice that support is a phase, not an afterthought. It is easy to treat go-live as the finish line. In practice the first weeks in production are where the design meets reality, and a consultant who vanishes at go-live leaves the client stranded at exactly the moment they need you.

## Where the lifecycle breaks

The phases only protect you if you run them in order. Every serious failure in an agent engagement traces back to a skipped phase.

**Skipping discovery** is the most common and the most expensive. You build a capable agent for a problem the client never stated, or you build for the problem they described instead of the problem they have. The client is under no obligation to pay for your guess, and they will not.

**Design by implementation** is the builder's instinct leaking into consulting. You start standing up the agent and let the scope reveal itself as you go. That feels agile until the client sees the invoice and asks what you agreed to, and you cannot point at a signed design.

**Leaving handover to the end** happens when the build eats the budget. The agent works, the validation passes, and then you realize there is no time to document or train, so you hand over a working system nobody can run. That is a failed engagement by the only definition that matters.

**Ignoring support** produces the call six weeks later: the agent has been quietly failing, nobody noticed, and the client is angry. The fix was cheap in a support window and expensive after it closed.

The discipline to remember is that each phase ends with a product and a decision, not just with time spent. Move on when the product exists and the exit test passes, not when you feel done.

## Worked example

Put all five phases on one engagement. A financial advisory firm has about forty advisers. Every morning a pile of routine client emails lands: move money between accounts, update an address, book a meeting, ask about a statement. A junior adviser spends hours drafting replies that mostly say the same things. The firm's compliance team is nervous about anything touching client data, and there is no IT team to run things.

The consultant starts in discovery. They interview the operations lead, three advisers, and the compliance officer. They learn the volume, about sixty routine emails a day, and that most are repetitive. They learn the constraint that shapes everything: compliance will not allow any system to send anything to a client, only to draft, and every draft must be reviewed by a human. They learn the non-goal: nobody is trying to cut headcount, they are trying to free the junior adviser for work that needs a human. The problem statement they land on is one sentence: routine email drafting eats junior time and produces inconsistent replies. The exit test passes, including the honest call that an agent fits here because the task is high volume, low judgment, and the approval step satisfies the risk.

Design. The consultant writes the shape of the solution: a Hermes Agent that reads the incoming email, drafts a reply using the firm's style templates, flags anything it is not sure about instead of guessing, and posts the draft into the team's chat channel for an adviser to approve. The guardrails are explicit. The agent never sends. It does not touch client data beyond the thread it is working on. It refuses anything that needs real judgment, tax advice, complaints, anything unusual. [^1] The approval step is not a compromise forced on the design. It is the design, because it is the thing that lets compliance say yes. The operations lead and the compliance officer sign the design before anything is built.

Implement. The consultant stands up a Hermes profile in the firm's environment, connects the inbox and the team's chat platform through the messaging gateway, writes the reply-drafting skill from the firm's templates, and configures the approval flow. [^2] Then they validate on a week of real, anonymized emails. The drafts match the firm's tone. The uncertain ones are flagged instead of invented. Nothing ships without approval. The validation record shows the cases handled, the cases refused, and the proof that the guardrails held.

Handover. The consultant documents how the system works, walks the advisers and the operations lead through approving and editing drafts, and teaches the ops lead how to add a template or adjust the instructions. They hand over the keys. The exit test passes when the ops lead can change the system without the consultant in the room.

Support. The contract includes a two-week support window. In that window the consultant watches real volume, fixes the edge cases that only appear when the system runs all day, and closes the window with a record of what changed and a clear decision on whether the firm wants an ongoing retainer.

Now step back. Nothing in that arc is specific to Hermes Agent. The same five phases run whether you are implementing Hermes Agent, a CRM, a data pipeline, or a document workflow. The tool fills in the details of each phase, and Hermes Agent happens to be a good vehicle for the demonstration because its design decisions map neatly onto the phases: profiles and multiple deployment backends support the discover and design work, the messaging gateway and approval controls support implement, documentation and skills support handover, and cron and monitoring support the support phase. [^3] But the skill being taught here is the arc itself. Get the arc right with any tool and you can carry it to any client.

## Try it

Pick an engagement, real or imagined, where someone wants an agent to do a recurring piece of work. Before you touch any tool, write one line for each of the five phases:

1. Discover: the problem in one sentence, and the one constraint that will shape the design.
2. Design: the approval point that lets the nervous stakeholder say yes.
3. Implement: the three cases you will test to prove it works.
4. Handover: what the client has to be able to do without you.
5. Support: when the support window closes and what counts as a clean close.

If you can write all five lines before building, you are thinking like a consultant. If you find yourself reaching for the agent first, stop and go back to line one.
