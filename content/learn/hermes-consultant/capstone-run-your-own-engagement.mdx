# Lesson 30: Capstone: Run Your Own Engagement End to End

Twenty-nine lessons taught you the technique in pieces. Discovery and scoping. Estimation. Contracts. Security and guardrails. Implementation discipline. Testing and verification. Documentation and handover. Each lesson isolated one craft and drilled it. This lesson is the capstone because it stops isolating. It asks the question no single lesson could answer: can you run the whole engagement, end to end, with no lesson sitting next to you telling you which skill to reach for next?

That is the real job. A client does not hire you to know one phase well. They hire you to carry the engagement from the first conversation to the point where they can run the system and you are gone. The phases only matter because they are the rails the whole journey runs on. An engagement that fails usually fails not because any one technique was weak but because the consultant could only do one phase in isolation and could not connect discovery to design to handover when each phase stopped being scaffolded by a course.

This capstone gives you a single, coherent arc. It defines the terms you will use, walks one full engagement from discovery through handover so you can see every prior lesson sitting in its natural place, and ends with a rehearsal you can run alone. When you finish, you should be able to take a real inquiry from a real company and carry it to a clean close without needing a tutorial at each step.

## What "run an engagement end to end" means

Run an engagement end to end means you own the full arc: you discover the real problem, you scope and price the work, you agree the contract and guardrails, you build and test the agent, you hand over a system the client can run and maintain, and you close the engagement cleanly with a record of what changed and a clear decision about what happens next. Nothing is skipped, and no phase is left to the client to figure out.

Define the terms you will use through the rest of this lesson, because the capstone depends on you being precise about them:

- **Engagement.** A bounded piece of consulting work with a defined start, a defined scope, and a clean end. It is not "being around to help." It is a project with a beginning and a close.
- **Discovery.** The phase where you find out what the client actually needs, not what they first asked for. It ends with a problem statement that both sides agree to.
- **Scoping.** The phase where you turn the agreed problem into a bounded, priced statement of what you will do and, just as importantly, what you will not do.
- **Guardrails.** The hard limits around what the agent can and cannot do, who must approve what, and what it will refuse. Security and governance live here, and they are set before anything is built.
- **Acceptance.** The moment where the client confirms, against the agreed tests, that the delivered system does what was promised. It is the formal handshake between implementation and handover.
- **Handover.** The phase where you make the client self-sufficient: documentation, training, credentials moved into their name, and the ability to change and run the system without you.
- **Clean close.** The deliberate end of the engagement: a summary of what was delivered, what changed, and a decision on whether there is follow-on work. A clean close protects both you and the client.

Keep that vocabulary. It is the map for the whole arc, and it is what lets you say, at any moment in an engagement, exactly where you are and what has to happen before you move.

## The full arc, end to end

Here is one engagement run start to finish. It is not a hypothetical teed-up for a single lesson. It uses every phase, and every phase connects to the one before it.

A mid-sized insurance broker has a claims intake process that runs on email and spreadsheets. A claims assistant spends the first two hours of every day reading incoming claim notifications, extracting a dozen fields, and entering them into a claims system. The work is real but it is mechanical, and the assistant resents it because it is not why they were hired. The broker's managing director has heard about AI and wants to "automate the email thing."

### Discovery

The managing director has framed the problem as "automate the email thing." That is a desire, not a problem. Discovery's job is to find the real problem underneath. You interview the claims assistant, the managing director, and the compliance officer. You watch the work happen. You learn the volume, roughly forty claim notifications a day, and you learn the real friction is not reading the email, it is that the assistant must copy the same fields into the claims system with no typo, and any typo causes a downstream rejection. You learn the non-goal: nobody wants the agent to decide a claim. You learn the constraint that shapes everything: the broker is regulated, and anything that touches a claim must be auditable, and no irreversible action may happen without a human.

The problem statement you land on is one sentence: claim intake consumes two hours of skilled time a day, and manual data entry produces enough errors to cost the broker time on the back end. The exit test for discovery is that the managing director, the claims assistant, and the compliance officer all agree that sentence describes the problem, and that an agent fits because the task is high volume, rule-following, and low judgment, with a human approval point that satisfies the compliance constraint. That is the honest go/no-go call that discovery exists to make. [^1]

### Scoping and pricing

You now turn the problem into a bounded, priced scope. You define exactly what the agent will do: read an incoming claim notification, extract the fields, present them to the claims assistant for review and approval, and write to the claims system only after approval. You define what it will not do: decide a claim, pay a claim, contact a policyholder, or take any irreversible action. You write the exclusions plainly because exclusions, not inclusions, are what stop scope creep.

You estimate the work by decomposing it. This is where earlier estimation lessons pay off. You break the engagement into the tasks that map to real work: standing up the profile, wiring the email intake, building the extraction field set, building the approval flow, writing the training set for validation, testing on real anonymized claims, and writing the handover docs. Each task is small enough to estimate, and the sum gives you a defensible price and timeline instead of a number you made up to sound confident. [^2]

### Contract and guardrails

Before you build, you write the agreement and the guardrails. The contract states the problem, the scope, the exclusions, the price, the delivery milestones, and the acceptance criteria. It exists so neither side can drift from the agreement quietly. The guardrails are set in the same step: the agent never takes irreversible action without approval, it never touches data outside the claim thread, and it refuses anything ambiguous instead of guessing. You write the human approval point into the design, not as an afterthought, because it is the thing that lets the compliance officer say yes. Security decisions, credential handling, and the approval boundary are decided now, before any code exists. [^3]

### Implementation

You build against the agreed scope and guardrails. Standing up a Hermes profile in the broker's environment, connecting the email intake, building the extraction and approval skill, and wiring the notification channel. [^4] You implement the field extraction against the agreed field set, you build the approval flow so every extraction is presented to the claims assistant, and you configure the guardrails so the agent refuses rather than guesses when a notification is missing a field or looks unusual.

You do not implement against your memory of the scope. You implement against the agreed acceptance tests. The discipline, finish the job and verify the thing you actually built, applies at every step of implementation, not just at the end. [^5] Guardrails are not a cleanup step after the agent is working; they are part of the build, because an agent that can act without limits is a liability, not a deliverable.

### Acceptance and testing

Before you hand over, the client confirms the system does what was promised. You validate on a week of real, anonymized claim notifications. You test the cases that matter: the standard notification, the missing-field notification (the agent must flag it, not invent it), and the ambiguous one (the agent must refuse rather than guess). You verify the extraction accuracy against the field set, you verify the approval flow works, and you verify the guardrails held, that nothing touched the downstream system without approval.

Acceptance is not you declaring it works. Acceptance is the client, against the agreed tests, confirming that the delivered system matches the agreed scope. That confirmation is the formal gate that lets you move to handover, and it protects you both. A system that passes its own tests but does not match what the client agreed to is not accepted, and you are not done.

### Handover and clean close

Handover is where you make the client self-sufficient. You write reproduce, operate, and evolve documentation, not a history of what you did. [^6] You walk the claims assistant through approving claims and the IT contact through adding a new field or adjusting the extraction instructions. You move credentials into the broker's name so the system runs on their identity, not yours. You test the handover the way you tested the implementation: you watch the client run and change the system, and you only leave when they can do it without you in the room.

Then you close cleanly. You hand over the summary of what was delivered and what changed. You record the decision on follow-on work, whether that is ongoing support or a retainer for the next problem. A clean close is deliberate, not a fade-out. It leaves the client with a system, documentation, and a clear sense of what happens next, and it leaves you with a standing relationship and a reference.

## The connective tissue

The full arc works because every phase connects to the one before it, and a consultant who can only do one phase well cannot carry an engagement. Look at how the pieces chain:

- Discovery produces the problem statement that scoping turns into a priced, bounded scope. If discovery was thin, the scope is a guess.
- Scoping produces the exclusions and estimate that the contract and guardrails formalize. If the scope was vague, the contract cannot hold.
- The contract and guardrails define the boundary that implementation and testing enforce. If the guardrails were an afterthought, they are gaps.
- Implementation produces the working system that acceptance verifies against the agreed tests. If there were no agreed tests, acceptance is theater.
- Acceptance produces the confirmation that lets handover begin. If acceptance never happened, you hand over a system the client never agreed to.
- Handover produces the self-sufficient client, and the clean close produces the summary and the decision on what happens next. If handover was skipped, the client depends on you, and you never really left.

Run that chain as one unit and you are a consultant. Run each piece well in isolation and stall at the seams, and you are a good technician who cannot finish an engagement. The capstone is not about doing any one phase better. It is about carrying the phase you are in into the next one, so the engagement survives contact with the client.

## Common ways the capstone fails

The arc looks simple, so the failures are instructive. Most engagements die at a seam, not in the middle of a phase:

- **Discovery that never ends.** The consultant keeps interviewing because talking feels like progress. The fix is the discovery exit test: a written problem statement both sides agree to. Until that exists, discovery is not done, but the moment it exists, discovery is over.
- **Scope that excludes nothing.** A scope with only inclusions cannot stop scope creep, because every new request is "part of the problem." The fix is written exclusions. Saying no in the contract is how you stay on time and on budget.
- **Guardrails added after implementation.** Setting the agent's limits after it is built means retrofitting safety onto an unconstrained system. The fix is to write the approval boundary and refusal rules during scoping and implement them as part of the build.
- **Acceptance that never formally happens.** The consultant finishes, declares it works, and moves to handover without a client sign-off. The fix is an acceptance gate: the client confirms the delivered system matches the agreed tests before you hand over.
- **Handover that teaches, not that produces.** A long walkthrough where the client nods is not handover. The fix is to watch the client run and change the system on their own, and to leave only when they can.
- **A close that fades instead of finishing.** Ending by going quiet leaves the client unsure what was delivered and what happens next. The fix is the deliberate clean close: a written summary and a decision on follow-on.

Every one of these is a seam failure. The engagement did not fail because a phase was done badly. It failed because the phase was not finished in a way that connected to the next one. Watch the seams and you will not need to watch anything else.

## Try it: the rehearsal

You cannot practice the capstone on a real client until a real client appears, so rehearse the full arc now, alone or with a friend playing the client. Pick a company you know well, a small business you actually understand, and imagine they have asked you to automate some routine recurring work, an email inbox, a report, a follow-up flow, a data-entry chore. Now run the whole engagement on paper:

1. Discovery: write the real problem in one sentence, the non-goal, and the one constraint that will shape the design. State the exit test and apply the honest go/no-go call.
2. Scoping: write the inclusions, the exclusions, and decompose the work into tasks you can estimate. Produce a defensible price and timeline.
3. Contract and guardrails: write the boundary, the human approval point, and the refusal rules, before any build.
4. Implementation: write the steps to stand up and build the agent, and the guardrails you will implement as part of the build.
5. Acceptance: write the three or four tests the client will use to confirm the system matches the scope.
6. Handover: write what the client must be able to do on their own, and the docs you will leave.
7. Clean close: write the summary and the decision on what happens next.

If you can produce all seven artifacts in a sitting, without reaching for the tool first, you have internalized the arc. If you find yourself wanting to skip straight to implementation on step three, that is the exact temptation the capstone is designed to expose. Go back and do the earlier steps properly.

## Where you are now

You began this course with a single question about what an agent-implementation consultant actually does. Twenty-nine lessons later you have the complete craft: when an agent is the right tool, how to discover and scope, how to estimate and price, how to contract and set guardrails, how to build and test, how to govern and secure, and how to hand over and close. You have seen the isolation lessons and now the whole arc where they all fit. What you have is not a bag of tricks. It is a repeatable engagement machine, and it transfers to any tool, not just Hermes Agent. The tool was the vehicle for the demonstration. The consulting skill is the point.

That is the capstone. You are not finished learning, you are finished with the scaffolded map, and the next time you face a client, you run the machine the way this lesson just showed you: end to end, from first conversation to clean close.
