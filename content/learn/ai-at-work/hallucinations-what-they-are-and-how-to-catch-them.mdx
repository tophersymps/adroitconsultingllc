# Lesson 3: Hallucinations, and How to Catch Them

By now you know the machine underneath these tools predicts the next token, over and over. That one fact explains a lot, and this lesson is where it bites hardest. A tool that predicts what sounds most likely is a tool that will sometimes say something confidently wrong. Those wrong answers have a name: hallucinations.

The word sounds dramatic, like a system glitching out. In practice a hallucination is quieter and more dangerous than that. It is a smooth, plausible, entirely invented answer delivered in the same even voice as a true one. IBM's definition is the one to hold: a hallucination is an AI output that "sounds plausible but is factually wrong, irrelevant or entirely fabricated." [^1] Fake facts, invented studies, URLs that do not exist, details about real people and companies that are simply not true. [^1]

Here is the uncomfortable part you need to accept before anything else: you cannot tell a hallucination from a true answer by how confident the tool sounds, because it sounds confident either way. [^3] The only way to catch one is to build habits that do not depend on the tool's tone of voice.

## Why they happen: a guess, not a lookup

The reason follows directly from lesson 1. The model does not look up "what is true" and report it back. It looks at your prompt and produces the sequence of words most likely to follow, based on patterns it learned in training. [^1]

Think about what that means. The most "likely" next sentence is not the same as the correct next sentence. If your company is Acme Widgets and the tool has seen a thousand cases where a company with a similar-sounding name has a headquarters in Chicago, then "Acme Widgets is headquartered in Chicago" is a very likely thing for it to say, whether or not it is true. It is not checking a database of companies. It is following a pattern it has seen before. [^1] [^3]

OpenAI's own explainer is blunt about this: hallucinations are "plausible but false statements" that the model generates because it is optimizing for what sounds right, not for what is verifiable. [^2] When the information you ask about is sparse or ambiguous, the model has less to anchor on, and the odds of it filling the gap with a plausible invention go up. [^1]

That is the whole cause, and it is worth saying plainly because it kills the two myths that get people into trouble. Myth one: "it only happens with obscure trivia." No. It happens with everyday facts too, especially when the details are specific (exact dates, dollar amounts, page numbers, real people's job titles). Myth two: "it is a bug that will get patched." It is not a bug you can fully remove, because it is the direct result of how the tool works. You reduce it, you catch it, you design around it. You do not eliminate it. [^2]

## What a hallucination looks like at work

Hallucinations come in a few recurring shapes, and naming them helps you spot them:

- **Invented facts.** A product description that says the warranty covers something it does not, a policy summary that adds a rule that was never written.
- **Fabricated sources.** The tool cites "a 2023 study by the Brookings Institution" that does not exist, with a title and a finding that sound perfectly reasonable.
- **Wrong specifics.** A date off by a year, a price off by a few dollars, a name slightly misspelled. These are the sneakiest, because the answer is mostly right and your eye skips the one wrong detail.
- **Confident flat wrongness.** A direct, definite answer to a question the tool genuinely does not know, with no hedge and no "I'm not sure." The absence of hedging is not a sign of truth. It is a sign the model picked the most likely pattern.

Keep that last one in mind. Humans hedge when unsure. A next-word predictor does not hedge unless its training taught it to for that type of prompt, and often it just commits to the most probable completion. [^3] A confident answer is not a truthful answer. It is an answer that sounded likely.

## Worked example: the customer and the "policy"

Here is a realistic scene. You ask an assistant to draft a note for a customer who is asking whether they can return an item after 45 days:

> Draft a reply to a customer asking if they can return the Deluxe Model after 45 days. Our standard return window is 30 days. If the item is defective we make exceptions. The customer says the charging port stopped working.

The tool replies that yes, the customer can return it within the standard 45-day window because "most retailers allow a 45-day return period for electronics."

Stop and look at what just happened. The model took the number 45 from the customer's question, matched it to a pattern it had seen ("electronics return windows around 30 to 45 days"), and produced a confident yes. It did not check your actual policy, which you told it was 30 days. It overrode the fact you gave it with a more likely-sounding one. [^1]

This is a textbook hallucination, and it is the kind that causes real damage, because it goes into a customer-facing reply sounding completely official. The fix is not to trust the draft. The fix is to know your own facts. You already knew the return window was 30 days. The moment you read "45-day window," you had everything you needed to reject it. The tool could not out-convince you on a fact you actually knew. [^3]

That is the central insight of this whole lesson: the tool is dangerous mainly in the gaps of your own knowledge. Where you know the answer, you catch its errors for free. Where you do not know the answer, you are vulnerable, and that is exactly where you need a different habit.

## How to catch them: four habits

You cannot eliminate hallucinations, but you can stop almost all of them from reaching your work. Four habits do most of the work.

**One: only trust it on things you can check.** If the output contains a fact you can verify (a date, a policy, a number, a price, a person's title), verify it. That does not mean fact-checking everything yourself. It means using the tool for what it is good at, drafting and summarizing, and keeping the facts in your control. When the tool produces a specific claim, treat it as a lead to check, not a fact to copy. [^1]

**Two: never let it generate sources or citations for you to use.** If the tool provides a reference, a study, a quote, or a link, assume it may not exist until you confirm it opens. The return-window example is mild next to the damage a fabricated source does. There is a well-known legal case where a lawyer used AI-generated citations in court filings, and several of the cited cases turned out to be entirely made up. [^1] The pattern behind that disaster works in any field: a plausible-sounding source is the most convincing lie the tool tells. If you need a real source, go find it yourself.

**Three: ask it to show its work and doubt the extra.** When you need accuracy, do not accept a single answer. Ask it to quote the part of your document it is basing its answer on, or to tell you what it does not know. Ask for the reasoning and then check the reasoning, not just the conclusion. When it adds details you did not supply (a specific date, a number, a name), that is a red flag to examine, because the tool fills gaps with its most likely guess. [^2]

**Four: for high-stakes output, get a second pass.** Two different tools, or one tool asked twice in different words, will not agree perfectly, and the disagreements are where you look. When the stakes are real (a customer reply, a compliance note, anything public), have a human who knows the subject read the final thing before it ships. That is not extra work. That is the job, and the tool is the draft assistant, not the reviewer. [^3]

## Try it

This exercise takes about three minutes and will show you a hallucination in the wild, which is worth more than a paragraph of warnings.

Open any AI assistant you have (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, or an internal tool all work). Ask it a question where the answer is a precise, recent, checkable fact that is easy for a model to get wrong:

> What is the current [specific policy, deadline, or procedure] in our [department or company]? Give me the exact wording and the date it took effect.

Watch what happens. If the tool does not actually have that internal document, it will do one of two things. It will either say it does not know, or it will confidently produce a plausible-sounding policy that may be wrong. If it invents a detail, you have just caught a hallucination live. [^1]

Now run the follow-up that trains the habit:

> For each fact in your answer, tell me which part comes from what I gave you, and which part you are inferring or guessing. Be honest about the parts you are not certain of.

That second prompt does not make the tool perfect, but it forces the uncertain parts into view, which is the whole skill. You are not trying to make the tool never wrong. You are trying to make its wrongness visible to you before it ships. [^2]

## What's next

Lesson 4 asks the question this lesson has been building toward: what is AI genuinely good at, and where does it fail? You now know the tool guesses instead of looking up, and that its guesses occasionally turn into confident inventions. The next lesson maps that behavior onto real work, so you know which tasks to hand the tool and which to keep for yourself. For now, hold the habit from today: the tool is a fast, confident drafter with no built-in truth check, and your job is to be the one who checks. On facts you know, you check for free. On facts you do not know, you slow down, you ask it to show its work, and you never let it hand you a source you have not opened yourself.
