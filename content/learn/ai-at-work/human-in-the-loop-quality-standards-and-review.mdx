# Lesson 23: The Human-in-the-Loop Quality Bar: Standards and Review

Last lesson you stood up a shared prompt library and gave every entry an owner and a version. That gives the team one set of prompts to work from. It does not, on its own, guarantee that what comes back is good enough to send. A prompt can be shared and still produce an email that runs too long, a summary that quietly drops a date, or a reply that invents a promise nobody authorized. [^1] The layer that catches those is the subject of this lesson: agreeing, before you run a task, on what good looks like, and checking the output against that agreement before it goes anywhere.

## The words we are using

Keep these plain, because the whole lesson turns on one distinction.

**A quality standard** (or quality bar) is the written answer to the question "what does good look like for this task?" It lists what must be true for the output to be safe to use: which facts to verify, what must be present, what tone and format it should hold, and what has to come from your own notes rather than the tool's imagination. The point is that you write it down before you run the task, not after you read the result. [^2]

**Review** is reading a finished draft against that standard before it leaves. It is not the same as editing, which Lesson 12 covered as fixing what is there. Editing improves a draft. Review decides whether the draft is good enough to ship at all, and it is the step where your accountability lives. You can edit a draft that is fine and you can review a draft you never touch. The review is the gate. [^3]

**An accountable owner** is the named person who is answerable for a piece of output going out as-is. When a tool drafts an email and you send it, you own it, not the tool. This is the through-line Lesson 6 and Lesson 17 made explicit: the tool produces a draft, a human decides whether it ships, and that human carries the consequence. Naming the owner is how a team keeps that line clear instead of letting it blur into "the AI did it." [^4] [^5]

**An exception rule** is a written trigger for when output needs more than one pair of eyes before it ships. High-stakes or irreversible outputs get flagged automatically: anything that states a price, promises a date, touches a contract, or goes to an angry customer. The rule turns "should someone check this?" from a judgment call you make at midnight into a list you agreed on in daylight. [^6]

## Why a written bar beats a shared vibe

The temptation is to skip the standard and just "look at it" before sending. That instinct is right that a human checks. It is wrong that you can do it reliably without knowing what you are checking for.

Here is what goes wrong without a written bar. First, the check becomes whatever each person happens to notice, and what they notice drifts with their mood, their deadline, and how good the output looks at a glance. A confident-sounding draft gets approved faster than a careful one that is genuinely wrong, which is exactly backwards, because the tool is at its most fluent when it is most wrong. [^2] Second, people disagree about whether a draft is acceptable without ever being able to say why, which turns review into argument. Third, when a bad output slips through, there is nothing to point at as the standard it failed, so the lesson does not stick and the same miss repeats next week. [^7]

A written bar fixes all three. It turns the check from a feeling into a list, gives the team one agreed definition of good instead of thirty private ones, and gives you a reference point when something fails so you can fix the standard, not just the one output. [^7]

There is a second reason a bar matters, and it is about the tool, not the people. The shared prompt from Lesson 22 can be excellent today and quietly degrade later, because the tools and the tasks both change. [^8] A fixed quality bar is how you notice that drift. When output starts failing the bar more often, that is the signal to update the prompt, not to blame whoever reviewed it. The bar is the tripwire that tells you the prompt or the workflow needs maintenance.

## Review is where the human earns the loop

You have now read the words "human in the loop" many times across this series. This is the lesson where that loop gets its real job description. It is not a warm assurance that a person was present. It is a specific, repeatable act: you check output against a standard you agreed on, before the output acts on the world. [^3]

The series has been building to this in pieces. Lesson 6 taught you to read AI output the way you would read a draft from a fallible colleague, which is a review habit. [^4] Lesson 12 taught the difference between editing and reviewing. [^3] Lesson 17 taught the filter for when AI is the wrong tool because your context and accountability are the point. [^5] Lesson 20 taught that a workflow needs a human at the check point to stay honest. [^9] Lesson 21 taught which steps are safe to hand over at all. [^10] This lesson pulls those threads together into one deliberate act and gives it a name: the review against a quality bar. If you take one idea from the whole series, take that review is not optional polish. It is the moment the tool's work becomes your work, and it is where the quality is actually decided.

## A worked example: Marcus sets a bar and reviews against it

Marcus is the customer-support lead from Lessons 19, 20, and 22. He already has a shared prompt that drafts replies to frustrated customers, with an owner and a version. [^11] The team uses it daily. What Marcus notices now is that the drafts come back consistently good in structure and inconsistent in judgment. One agent ships a reply with a concrete date the agent then cannot meet. Another edits the tone until it reads stiff. A third sends a version that over-apologizes and promises a refund the company does not offer.

Marcus does not blame the agents, because the prompt did not tell them what good meant. He runs a short meeting and they write the quality bar for the reply prompt together. The bar has five checks, and every draft must pass all five before it is sent:

1. **Accurate.** Every claim matches real policy. No invented refund, discount, promise, or timeline.
2. **Complete.** It states what Marcus can do, by when, and ends with one clear next step or question.
3. **On-voice.** Calm and direct. Not stiff, not sycophantic, not sharp.
4. **Right length.** Under the agreed 120 words. If it runs longer, something is being over-explained.
5. **Owned.** A named agent reviews and sends it. Nobody forwards a reply they have not personally read and decided on. [^12]

The bar changes the review in two ways. First, the agent checking a draft now has five concrete questions instead of a vague "does this look okay?" That makes the check fast and consistent across four people. [^7] Second, when a reply slips through that over-promises, Marcus can point at check one and ask what happened, then decide whether the fix is coaching the agent, tightening the prompt, or adding an exception rule for anything that states a refund amount. The miss stops being a one-off and becomes data about the workflow. [^13]

## What a good quality bar contains

You do not need a page of criteria for every task. Most tasks need a short list like Marcus's. When you write one, cover these four angles.

**What must be true.** The facts, figures, names, dates, and policy points that have to be right. These are the things to verify, and they are the things a tool can get confidently wrong. [^2]

**What must be present.** The required pieces of the deliverable, so "good" does not mean merely "not wrong." A status email that is factually fine but never states the next step is incomplete. Name the required parts.

**What must come from you.** The context, decisions, and commitments that only you hold, which the tool must not invent. [^5] For a reply that is the refund decision. For a report it is the recommendation. For a message to your boss it is the plan.

**What it must sound and look like.** The tone and the format, so a customer never hears a different voice depending on who answered. This is the part you can bake into the prompt as well, which is worth doing, because tools respond to a clearly stated format and to examples of what right looks like. [^8] But keep it in the bar too, so the reviewer checks the same thing the prompter asked for.

## A review routine that stays fast

A review that becomes a bureaucratic queue is a review people will route around, and then you have no review at all. The fix is to match review depth to how much the output would cost if it were wrong, which is the same risk instinct Lesson 21 used for deciding what to automate. [^10]

Three tiers keep the routine fast.

**Runs free.** Work that stays between you and the tool, where an error costs you a few minutes to catch. Internal summaries, early drafts, research notes. Read these lightly or not at all. The whole point is that this bucket does not eat your attention. [^10]

**Spot check.** Recurring output where quality can drift even when each item looks fine. Check a sample on a set cadence, not every item. If the sample passes the bar, the process is healthy. If it does not, stop and fix the prompt or the standard before you keep shipping. [^13]

**Full review, every time.** Output that leaves the team or the company, states a commitment, or would be costly or embarrassing to recall. Emails to customers, posts, anything with a price or a date, anything a regulator or a lawyer might read. This tier gets the complete check against the bar, by the accountable owner, every single time. [^6]

Two habits keep the routine honest. Keep the standard written where reviewers can see it while they review, not filed away. And keep a short note when you reject output, just enough to see the pattern: which check it failed and why. You are not building an audit trail for its own sake. You are collecting the evidence that tells you when to fix the prompt, update the bar, or retrain a person, instead of guessing. [^7] [^13]

## Where it helps, and where it does not

A quality bar and a review earn their place on exactly the work this series has pointed you toward: the recurring, rule-based tasks where the output is checkable and the standard is real. Drafting the standard email, summarizing the meeting, writing the routine report, replying to the recurring question. On those, a written bar makes review faster, more consistent, and more fair. That is the lane.

Do not stretch a quality bar into pretending that checking a list makes a genuinely hard judgment safe. If the task is one where you are not sure what good looks like even after thinking about it, then the problem is not a missing checklist, and no review routine fixes it. [^5] That is a sign to slow down, get a second opinion from a person, or not use a tool at all. The bar is a tool for work you already know how to judge. It is not a substitute for the judgment itself.

One honest warning before you set this up: a review routine only helps if the person reviewing actually has the authority to stop output that fails the bar, and the time to do it. If reviewers are expected to rubber-stamp everything to hit a target, the bar becomes theater and the routine exists to be ignored. [^12] Set the bar, fund the review, and mean it, or skip the whole thing and accept the misses.

## Try it

Pick one recurring task your team runs through a tool, ideally the same one you built a shared prompt for in Lesson 22. In one sitting, write its quality bar: the four angles from this lesson, kept to a short list that fits on one screen. What must be true, what must be present, what must come from the human, and what it must sound and look like. Share it with the two or three people who do that task and adjust it until everyone agrees.

Then get a second opinion on the design, using this prompt in any tool with the task and the bar you drafted:

> Here is a recurring task my team runs through an AI tool: [describe the task]. Here is the quality bar I drafted for its output: [paste your bar]. Read the bar as a reviewer would. Is anything missing that would let a bad output slip through? Is anything so vague that two reviewers would check it differently? Is the list short enough to actually use every time, or would a busy person skip it? Suggest concrete edits, not general advice, then propose a one-line exception rule for when this output needs a second human before it ships.

Read the answer the way this series has taught you. [^4] The tool is a useful second pair of eyes with no knowledge of your actual work or your team. Take the concrete suggestions you agree with, ignore the rest, and use your judgment about your own stakes. The bar is yours. You are drafting it, not the tool.

## What's next

You now have the full loop in place: shared prompts that encode how the team works, a written quality bar that says what good means, and a review that checks output against it before anything ships. That raises the natural follow-on question, which the next lesson answers. Lesson 24, Measuring what's working: time saved, quality, and adoption, is about how you know whether all of this is actually earning its keep: how much time the tools really save you, whether quality is holding steady or drifting, and whether people are actually adopting the workflows or quietly abandoning them. It is the direct next step, because a quality bar only matters if you measure whether it holds, and you cannot manage a workflow you are not tracking. For now, write the bar for one task, agree on it with the people who do that task, and run a full review against it the next time the task comes up. You are not adding paperwork. You are deciding, together and in advance, what good means, so the human check at the end of the loop is fast, fair, and real instead of improvised. [^12]
