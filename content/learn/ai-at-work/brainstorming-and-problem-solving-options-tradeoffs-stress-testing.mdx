# Lesson 16: Brainstorming and Problem-Solving: Options, Tradeoffs, and Stress-Testing

Lesson 15 closed the loop on meetings: draft the agenda, keep the transcript, verify the action items, and you turn a conversation into a list of real commitments. That is the easy half. The harder half is what this lesson is about. Once you have decided what to do, how do you know it is worth doing? How do you know it is the right option, and not just the first one that sounded reasonable?

Most people use an AI tool for this the wrong way. They ask it a question, get one answer, and treat that answer as the decision. That is backwards. The tool is not a decision-maker. It is a generator. It is very good at producing options, and it is genuinely bad at telling you which option is good. The skill is to use it for what it is good at, and to keep the choosing for yourself.

This lesson gives you a three-part loop that covers most problem-solving with a tool: generate options, weigh the tradeoffs, and stress-test the plan before you commit. Each part is a deliberate move, and each one keeps your judgment in the driver's seat.

## The words we are using

Let me pin down the terms so the moves are crisp rather than fuzzy.

**Brainstorming** is the act of generating a wide set of possible answers to a problem before you evaluate any of them. The point is volume and range, not quality. You are trying to get the field wide enough that a good option has room to appear.

**Options** are the candidate answers themselves. A good brainstorm produces options that differ from each other in real ways, not five versions of the same idea wearing different clothes.

**Tradeoffs** are what you weigh when you compare options. Every real option costs something, risks something, and assumes something. A tradeoff is the honest accounting of what you give up to get what the option offers. There is almost never a free option, and the moment you think you have found one, you have usually missed a cost.

**Stress-testing** is the act of trying to break a plan before it is real. You imagine the plan failing, or you ask what could go wrong, and you use the answers to fix the plan or abandon it. The point is to find the weaknesses while they are cheap to fix, not after you have committed.

**Premortem** is the name for one specific stress-testing technique. Instead of asking "what could go wrong," you imagine the project has already failed, and you work backwards to explain why. Gary Klein, who developed the technique, describes it as prospective hindsight: you place yourself in the future, look back, and ask what went wrong. [^3] It works because it makes it safe to name problems before they happen, instead of waiting until they are undeniable.

Hold those five. They are the whole toolkit, and the rest of this lesson is practice with them.

## Why the tool is a generator, not a chooser

Here is the fact that should shape how you use any AI tool for problem-solving. The tool can produce a lot of ideas quickly, and it cannot reliably tell you which idea is good. That is not a flaw you work around. It is the nature of the thing.

Research on this is direct. A Harvard Business School study of entrepreneurs using an AI business advisor found that the tool did not, on its own, improve performance. The entrepreneurs who were already doing well got a boost, and the ones who were struggling got worse. The difference was not in the advice the tool gave. It was in which advice the users chose to follow. The high performers picked the specific, tailored suggestions. The low performers took the generic ones, like lowering prices or focusing on advertising, which cost money without fixing the underlying problem. [^2] The tool offered the same menu to everyone. The judgment about what to pick is what separated them.

That is the whole lesson in one study. The tool is a menu. You are the one who reads the menu and decides. If you treat the tool's first answer as the answer, you are letting a generator make a decision it is not equipped to make. If you use it to widen the field and then apply your own judgment, you get the benefit without the risk.

The same point shows up in how teams actually work with these tools. In an AI-enabled innovation challenge, teams used AI to generate design options and build prototypes quickly, and that part was fast. But the parts that took the most time and discussion were problem definition and solution design: pushing back on assumptions, deciding what they were actually trying to solve, and stress-testing the designs. [^1] The tool accelerated the generating. The humans did the deciding. That division of labor is the model for everything in this lesson.

## Move one: generate options deliberately

The first move is to get the tool to give you a menu, not a single answer. Most people skip this. They ask "how should I handle X" and take the one response. That is a coin flip, because the tool's default answer is the most statistically ordinary version of your problem, the average case, and your problem is never average.

Instead, ask for range. Say "give me six different ways to approach this" or "list five options for handling this, and make them genuinely different from each other." Ask for options that vary on a specific axis: cost, speed, risk, who does the work, how much it changes the current process. The more you name the axis, the more the options will actually differ.

A useful trick is to ask the tool to argue for each option as if it were the right one. "For each of these five options, make the strongest case for it." That forces the tool to develop each option fully instead of giving you one good one and four throwaways. It also surfaces options you would not have thought to ask for, because the tool has seen many more versions of "how people solve this kind of problem" than you have.

The discipline in this move is to resist evaluating while you are generating. If you stop to judge each option as it appears, you will kill the brainstorm early and settle for the first thing that sounds plausible. Generate first, judge later. The tool is fast, so the cost of a wide field is a few seconds. The cost of a narrow field is a decision you make without seeing the better option that was one prompt away.

## Move two: weigh the tradeoffs

Once you have a real set of options, the second move is to make the tool lay out the tradeoffs honestly. This is where most people get lazy. They look at the options, pick the one that feels best, and move on. The tool can do better than that, if you ask it to.

Ask for the accounting. "For each of these options, tell me what it costs, what it risks, and what it assumes." That is the three-part tradeoff: cost, risk, and assumption. An option that looks cheap may assume something you cannot count on. An option that looks safe may cost more than you think. The tool can lay all of that out side by side, and seeing it in one place is what makes the comparison real.

A decision matrix is a clean way to force this. Give the tool your criteria, ask it to score each option against each criterion, and ask it to show its reasoning. You are not asking the tool to decide. You are asking it to make the tradeoffs visible so you can decide. The scoring is a starting point for your judgment, not a verdict.

The key move here is to ask the tool to be the skeptic. "What is the strongest argument against each of these options?" or "Which of these options would you expect to fail, and why?" A tool that is only asked to praise will give you a sales pitch. A tool that is asked to attack will give you the information you actually need. You want the attack, because the attack is where the real tradeoffs live.

## Move three: stress-test the plan

The third move is the one people skip most, and it is the one that saves the most pain. Once you have picked an option, you stress-test it before you commit. You try to break it while it is still cheap to fix.

The cleanest way is the premortem. Imagine the plan has already failed, and ask the tool to explain why. "It is six months from now and this plan failed. List the most likely reasons it failed, in order of likelihood." Because the tool has seen many versions of "how plans fail," it can surface failure modes you have not thought of: a dependency you forgot, a stakeholder you did not include, a step that takes longer than you estimated. [^3]

The premortem works because it flips the framing. Asking "what could go wrong" invites a shrug. Asking "it failed, why" forces a specific answer. That is the prospective hindsight Klein describes, and it is exactly the kind of question a tool is good at, because it has a large sample of failure stories to draw from. [^3]

You can also stress-test the assumptions you found in move two. "Which of the assumptions behind this plan is most likely to be wrong?" or "What would have to be true for this plan to fail?" Each answer is a risk you can either fix, plan around, or decide to accept. The point is not to find a plan with no risks. There is no such plan. The point is to know the risks before you commit, so you are choosing them on purpose instead of discovering them later.

## The whole loop in one scene

Let me put all three moves together in a scene you will recognize. You are on a small team, and your manager has asked you to cut the time the team spends on weekly status reports. Right now it eats a full afternoon every Friday. You need a plan.

Generate. You ask the tool: "Give me five genuinely different ways to reduce the time our team spends on weekly status reports. Make them differ in how much they change the current process." The tool offers a range: automate the report from a shared tracker, simplify the template to a few lines, rotate who writes it, drop the written report and do a short standup instead, or keep the report but write it in batches. Five options, and they differ in real ways. You do not judge them yet. You just have the field.

Tradeoffs. You ask: "For each of these five, tell me what it costs, what it risks, and what it assumes." The tool lays it out. Automating assumes the tracker has the data and someone can set it up. Simplifying is cheap but risks losing detail the leadership reads. Rotating spreads the work but means inconsistent quality. Dropping the report saves the most time but risks the team losing visibility. Batching is low risk but only saves a little. Now the tradeoffs are visible, and you can see that the cheap options assume things, and the safe options save less.

Stress-test. You pick automation, because it saves the most time and the tracker already has the data. Before you commit, you run the premortem: "It is three months from now and the automated report failed. List the most likely reasons." The tool surfaces them: the tracker data is messier than expected, the setup took longer than a week, leadership did not trust the automated output and asked for the old report anyway. Those are real risks. You decide the setup time is worth it, and you plan to show leadership a sample before you switch over. The plan is better because you found the risks before they found you.

That is the entire skill, in three moves that took a few minutes. Generate to widen the field, weigh the tradeoffs to see the real costs, and stress-test to find the weaknesses while they are cheap. No move was wasted, and your judgment made the call at every step.

## The judgment stays with you

One honest caveat, because this series does not do false comfort. The whole loop depends on you bringing the judgment, and the research is clear that the tool cannot supply it. The entrepreneurs who did well with the AI advisor were the ones who could tell the specific advice from the generic advice. The ones who could not, got worse. [^2] The tool widened the field for everyone. It did not choose for anyone.

That is why the loop is built the way it is. Generate, weigh, stress-test, and decide. The tool does the first three as a partner, and you do the last one alone. If you hand the decision to the tool, you are back to taking the generic suggestion, the one that sounds reasonable and fixes nothing. If you keep the decision, the tool becomes a genuinely useful thinking partner, one that has seen more failure modes and more options than you have, and that is exactly what you want it for.

There is also a limit to how much stress-testing helps. A premortem surfaces risks, and it does not tell you which risks are worth acting on. That is a judgment call about your situation, your team, and your tolerance for risk, and no tool has that context. Use the premortem to find the risks, and use your own sense of the situation to decide which ones matter.

## Try it

This exercise takes about fifteen minutes and builds the loop into your hands. Pick a real decision you are facing at work. It can be small, like how to run a recurring task, or larger, like which of two approaches to take on a project. The size does not matter. The loop does.

Round one, generate. Ask the tool for five genuinely different options, and name an axis they should vary on. Do not judge any of them while they come in. Write down the full list.

Round two, weigh. Ask the tool for the cost, risk, and assumption of each option. Then ask it to make the strongest argument against each one. Read the tradeoffs and pick the option that looks best to you, knowing what it costs.

Round three, stress-test. Run the premortem on your chosen option: "It is [a few months] from now and this failed. List the most likely reasons." Write down the risks, and for each one, decide whether you would fix it, plan around it, or accept it.

Here is the answer to the exercise, what you should notice. The generate round shows you that the first option is rarely the best one, because the field was wider than you thought. The tradeoff round shows you that the option that felt best often has a cost you had not named. And the stress-test round shows you that the plan is stronger after you try to break it, because you found the weaknesses while they were cheap. If one of the three rounds clearly changed your decision, that is the move you personally forget to make, and you have found the lever to pull next time. [^1] [^2] [^3]

## What's next

You now have the loop: generate options, weigh the tradeoffs, and stress-test the plan before you commit. That loop, plus the meeting skills from lesson 15, is how you turn a decision into a good one instead of just a made one. The next lesson asks the question that sits underneath all of this: when should you not use AI at all? It is about knowing when the tool is the wrong tool, when the task needs a human judgment or a human relationship that no model can supply, and when the cost of using AI is higher than the benefit. That is the other half of the skill, and it is just as important as knowing how to use the tool well. For now, hold the habit from today. Generate wide, weigh honestly, stress-test hard, and make the call yourself. That is how you use a generator without letting it decide for you. [^2]
