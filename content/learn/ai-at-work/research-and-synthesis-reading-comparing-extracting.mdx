# Lesson 13: Research and Synthesis: Reading, Comparing, and Extracting the Useful Bit

Most of the work in this series so far has been about output: how to ask, how to judge what comes back, how to edit it, how to review it. This lesson moves one step earlier in the process, to the front of a task. Before you can write a report, make a decision, or brief a team, you have to do the reading. You have three sources on the same question, or one long document with the answer buried in it, or a pile of notes that need to become one clear summary. That is research, and the part where you turn several sources into one useful answer is synthesis.

This is exactly where an AI tool earns its keep, and exactly where it quietly does damage if you let it. The tool reads fast. It will happily read forty pages and hand you four paragraphs. It will compare three vendors and tell you which one to pick. It will extract the number you need from a contract you do not want to reread. That speed is real and it is worth using. The damage happens when you treat the tool's summary as the truth about the sources, instead of as a draft you have to check against them. Lesson 12 gave you the review pass. This lesson puts it at the front of the task, where it belongs.

Here is the whole lesson in one line. For research, you bring your own sources, you keep every claim traceable to one of them, and you specifically ask the tool to surface disagreements instead of smoothing them over. Do those three things and the tool is a genuine research assistant. Skip them and you are importing its guesses into your work.

## The words we are using

Pin the terms down before anything else, because each one names a step with its own failure.

**Research** is the act of gathering information from sources to answer a question or inform a decision. In this lesson the tool does the gathering and the first pass of reading, and you own the question and the final judgment.

**A source** is a specific, named piece of material you can point to: a document, a page, a report, a contract, a dataset, your own notes. The word matters because the whole method rests on being able to say "this claim came from that source." If you cannot point to a source, you do not have research, you have a guess.

**Synthesis** is combining information from more than one source into a single coherent answer, without losing what is actually different between them. It is the hard part. A machine summary of one source is easy. Folding three sources together honestly, keeping their contradictions visible, that is where the skill lives.

**Reading** here means getting the content of a source into a form you can use: a summary, an extraction of specific facts, an answer to a question. It is lossy. Any summary throws information away, and the tool decides what to keep.

**Comparing** means putting sources side by side and identifying where they agree and where they disagree. The failure mode is a tool that makes disagreement disappear by averaging it into a plausible middle.

**Extracting the useful bit** means pulling out the specific fact or claim you actually need, rather than getting back a general summary. It requires you to know what you need before you ask.

## Bring your own sources

The single biggest lever in AI research is giving the tool the actual documents you want it to work from, instead of asking it to produce information from what it remembers. This is not an opinion about one vendor. It is a property of how these models work. The model was trained on a fixed slice of the internet, and it has a knowledge cutoff, a point after which it knows nothing new. It also does not actually know what is true. It predicts what is plausible, which is why it can present invented facts with total confidence. [^2]

When you paste in the real documents, you change what the model is working from. In technical terms this is called grounding or retrieval augmented generation: connecting the model to external, current, domain-specific material so its answers come from that material instead of from its training. [^1] IBM lists the payoff plainly: access to current and domain-specific data, and a lower risk of hallucinations. [^1] When the source is in front of it, the model has something to anchor to. When it is not, it reaches for the most plausible thing it has seen, which may not be your thing at all.

So the habit is: your sources go in the prompt. Paste the page, upload the PDF, drop the report in. Do not ask "what does the industry say about X" and expect a reliable answer about your situation, because the model cannot see your situation. Ask "here are my three sources, here is my question, answer from these." [^1] That one move removes most of the fabrication risk before it starts, because now there is a ground truth in the conversation you can check against.

There is a corollary worth naming. The tool will happily invent a source if you do not give it one. If you ask it to find facts and it returns citations, those citations may point to pages that do not exist. The rule from lesson 9 applies here with extra force: a citation is only trustworthy if it points to a source you actually provided. Any reference to material you did not hand it is unverified until you confirm it yourself. [^2] [^7]

## Reading: a summary is compression, not truth

Once your sources are in, the natural first ask is "summarize these for me." That is a fine start and a real time saver. Just remember what a summary is. It is a compression of the source into fewer words, which means information has been thrown away, and the tool decided what to keep. [^1] It can keep the wrong thing. It can flatten a carefully hedged sentence into a confident claim. It can drop the caveat that changes the meaning.

Here is the test that keeps you safe. For any summary that matters, spot-check it against the source. Pick the claims you would act on, the numbers, the dates, the commitments, and find them in the original. Do not read the whole thing. Read the parts that would hurt you if they were wrong. [^4] This is the same verification from lesson 6, applied to the front of the task, and it is the difference between using a summary and being taken in by one.

A second, cheaper habit multiplies the value of reading. Do not ask for one summary of everything. Ask for the specific things you need. "What are the three renewal dates in this contract?" "What does section 4 say about liability caps?" "List every number in this report and the page each one is on." An extraction beats a summary whenever you know what you are after, because it is easier to verify one precise answer than a whole compressed essay. [^3]

## Comparing: ask for the disagreement

Here is the failure that most people never notice, because it produces output that looks great. You hand the tool three sources that disagree on a number. It returns a clean paragraph that sounds like a consensus. It split the difference, or it picked the more flattering figure, or it merged two different policies into one that neither source stated. The contradiction disappeared, and with it the information that actually mattered to your decision.

This is not the tool being sneaky. It is the tool being normal. Its default is to produce the smoothest, most coherent text, and a coherent text has no visible contradictions. [^2] So you have to force the disagreement out. The prompt that does it is short: "Where do these sources disagree, and what exactly does each one say?" Make comparison a search for conflict, not a search for consensus. When you ask it to find the differences, it finds them, and differences are the part of research that makes a decision possible. [^3]

The other half of comparing is attributing each claim to its source. Ask the tool to say which source each point came from. "For each claim in your summary, tell me which of the three documents it came from." This keeps the synthesis honest and gives you the exact place to look when you verify. [^1] A synthesis where every claim carries its source is a draft you can trust and check. A synthesis with no source tags is a smooth paragraph you have to distrust wholesale.

## Extracting the useful bit: know the decision first

You do not extract by reading. You extract by deciding, before you start, what the useful bit is. This is the lesson 7 brief applied to research. [^6] What decision does this research feed? What facts would change your mind? What shape does the answer need to be? If you cannot name what you are looking for, the tool will hand you a general summary, and a general summary rarely contains the one number you actually needed.

So instead of "tell me about these vendors," you say "I am choosing between Vendor A and Vendor B for a twelve-month contract. Using only these two documents, compare them on total annual cost, data retention terms, and exit penalties, as a table with one row per factor, and flag any point where the two sources disagree." That prompt names the decision, the sources, the factors, the shape, and the disagreement rule. It will come back aimed at your actual choice, and you can verify each row. [^6] [^8]

The phrase "using only these two documents" is doing real work. It tells the model not to pad the answer from its training knowledge, which keeps the output grounded in what you gave it. [^1] Use it whenever you want a synthesis that stays inside your sources.

## Verification: every claim points to a source you gave it

Pull the whole thing together into one rule you can carry: for research, every claim you use points to a source you gave the tool, and you checked the ones that matter against that source. [^4] [^5] That is the research version of human-in-the-loop. The tool reads, compares, and extracts. You confirm that what it extracted is actually in the sources, and you carry the responsibility for what you publish. Lesson 12 made the case that you are the review pass. [^5] Here it means you do not ship a synthesized number until you have seen it in the original.

This is not extra bureaucracy. It is where the speed comes from. The tool collapses forty pages to the four that matter, and you read those four pages to confirm them. You spent two minutes instead of an afternoon, and you are still the one who owns the result.

## Worked example: one decision, three sources

Put the method together in a scene you will recognize. You are a program manager deciding whether to extend a pilot with a software vendor for another year. You have three sources in front of you: the vendor's own pricing and terms page, an independent review from an industry publication, and your team's internal notes from the pilot. That is your source set. You paste all three into the tool, not a description of them, the actual text. [^1]

You start with a clean brief: "Using only these three sources, tell me whether the renewal makes sense. Compare total annual cost, support quality, and the data export terms. Flag anywhere the sources disagree." [^6]

The tool comes back with a confident summary. Renewal is justified. Costs are in line with the market, support has been responsive, and the data export terms are workable. It even volunteers a number you did not give it: "the pilot scored 92 percent satisfaction." That is your first alarm. You did not hand it a satisfaction score, and your internal notes do not contain one. [^2] This is the invented-fact failure mode from lesson 9, arriving quietly inside an otherwise good answer. [^7] You drop it. You only use numbers that are in your sources. [^4]

Then you do the compare pass. You ask the tool the disagreement question: "Where exactly do these sources disagree?" This is where it gets interesting. The vendor's pricing page quotes a per-user monthly price. The independent review quotes a higher figure, and it explains that the headline price excludes the setup and migration fees that show up on the first invoice. The tool's first summary had folded these together into a vague "costs are in line with the market." When you force it, the real story appears: the list price and the all-in cost are different numbers, and that difference is the single most important fact for your budget. The disagreement was the useful bit. [^3]

So you ask for the extraction: "Give me the all-in first-year cost per user according to each source, and quote the line each figure comes from." [^8] Now you have two specific figures, each traceable to a source, and you open the two documents and confirm both quotes. [^4] One summary hid the conflict. One targeted question surfaced it, and the conflict is exactly what you needed to take back to your finance lead.

The tool did the heavy reading. You provided the sources, forced the disagreement out, and verified the two numbers that would have embarrassed you if you had shipped them wrong. That is the whole lesson. [^5]

## Try it

Take a real decision you have on your plate, one where you have at least two sources, a vendor page and a review, two policy documents, your notes and a colleague's. Give it ten minutes.

First, feed the tool your sources, real text, not descriptions, and ask for a plain overview. Write down what it says. Then ask the disagreement question: "Where do these sources disagree?" Note the difference. The first overview probably sounded like consensus. The second one probably shows you the actual conflict, which is usually the fact that matters. [^3]

Then extract one specific number or date you need for the decision, and ask the tool to quote the source line it came from. Open the source and confirm that line exists. [^4]

Here is the answer to the exercise, what you should notice. The plain overview is fast and useful but it hides conflict, so never trust it alone for a decision. The disagreement question is where the real value shows up, because a decision usually rests on a difference, not a similarity. And the verification pass, checking that one quoted line, is what turns the tool's output into research you can stand behind. If you caught the tool inventing a number or citing a source you never gave it, you found exactly the failure this lesson exists to catch. [^2] [^7] The habit to keep is the three-part rhythm: bring your own sources, force out the disagreement, and check the claims that matter.

## What's next

You now have the front end of a task: gather your sources, let the tool read and compare them, force the disagreements into the open, and verify the claims you will use. It is the same human-in-the-loop habit from lesson 12, moved to where the information first comes in, and it sets you up for the next lesson. Once you have gathered and synthesized the raw material, the next stage of working with AI is handling the numbers themselves. The next lesson is about spreadsheets and data: analysis, cleaning, and formulas, how to use a tool to make sense of a dataset, tidy it up, and turn it into the figures that back your decisions. [^1] For now, hold the rhythm from today. Bring your own sources, ask for the disagreement, and never use a number you have not seen in a source you gave it. That is how the tool becomes a research assistant instead of a confident guesser.
