# Lesson 5: Data Safety: What You Can and Cannot Put Into an AI Tool

By now you know the machine predicts the next token and that it can sound confident while being wrong. This lesson is about something different. It is about what happens to the words you type before the tool answers. The short version: assume that anything you paste into an AI tool could be seen by the company that makes it, stored on their servers, and in some cases used to improve the product. Whether that is a real problem for you depends on what you paste and which tool you are using. [^1] [^2]

## Terms you need first

Before the rules, the vocabulary, because these words carry the whole argument.

**Personal data** is any information that can identify a real person, on its own or combined with other facts: a name, an email address, a phone number, a client's company and role, a date of birth. **Sensitive data** is the more protected subset, and in most privacy laws it covers health details, financial records, trade union membership, ethnicity, religion, and sexual orientation. [^3] **Confidential information** is broader and depends on context: anything your employer would not want published, from a pricing sheet to a product roadmap to a client's contract terms. **Anonymization** means stripping out names and identifying details so a person or a deal can no longer be recognized. And a **data processing agreement** is a contract between an organisation and a tool vendor that sets out what the vendor may do with the data it receives. [^4]

## The general rule

Hold onto one sentence: treat anything you type into an AI tool as something the vendor could read and, depending on the tool, use later. [^1]

That is not paranoia. It is the default. A Stanford review of the privacy policies of six leading AI companies found that all six used users' conversations by default to help train their models, and that some kept that information indefinitely. [^1] The researchers put it plainly: if you share sensitive information in a dialogue with a mainstream chatbot, it may be collected and used for training, even if it was in a separate file you uploaded during the conversation. [^1]

Regulators say the same thing in their own way. Australia's privacy regulator, the OAIC, recommends that organisations, as a matter of best practice, do not enter personal information, and particularly sensitive information, into publicly available AI chatbots, because of the privacy risks involved. [^2]

So the rule is not "never use these tools." It is: know what you are handing over, and keep the things that would hurt you or your clients out of the box.

## What is almost always off limits

You can build a practical list straight from the definitions above. Keep these out unless you are using an approved enterprise tool and your company policy explicitly permits them:

- **Customer and client personal data.** Names, emails, addresses, phone numbers, payment details, account numbers. Anything that identifies a person you serve. [^2]
- **Health data.** Symptoms, diagnoses, medications, insurance claims, anything a doctor or a benefits provider would touch. [^1]
- **Financial details.** Bank account numbers, card numbers, tax identifiers, salary figures, contract amounts that are not public.
- **Trade secrets.** Your recipe for your product, your unpublished pricing, your next launch, your negotiation strategy. The thing a competitor would pay for. [^5]
- **Credentials and passwords.** Login details, API keys, security codes. There is no reason for an AI tool to ever see these.
- **Unreleased proprietary information.** Anything not yet public: an upcoming feature, a draft announcement, a planned acquisition, a restructuring.

A useful test: if you would not want this printed on the front page of the trade magazine for your industry, it does not go into a public AI tool.

## What is usually fine

The other side matters too. If the rule were "never paste anything," these tools would be useless, and your company would have approved them for a reason. Most everyday tasks are safe if you keep them clean:

- **General questions** with no identifying information. "How should I structure a follow-up email after a proposal meeting?" carries no risk.
- **Your own drafts that contain no sensitive detail.** A rough outline of a newsletter, a draft agenda, a first pass at an announcement where the names, numbers, and confidential specifics are still placeholders.
- **Anonymized summaries.** The substance of a problem without the identifying details. "A client on the Enterprise plan has been waiting two weeks for a response and is frustrated" is safe where naming the client and their bank is not.

The difference between the safe and unsafe versions is almost never the topic. It is the identifying detail. [^2]

## Consumer tools versus enterprise tools

Not all AI tools treat your data the same way, and this is the single most useful distinction to understand. [^4]

A **consumer tool** is the free or individually paid version anyone can sign up for with a personal account. Your conversations there may be used to improve the model, and you usually have to hunt through settings to change that. [^1]

An **enterprise tool** is what a company buys for its employees, often through a business agreement or an API with a data processing agreement attached. With these, the vendor commonly promises not to train on your data and to treat your inputs as confidential. [^4] The French regulator, CNIL, makes the point that a consumer service can be acceptable for non-confidential uses, but for personal data or sensitive documents you should prefer a deployment with contractual protections and the vendor's ability to reuse your data disabled. [^4]

Two practical consequences follow. First, a tool you signed up for with a personal email is a consumer tool, even if you use it to draft work emails. Second, your company may have already made this decision for you by providing an approved tool, and that approved tool is the one you should use for anything that touches real clients.

## How to check your company's policy

Your employer almost certainly has a written answer to "what can I put in an AI tool?" Find it before you need it. [^3] [^5]

- Look in the employee handbook, the IT policy, the information security pages, or the data protection pages on the intranet.
- Ask your manager or your security, IT, or data protection team which tools are approved and what the rule is for personal data.
- If the policy is silent, ask rather than guess, and default to the conservative choice while you wait.
- Regulators increasingly expect organisations to give staff clear guidance on this, so a company being quiet on the topic is not a reason to treat it as an open question. [^3]

## Worked example: the client email

Let me walk through the decision in a realistic scene. Priya works in account management. She wants an AI assistant to help her draft a reply to a client who has asked why their invoice is late and whether a discount from an earlier conversation still applies.

Her first instinct is to give the tool everything, because the tool writes better with more context:

> Draft a reply to Maria Alvarez of Horizon Logistics, account number 88312, who is upset that our invoice #4521 for $48,500 is a week late. She was promised a 10% discount on the expansion deal in our contract. Our net terms are 30 days. Be apologetic and professional.

That prompt is a data-safety problem in several places. It names a client and her employer, gives an account number, an invoice number, a dollar amount, and a discount term that may be commercially sensitive. If this is a consumer tool and the conversation is used for training, all of that detail has left her company. [^1] [^2]

Now watch what happens when Priya applies the rule. She keeps the structure and the tone she wants, and strips out everything that identifies a person or a deal:

> Draft a reply to a business client who is upset that our invoice is a week late. They were told they had a 10% discount on an expansion deal. Our net terms are 30 days. Be apologetic and professional.

That version still lets the tool do the work. It knows the situation, the tone, and the shape of a good reply. It just does not hand over names, account numbers, invoice numbers, or specific amounts. Priya pastes the finished draft back into her email, fills in the real names and the real numbers herself, and sends it.

That is the whole skill in one scene. The tool's value did not drop. The exposure did. [^2]

## Try it

Take a real email or message you have to write this week, one that involves a client or a customer. First, write the prompt the way you normally would, including every identifying detail. Then rewrite it so that:

1. All names, email addresses, and phone numbers become placeholders like [CLIENT].
2. Account numbers, invoice numbers, and specific dollar amounts become [AMOUNT] or a rounded figure.
3. Anything that is not public about your company, your pricing, or a deal becomes a description of the situation instead of the specific fact.

Now look at the second version and answer honestly: is it still usable for the task? If the answer is yes, you have found a habit you can use every day.

**A sample answer.** The original prompt said "draft a reply to Jane Doe, account 5521, who is disputing our $12,000 invoice." The anonymized version says "draft a reply to a client who is disputing an invoice for a professional-services project that ran over the quoted hours. Our policy is to offer a one-time goodwill credit of up to 10%." The anonymized version is slightly more general, but it still gives the tool everything it needs to write a professional, apologetic draft. You add the name and the exact figure only when you paste the final version into your email.

## What's next

You now know what to keep out of an AI tool and why. The next lesson turns from what you put in to what you get out: how to judge AI output the way you would judge a colleague's work, reading a draft as a fallible product to be checked, not a truth to be trusted. Before you move on, hold the habit from today: before you paste, ask what would happen if a stranger read the text. If the answer makes you uncomfortable, anonymize it, use the approved enterprise tool, or leave it out and draft it yourself.
