# Lesson 28: The One-Page Cheat Sheet: The Essentials on a Single Sheet

Every lesson before this one added a skill you could use with any AI tool. What is still missing is the thing that lets you actually use those skills at the moment you need them. You are three quarters of the way through a real task, the priority shifts, a message lands, and there is no time to re-read a lesson. You need the essentials in a form you can reach for in ten seconds, without stopping your work. That is what a one-page cheat sheet is for, and it is the focus of this lesson.

The core move is simple: take the handful of things this series has taught that matter every day, and put them on a single sheet you keep where you work. This lesson defines what a good one-page sheet is, builds one block by block from the essentials already covered, walks a full worked example, and leaves you with a prompt that drafts your own in any AI tool. It is a capstone in the practical sense, not a new skill. It turns the last twenty-seven lessons into something you can reach for while you are working.

## What a one-page cheat sheet is, and what it is not

A **cheat sheet** is a reference you can absorb in a glance and act on immediately. It is not a summary to read, and it is not a collection of long examples. It is a list of the few things that, if you forgot them, would make your work with an AI tool noticeably worse.

Two rules keep the form honest, and they are the same rule from two angles.

The first is the one-page limit. If a sheet fits on one page, you can see all of it at once, which is what makes it a glance instead of a search. The instant it grows to two or three pages, it becomes something you page through, at which point you are reading again and the whole point is gone. The one-page limit is not a style preference. It is the constraint that forces you to keep only the essentials, and that is the actual value.

The second rule is that a cheat sheet is a cue, not a lesson. Each line exists to remind you of a skill you already know, not to teach it to you cold. You have done the work of learning the brief, the iteration loop, the review pass, the truth check, and the stop lines. The sheet's job is to make sure you do not forget to apply them when the pressure is on. [^1] If a line needs a paragraph of explanation for you to understand it, that line does not belong on the sheet.

Keep those two rules in mind, because they drive every decision in this lesson and they are the difference between a sheet you actually use and a decorative document.

## The five blocks every work cheat sheet needs

Across this series, the day-to-day, never-go-wrong essentials cluster into five blocks. Almost anything you do with an AI tool in a normal workday touches one of them, and a good sheet has a line for each.

### Block 1: the brief, in four words

The single most used product of this series is the brief. Lesson 7 showed that a prompt works far better when you tell the tool who it is being, what you want it to do, what it needs to know, and how you want the answer. The shorthand is four words: **Role, Task, Context, Format**. [^1]

On a cheat sheet this block is the shortest, because the whole lesson fits in the four-word reminder plus one line of example. "You are a [role]. Do [task]. You should know [context]. Give me [format]." That one line plus the reminder is enough to jog the skill back into your hands when you are drafting under time pressure.

### Block 2: the iteration loop, one change at a time

Lesson 8 taught that the first draft is a starting point, not an answer, and that the productive move is usually to ask for a specific change rather than start over. The rule that makes iteration work is to change one variable at a time, so you can see what moved the output. [^2]

The sheet line for this block is a set of four short commands you can type at whatever the tool gave you, each changing one thing: make it shorter, make it plainer, make it warmer, give me two alternatives. If a draft almost works, the fix is a single targeted request, not a rewrite of the whole ask.

### Block 3: the review pass, in two stages

Lesson 12 made the case that a draft you ship is your work, not the tool's, and that review works better split into two passes. First you edit the draft to sound like you, because a tool's default voice is not yours. Then, as a separate pass, you check whether it is safe to ship, because editing and fact-checking are different jobs and doing them together means you skip one. [^3]

The sheet line remembers the split: edit for voice first, then check for truth. Doing them as two distinct passes is the whole skill, and a single reminder line keeps you from merging them back together.

### Block 4: the truth check

Lesson 3 explained hallucinations and taught the habit that catches them: anything the tool states that you did not supply, a number, a date, a name, a claim about someone, is a candidate for being invented, and you verify it before you act on it. [^4] The worst hallucinations are not the obviously wrong ones. They are the confident, plausible ones that match what you wanted to hear.

The sheet line is a question you ask of every consequential output, not a paragraph: what did the tool assert that I did not tell it? If the answer is a fact you need to rely on, you check it before it goes anywhere.

### Block 5: the stop lines

Lesson 17 covered when not to use AI, and Lesson 5 covered data safety. They both reduce to a short list of things you do not paste in and tasks you do not hand over, and both stay current every single day, which is why they belong on the sheet. [^5] [^6]

The sheet line is three short branches. Do not paste in client data, personal data, or anything covered by your organization's data policy. Do not use the tool for a judgment call, a sensitive decision, or something you are accountable for getting exactly right without checking it. And when in doubt about policy, keep it out rather than ask forgiveness. One line can hold all three if it is written tightly.

These five blocks are the spine. Everything else in this series is depth on top of them, valuable when you need it and safely off the sheet when you do not.

## A worked example: the sheet that puts them together

Let me build the sheet in full, one block at a time, so you can see how the five blocks compress into a single page a real person would tape to the side of their monitor or keep as a note they actually open. I will use Dana, the customer-success manager from Lesson 27, because her work is ordinary and most of you share pieces of it. [^7]

Dana answers client questions, summarizes support tickets, and writes weekly updates. Here is her one-page sheet, exactly as it ends up.

> **The one-page AI cheat sheet, Dana's version**
>
> **Pick something real.** Use AI for a task you actually have in front of you today, never a generic question you got curious about. If you cannot name what you will do with the answer, you do not need the answer yet.
>
> **Brief it: Role, Task, Context, Format.** "You are a customer-success writer. Draft a reply to a client asking why the invoice total changed. Context: the change came from a new usage tier. Format: three short paragraphs, no jargon." If you name the role and the context, you do most of the work before the tool does.
>
> **Iterate, one change at a time.** First answer not good enough? Ask it to make it shorter. Then plainer. Then warmer. Then give two alternatives. Change one thing per request so you can see what worked.
>
> **Review in two passes.** Pass one: make it sound like you wrote it. Pass two: check every fact. A number about this client that you did not type is invented until you verify it against your records.
>
> **Stop lines.** No client PII, no contract numbers, nothing our data policy says stays inside. Drafting client replies is fine. Making a client-facing promise on my judgment is mine, not the tool's. When in doubt, leave it out.

That is a full page in Dana's case, and it is genuinely all on one sheet. Every line is a cue pointing back to a lesson she already worked through, not a lesson itself.

![Dana's one-page sheet maps each of the five blocks to a single cue line she can glance at mid-task, from the brief to the stop lines](</diagrams/the-one-page-cheat-sheet-essentials-on-a-single-sheet/2-danas-one-page-sheet.png>)

Notice what the sheet does not contain. It has no instructions for which AI tool to open. It has no long worked prompts. It has none of the theory about how models predict text, which was worth learning in Lesson 2 and is not something you need to re-read mid-task. [^8] The act of forcing all of it onto one page is what made Dana cut to the essentials, and the cutting is the point of the exercise.

Here is what using it looks like in a real moment, so the sheet is not abstract. A client emails Dana asking about a charge she already knows is a new tier. She glances at her sheet, and the brief line is enough: "You are a customer-success writer. Explain this charge. Context: new usage tier. Format: plain, short." The tool returns a draft that is close. The iteration line makes her ask it to cut it in half and drop the jargon, one request at a time, and the draft gets tighter with each pass. Before she sends it, the truth-check question is the muscle memory her sheet trained: did the tool assert a number I did not supply? It did not this time, and she catches that as much as any catch, because the question is now automatic. The send button is hers. The whole cycle took a few minutes and every step came from a line on one page.

## Try it: build your own sheet in fifteen minutes

You now have the structure, so build the piece you own. Before you read the guidance below, set aside fifteen minutes and draft your own one-page sheet. Use the five blocks as the frame, but fill each line with your work, not Dana's. If you are an accountant, your truth check matters more than your iteration line. If you draft proposals, your review pass is the block you lean on. If you are in sales, your stop lines matter most, because the data stakes are highest there.

A concrete way to start: take one real task you do at least once a week, write the brief line for it as if it were already on your sheet, and then ask yourself whether a colleague who knew nothing about this series could follow the line. If they could not, the line is either too long or missing a term, and both are fixable by tightening it toward the four-word brief.

If you would rather have the tool help you draft it, this prompt works in any AI assistant:

> I want to build a one-page AI cheat sheet for my job, the essentials I need to glance at while I actually work. Do not teach me general AI concepts. Base every line on these five blocks, adapted to my real work: (1) the brief, Role, Task, Context, Format; (2) iterate one change at a time; (3) review in two passes, voice first then truth; (4) check anything I did not supply before I rely on it; (5) stop lines for data I do not paste in and tasks I keep manual. Start by asking me for my real work, at most three questions. Then draft the sheet as a short list, one line per block, no prose. Do not invent facts about my job, my tools, or my data policy. End by asking me which lines are wrong so I can fix them.

Then do the part the tool cannot do for you: cut it to one page. The draft the tool returns will be too long, because a tool's default is to give you options and examples. Your job is to enforce the one-page rule by deleting everything that is not a cue. A sheet you spent two minutes trimming is worth more than a long reference you never open. [^7]

## A note on the sheet living where you work

A cheat sheet that lives in a folder you never open is a poem, not a tool. The final step is placement. Put it where you can see it while you work: taped to the monitor, as a pinned note at the top of the app you use most, as a sticky in the file you work out of every day. The whole design assumes you can reach it in seconds, and if it is not reachable, none of the blocks matter.

It is also a living document, not a monument. When you find a line that no longer matches how you work, or a command that consistently gives you a better result, change the line. A cheat sheet that drifts out of date becomes noise you skim past, so keep it honest about what you actually do, the same way Lesson 10 taught you to keep a template honest. [^9] A five-minute refresh every few weeks keeps it accurate, and an accurate sheet is one your eye trusts. The day you stop reading the sheet because you have internalized the blocks is the day it has done its whole job.
