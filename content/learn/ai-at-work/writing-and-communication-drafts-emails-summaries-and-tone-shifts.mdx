# Lesson 11: Writing and Communication: Drafts, Emails, Summaries, and Tone Shifts

Here is the most practical lesson in this series, because it covers the task you actually do all day. Most knowledge work is writing in some form. A 2023 Microsoft study of workplace habits found that professionals spend about a third of their time on email and messaging, and another third on note-taking, documents, and preparing materials. [^2] That leaves shockingly little of the day for anything else, and it is exactly the kind of work AI is built for. Writing is what these tools do best. If you take one thing from this series, take this: use them for the words you produce every day, the drafts, the emails, the summaries, and the tone of all of it.

But there is a catch, and the catch is the whole lesson. AI is a fast first-draft writer, not a finished sender. It produces fluent prose in seconds, and that fluency is seductive. It reads well, so it feels done. It is not done. It is a draft you edit, with facts you verify and a voice you supply. An experimental study cited in Harvard Business Review found that professionals using ChatGPT were more than 50 percent faster at their writing tasks while improving the quality of their work. [^1] That speed is real, and it is the payoff. The skill is knowing what to keep, what to check, and what to change before anything leaves your hands. This lesson builds that skill.

## The words we are using

Let me pin down the terms, because they do most of the work in this lesson.

**A draft** is a working version of a piece of writing, meant to be edited before it is used. The word matters because it sets the expectation. A draft is not a finished message. It is raw material.

**A first draft writer** is the role AI plays when you use it for writing. It turns your facts and instructions into a complete, well-formed first pass in seconds. **Editing** is the role you play: checking the facts, fixing the tone, cutting what is wrong, and making it sound like you. The division is the whole skill. The machine writes the draft. You own the result.

**A summary** is a condensed version of longer material that keeps the points that matter and drops the rest. A good summary is not a shorter copy of the original. It is a different thing: a selection, made by someone who knows what matters. That "someone" is a choice you get to make.

**Tone** (sometimes called register) is the emotional character of writing, the way it feels to read. The same facts can be delivered warmly, firmly, formally, or plainly, and each version lands differently. Tone is not decoration. It is part of the message, because it tells the reader how to take what you wrote.

**A tone shift** is changing that character without changing the facts, rewriting the same content for a different audience or situation. The facts stay identical. The feeling changes. It is one of the most useful moves AI gives you, because it costs seconds instead of the twenty minutes a manual rewrite used to take.

## Why writing is where AI earns its keep

Here is the honest reason this topic comes so early in the series. Language is the model's home turf. A large language model is built to predict the next word, and producing fluent, well-structured prose is the one thing it does more reliably than anything else. [^6] It knows the standard shapes of business writing cold: the polite email, the status update, the meeting summary. Give it a clear brief and it will hand you a competent version of any of them in seconds. That is why the productivity numbers are so dramatic, and why writing is the highest-value place to spend your AI time. [^1]

The danger is hiding in that same fluency. Because the prose is smooth, you stop looking at it, and smooth prose can be wrong in two quiet ways.

The first is a generic voice. The model writes toward the most ordinary version of what you asked, and ordinary tends to be safe, familiar, and forgettable. A 2026 Harvard Business Review piece on leaders and AI makes this point bluntly: as teams leaned harder on AI, their work "began to gravitate toward the most familiar, defensible ideas," polished, well-reasoned, and generic. [^4] If you let the model set the voice, everything you send starts to sound like everything everyone sends.

The second is invented specifics. The draft can add a number, a date, or a commitment you never gave it, because that is what the next word prediction does. [^7] You already met this failure mode in lesson 9, and it applies to writing harder than anywhere else, because a good draft hides a wrong detail in a paragraph that otherwise reads perfectly. [^7]

So the working model for this whole lesson is simple. You bring the facts and the judgment. AI brings the fluent first pass. You check what it wrote against what you know, you fix what is wrong, you adjust the tone, and then it becomes yours. [^1]

## Worked example: one situation, three writing moves

Let me show you the moves on one scene, using the project you know from lessons 9 and 10. The Phoenix rollout slipped, the new delivery date is November 12, and you now have real status to report. Integration testing started Tuesday. It surfaced three blocking defects. Fixes are underway, and two of the three are confirmed to land this week. Those are the facts. Write them down, because they are the only things the model is allowed to use.

Move one, turn your notes into a draft email for the client. You give the model the role, the audience, the facts, and the tone, using the four-part brief from lesson 7. [^9]

> Act as a program manager. Draft an email to our client contact at Phoenix telling them we will make the November 12 delivery date. Include these facts: integration testing started Tuesday, it surfaced three blocking defects, fixes are underway, two of three are confirmed this week. Tone: warm, direct, honest, no jargon. Keep it under 150 words. Do not add any numbers, dates, or promises I did not give you.

Read what comes back against your list of facts. Lesson 9 trained you for this. [^7] The draft will probably sound confident and reassuring, and buried in it there may be a sentence like "this leaves us a comfortable buffer before the November 12 date" or "we expect to complete the remaining fixes early next week." The first is a claim you did not make. The second is a date you did not supply. Both come out, because you did not put them in. What stays is what you actually know. That is move one, and it is the whole job: draft it fast, then strip it back to the truth you supplied.

Move two, write the leadership version of the same situation. Same facts, different audience, different tone. Leadership wants the decision, the date, and the confidence level, with no reassurance and no color. So you say so.

> Rewrite the same update for our leadership team. Tone: brief, factual, no reassurance, no jargon. Format: three short sections with headings: Status, Remaining risk, Delivery date and confidence. Two sentences per section max.

The facts did not change. The register did. That is a tone shift, and the prompt that caused it was just a clear description of the audience and the feeling. [^3]

Move three, the version for the project team. They do not need the delivery date restated. They need to know what is broken and who is fixing it.

> Now rewrite it for the project team. Tone: practical and direct. Lead with the two defects being fixed this week and what the team should watch for. Drop the delivery date framing entirely.

Three audiences, three tones, one set of facts, about a minute of typing. That is the whole lesson working. The model did the drafting. You made the calls: which facts were true, which audience got which tone, and which invented extras to cut. [^1]

## Emails: the highest-volume task

Email is the writing you do most, so it deserves its own habits. The good news is that an email is a small, well-defined thing, which makes it the easiest place to get a good result. The bad news is that it is also the easiest place to send something you should not have sent, because it is short and you send it fast.

A good email prompt gives the model the six things a colleague would need to write the email for you: who the recipient is and what they know, what you want the email to accomplish, the facts to include, what to leave out, the length, and the tone. [^3] That is the lesson 7 brief applied to email, plus the two things emails need most: a statement of what to leave out, and a named tone.

The "what to leave out" line matters more than people expect. If you do not say it, the model will include context the recipient already has, or reassurance they did not ask for, or a polite apology that weakens your point. Saying "do not apologize, do not recap what they already know, no hedging" is as important as listing the facts. [^7]

The tone line is where examples beat adjectives. Anthropic's prompting guidance is explicit about this: examples are one of the most reliable ways to steer output format, tone, and structure. [^3] A tone word like "friendly" gets you a generic idea of friendly. One past email you actually wrote gets you your kind of friendly. The practical trick is to paste a sample of your own writing into the prompt and say "match this tone," because the model copies the pattern far better than it executes the adjective. [^3]

And the rule from lesson 9 applies to every draft, no matter how small. Never send an email that contains a number, a date, or a commitment you did not supply and verify. [^7] The model does not know your team's capacity or your client's expectations. If a draft commits to "early next week," that is a commitment you are about to make by not noticing it. Check every draft for things you did not put in, and cut or verify them before you send. [^6]

Some AI products bundle this into their email tools, with one-click options to shorten, formalize, or draft a reply in your desired tone. [^5] Those buttons are fine. They are the same skill underneath: you name the tone, you bring the facts, and you check the result. The button just saves you the typing.

## Summaries: save the reading, not the thinking

The other giant time sink is reading. Meetings, long email threads, contracts, reports, all of it takes time you do not have, and summarizing is the one task people reach for AI for more than almost anything. It is a good instinct. A summary is exactly what a language model does well: it compresses language into a shorter form. [^5]

But a summary is not a neutral machine output. It is a selection, and selection is judgment. This is where the nuance gets lost, and it is worth one concrete warning from that same Harvard Business Review piece. The author describes a business-unit president who had his team summarize the quarterly data with AI before the meeting. Over time, whatever the AI summary emphasized became the lens for the whole conversation. One quarter it opened with rising supplier costs, the team spent the meeting chasing that, and they nearly missed the real driver, a shift in sales toward deep-discount distributors. [^4] The summary did not lie. It just chose, and the choice steered the thinking.

The fix is to decide what matters before you ask, not after. Write your own one-line read of the situation first, then use the AI summary to test it and to challenge it, not to replace it. [^4] That keeps the judgment yours and the summary in its proper role, which is speed, not authority.

For the practical prompt, give the summary three things: the source material, the length, and the point of view. "Summarize this thread as three bullets on what was decided and one line on what is still open." "Summarize this 40-page proposal in 200 words for a finance reader, with the cost figures and the risks called out." The length line is not fussy. It is what forces the selection, and the selection is where you get value. [^3]

And the lesson 9 rule still applies. A summary can contain a detail that is not in the original, because the model filled a gap with a plausible next word. [^7] When a summary makes a claim you will act on, check it against the source before you act. Especially numbers. Especially dates. Especially anything going into a decision.

## Tone shifts and the voice that stays yours

Tone is the most controllable lever in all of this, and the most personal. The same facts can be warm, firm, formal, or plain, and you can move between them in one prompt. That power is worth using deliberately, because it is also the place where letting AI run free does the most damage.

Here is the trap. If you never name a tone, the model picks the most ordinary one, and over time your outgoing writing converges on that same safe, neutral register. Everything starts to sound like everything else. [^4] The HBR authors put the solution in a phrase worth keeping: for communications, AI helps with the structure and you author the meaning. [^4] The structure is drafting, summarizing, and formatting, which the machine does well and fast. The meaning is what you want to say, who you are saying it to, and how it should land, which is yours and should stay yours.

Two habits make this concrete. First, name the tone every time you ask for writing. One line, "warm and honest," "brief and factual," "firm but fair," gives the model a target it cannot infer on its own. [^3] Second, keep a small file of your own good writing, a couple of emails and updates you are proud of, and paste one in when the voice matters. "Match this tone" beats any description, because it shows the pattern instead of naming it. [^3] Your voice is the one thing the model cannot invent for you, and it is the thing your readers actually recognize. Protect it by being the editor of everything that goes out under your name. [^4]

## Try it

This exercise takes about fifteen minutes and it will change how you write emails. Take a real email you need to send this week. Not a hypothetical one. The one that is actually on your list.

Round one, list the facts. Before you open any tool, write down the facts you know are true: names, dates, numbers, commitments. This list is your contract, and it is the thing you will check the draft against.

Round two, get the draft. Prompt the tool with the six-part email brief: recipient, purpose, facts to include, what to leave out, length, and tone. Use the format from the worked example. [^9]

Round three, shift the tone twice. Run the same brief with a warmer tone, then with a more formal one, and compare. You will see the facts stay the same and the feeling change, and you will learn how much of the message lives in the register.

Round four, edit. Check the drafts against your fact list. Cut any number, date, or commitment you did not put in. Adjust a sentence or two so it sounds like you. Then send the version you would actually say out loud.

Here is the answer to the exercise, what you should notice. The drafting is the fast part and the editing is the valuable part, and the editing is not slower than writing from scratch, it is faster, because the hard work of finding the right structure is done. [^1] If a draft felt too generic, the fix was almost never a better adjective. It was a sample of your own writing in the prompt, so the model could copy your actual voice instead of its idea of a voice. [^3] And if you caught yourself about to send a number you never supplied, you just did the most important check in this entire series. [^7]

## What's next

You now have the skills that make up most of your AI use at work: drafting, emailing, summarizing, and shifting tone on demand. Lesson 10 taught you to lock a good prompt into a reusable template, and you have already seen how naturally a template fits here, the weekly client update you run every Friday is a template with the facts swapped in each week. [^8] The next lesson keeps the editing thread and turns it into a system. It is about editing and reviewing AI's writing the way you would a colleague's work, a human-in-the-loop process of checking facts, tone, and what got left out before anything ships. Where today taught you the draft, the next lesson teaches you the review. For now, hold the habit from today. When the tool writes, you edit. Bring the facts, name the tone, check what you did not supply, and keep your voice. That is how you get the 50 percent and keep the person. [^1] [^4]
