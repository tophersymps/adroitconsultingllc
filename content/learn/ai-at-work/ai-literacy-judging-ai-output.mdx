# Lesson 6: AI Literacy: Judging AI Output the Way You'd Judge a Colleague's Work

By now this series has shown you what the machine is, how it produces language, that it occasionally invents facts, and where it is genuinely useful. This lesson is the skill that ties all of that together. It is the difference between someone who uses AI and someone who gets burned by AI, and it is a skill you already have in a different costume. You use it every time a colleague hands you a draft.

Think about what you do in that moment. You do not assume the draft is correct just because a competent person wrote it. You also do not throw it out because it is not final. You read it as what it is, a first pass, and you check the parts that matter before it goes anywhere. That is the entire lesson. The only change is that the colleague is now a tool, and it is faster, more confident, and more likely to be wrong in ways that sound exactly right.

## First, the words we are using

Before anything else, define the terms so we are speaking the same language.

**AI literacy** is the ability to evaluate AI output critically and use it responsibly rather than taking it at face value. Stanford's Teaching Commons describes it as a framework of skills and knowledge for independently navigating the opportunities and challenges of generative AI. [^1] In practice, it is the habit of treating everything the tool produces as a claim to be checked, not a fact to be copied.

**A claim** is any statement that could be true or false. "Our renewal rate is 94 percent" is a claim. Once you hear the word claim, you switch into check mode, because claims are exactly the things that can be wrong.

**Confidence** is how sure the output sounds. It has nothing to do with how true the output is, and this distinction matters more than any other idea in this lesson.

**Verification** is the act of checking a claim against something you trust, usually a primary source you can see and date.

**Lateral reading** is a specific fact-checking technique: you leave the AI output and open other tabs to find independent sources that either confirm or contradict the claim, instead of judging the claim by how convincing it looks. [^3] The name comes from moving sideways, away from the single page, rather than scrolling straight down it.

Keep those five words. They are the whole toolkit.

## The core shift: draft from a fallible colleague

The single most useful mental model for AI output is this: the tool is a helpful but fallible colleague who writes fast, sounds certain, and has no idea what is true. You would never forward a colleague's draft to your manager without reading it first. You would never take a number from a colleague's memo and put it in a client report without checking the source. The tool earns the same treatment.

Most people miss this because of how the tool talks. It does not say "here is a draft I am unsure about." It says "the market is shifting toward X," in the same even, complete voice it uses for everything. There is no wobble, no hedge, no tell that separates a fact it is sure of from a guess it is less sure of. The voice is flat and confident across the whole answer, which is precisely why you cannot judge the output by how it sounds. You have to judge it by what it claims and whether that claim checks out.

Here is the shift in one line: the tool's answer is not information, it is a draft of information. Your job is to sort it. Some you will use as is, some you will fix, some you will throw away. Doing that sorting is AI literacy.

## Four checks before you trust anything

When a colleague hands you work, you run a few quick checks without even naming them. Here are the four that matter most with AI output.

**Does it answer the actual question?** Start here, because it fails more often than people realize. Ask a complex question and the tool will sometimes answer a nearby, easier version of it, or produce a plausible answer to the question it guessed you meant. Read the output against your exact question, not against the question you now think you asked. If the answer is about something adjacent, that is not a fact error, it is a misfire, and it means you are about to act on the wrong thing.

**Is it specific or vague?** Specific claims can be checked. "Customer satisfaction improved after the rollout" is vague and nearly impossible to verify. "Customer satisfaction rose from 71 to 84 percent between March and June" is specific and checkable, and also, conveniently, exactly the kind of thing the tool might invent. Specificity is a gift because it gives you something to test. Vagueness is a warning, not because the tool is hiding something, but because a vague claim cannot be caught when it is wrong.

**Does it cite or hedge?** A good answer tells you where a figure came from, or flags that it is estimating. A bad answer states everything with the same certainty and lets you assume the rest. When the tool attaches a source, treat that source with the same suspicion as the claim itself. When it does not, ask for one. UMD Libraries makes this point directly: with AI output you often have few identifying details to work with, so instead of asking who is behind the information, you have to ask who can confirm it. [^3]

**Does it match what you already know?** You are not starting from zero. You know your company, your customer, your project, and your field. If the tool tells you something that contradicts what you already know to be true, that is a problem, not a prompt to update your beliefs. The tool has no context on your world, so when it disagrees with a fact you are sure of, bet on the fact you are sure of and go find out why they disagree.

These four checks take seconds once they become habit, and they catch the majority of dangerous output before it does damage.

## The confidence trap

Here is the idea that separates people who get caught from people who do not. An AI tool states things confidently even when they are completely wrong, so confidence is not evidence. It is not a sign of truth. It is just how the tool talks.

IBM's explanation of AI hallucinations makes the mechanism clear. A large language model presents fake facts, invented studies, nonexistent URLs, and incorrect details about real things as if they were factual output. [^2] Notice what is in that list: invented studies and nonexistent URLs. The tool is not just rounding a number here and there. It can generate an entire citation to a paper that does not exist, with a real-looking author and journal, and present it without a flicker of doubt. It does this because it is predicting text, not checking truth, and the most plausible-sounding text is confident text.

So when the tool sounds certain, treat that as the default, not as a reassurance. Certainty tells you nothing. The only things that tell you anything are the specific claims and whether they hold up under a check. If you catch yourself thinking "it sounded so sure, it must be right," you are in the trap.

## Verifying facts and numbers

When a claim matters, check it against a primary source. A primary source is the thing itself, the official report, the government page, the company's own announcement, the document you are actually summarizing. Not a secondhand summary of it, and not another AI tool. If the tool tells you a statistic, go find the report that contains it and read the number there.

Lateral reading is built for exactly this. [^3] Instead of judging the claim by how much it sounds like the truth, you open a new tab and look for independent confirmation. If the tool says a regulation changed, you do not read the answer twice and nod. You search for the regulation and find the source that either confirms the change or reveals that no such change happened. You break the answer into its individual claims, check each one separately, and keep only the ones that survive.

This connects to the earlier lesson's split between draftable and decidable work. [^4] For work with a right answer, a number, a fact, a policy, a deadline, the tool's draft is a starting point, never the final word. You verify the decidable parts against a source you can see, and you keep the draftable parts, the wording and structure, that you are comfortable leaving as the tool wrote them.

## Spotting invented references

A close cousin of the invented fact is the invented reference. The tool will happily produce a citation that looks scholarly, complete with authors, a journal, a year, and a page range, and that points to nothing that exists. This has been documented since the early days of these tools. Macquarie University's TECHE ran an experiment in 2023 where ChatGPT produced a reference list for a prompt and included entries that turned out not to exist, authors and journal details assembled from plausible components. [^5]

The habit that protects you costs fifteen seconds. When a citation matters, search for it. If you cannot find the thing, that is your answer, it does not exist. If you do find it, open it and confirm the tool summarized it correctly, because a real source attached to a wrong summary is its own failure mode. [^3] Never forward a reference from AI output without confirming it is real and says what the tool said it says.

## Building a habit of quick verification

None of this needs to be slow. The skill is not "do a full research project on everything the AI says." It is knowing which claims are cheap to check, which are expensive if they are wrong, and spending your effort where being wrong costs the most.

A fast default looks like this. Read the output once against your actual question. Skim for the specific claims, the numbers, names, dates, and references. Instantly spot-check anything that would embarrass you if it was wrong. If it is a number, find the source. If it is a reference, search for it. If it is a fact about your own company or customer, hold it against what you already know. The whole pass takes a minute for a typical answer, and it converts the tool from a liability into a time-saver.

## Worked example: the company stat

Put it together with a scene you will recognize. You are preparing a short update for a stakeholder, and you want to lead with a quick company stat. You do not remember the exact figure, so you ask an AI assistant: "What is our company's customer renewal rate, and how does it compare to last year?"

The tool answers in a confident, complete paragraph: "Our customer renewal rate is currently 94 percent, up from 91 percent last year, driven by the expanded onboarding program."

Read that against the four checks. It answers the question, good. It is specific, good, it gave you real numbers. But now the critical part. This is a claim about your own company, and the tool has no access to your company's records. It cannot know your renewal rate. It produced those numbers the same way it produces everything, by predicting what a plausible answer looks like, and a plausible answer about a healthy business has a rising renewal rate. The confidence is exactly what you would expect from a hallucination, a specific, confident, completely fabricated figure. [^2]

So you do not write it down. You open the finance dashboard or the quarterly report, your primary source, and you read the actual number. Whatever it is, that is the number you use. You keep the sentence the tool drafted, because the wording is good, and you drop in the verified figure. That is the whole skill in one example: trust the draft for the language, verify the claim, and only ship what survives the check.

## Try it

Here is a five-minute exercise that turns this lesson into muscle memory. Open any AI assistant you use and ask it for a specific, checkable fact about a well-known public thing. A good one is a statistic about your industry, a company's reported revenue for a named year, or a summary of a named regulation. Ask for at least one specific number and, if possible, ask it to name a source.

Write down what it gives you. Then run the checks: read it against your question, decide whether it is specific or vague, note whether it named a source, and compare it with anything you already know.

Now verify. If it gave you a number, find the source and compare. If it named a reference, search for that reference and confirm it exists and says what the tool claimed. If it could not give a source, ask directly: "What is the exact source for that figure, and where can I check it?" Watch what happens when you push for a source.

Here is what to look for, which is the answer to the exercise. In many runs, the tool states the number confidently, then either cites a source that does not match, or hedges the moment you ask where the figure came from, or produces a reference that does not exist. That sequence, confident number, shaky or missing source, is the confidence trap showing itself in your own hands. [^2] [^5] If your run came back clean and you found the source and the number matched, that is good too. Either way, the confidence in the first answer told you nothing, and the verification told you everything.

## What's next

You now have the core literacy skill, the habit of reading AI output as a draft from a fallible colleague and checking the claims that matter. That one shift, plus the four checks and a working sense of the confidence trap, is the foundation for every other skill in this series. Lesson 7 takes you one step earlier: how to write a prompt that gets a better first draft in the first place, by giving the tool the role, the task, the context, and the format it needs. Good prompting and good judging are two halves of the same skill, prompting reduces how much you have to fix, and judging catches what still needs fixing. For now, hold the habit from today. When the tool sounds certain, that is normal. When a claim matters, check it. And when in doubt about a number, a fact, or a reference, treat the answer as a draft and find the source.
