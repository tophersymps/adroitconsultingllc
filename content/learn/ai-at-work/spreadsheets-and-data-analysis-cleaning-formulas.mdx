# Lesson 14: Spreadsheets and Data: Analysis, Cleaning, and Formulas

Lesson 13 gave you the front end of a task: gather your sources, read and compare them, and pull out the useful bit. This lesson moves into the numbers. Whether your job is finance, operations, marketing, HR, or sales, a spreadsheet shows up in your week somewhere, and most of the time the honest truth is that the sheet is a mess. Names are spelled three ways. Some cells hold numbers and some hold text that looks like numbers. The date column is half text. A total is off by exactly the rows you did not notice. Before any of it can tell you anything, it has to be cleaned.

That is where an AI tool earns its keep, and it is also where the tool most easily tricks you, because a spreadsheet result feels concrete. It is a number on a screen. It is harder to question than a paragraph, and easier to be wrong in a way that nobody catches until the decision is already made. This lesson is about the three things you will actually do with a tool on a spreadsheet, analysis, cleaning, and formulas, and about the one habit that keeps all three honest: you check the tool's numbers against the cells in front of you. [^1]

## The words we are using

Let me pin the terms down before we touch a single cell.

**Cleaning** (data cleaning, data wrangling) is the work of turning raw, messy data into a tidy, consistent table that can be analyzed: fixing inconsistent labels, removing duplicates and blank rows, converting text that should be numbers, and standardizing formats so that "August 3, 2026" and "03/08/2026" mean the same thing to the tool. It is unglamorous and it is most of the job. A commonly cited figure, from a long-running survey of data scientists, puts the time spent on cleaning and organizing data at roughly 80 percent of a data project, with the collection itself taking up much of the rest. [^2] You probably are not a data scientist, but the ratio feels true in any spreadsheet you have ever wrestled with.

**Analysis** is using the cleaned data to answer a question: what is the total, what is the average, which product sells best, is there a trend over time, where are the outliers. Analysis turns cells into a decision.

**A formula** is an instruction the spreadsheet executes to compute a value, like summing a column, counting how many rows meet a condition, or averaging a filtered group. Formulas are how you tell the spreadsheet what math to do, and they are the part most people either know or dread. [^3]

**A spreadsheet tool** in this lesson means the whole family of them, Excel, Google Sheets, Numbers, or the internal tool your company uses. I will name one or two concrete features as examples, but everything here transfers to any of them, because the underlying habit, give it your real data and check its work, does not depend on the brand. [^1]

## Why cleaning comes first

The single most important idea in this lesson is that you do not clean data as a chore you endure before the real work. You clean data because the analysis is only as good as the table it runs on. Feed a tool a messy sheet and it will happily produce a wrong total with total confidence, because the mess is invisible to it. Microsoft's own guidance makes the point directly: its AI analysis feature works best with clean, tabular data, meaning a single row of unique headers, no merged cells, and numbers stored as numbers. [^1] The tool is not being picky. It is telling you the truth about how these systems behave, garbage in, garbage out, except the garbage comes back looking polished.

Here is the failure to watch for. In a spreadsheet, a column of numbers can look clean and still be lying. Suppose a "sales amount" column holds real numbers in most rows, but a few rows hold text, maybe a currency symbol got pasted in, or an entry reads "1,234" with a comma and a space. When the tool sums that column, it silently skips the text rows, or counts them as zero, and reports a total that looks complete. The number is specific. It is wrong, and nothing about its surface says so. This is the invented-fact failure mode from lesson 9, arriving in a different costume. [^4] The fix is not to trust the tool's summary. The fix is to clean the data first and then to spot-check the result against the cells.

## Cleaning: what to look for

You do not need to be a spreadsheet expert to clean data well. You need to know the handful of things that go wrong, and you need the tool to do the repetitive fixing while you do the judgment. Here is the short checklist, and each item is something you can ask the tool to do.

First, consistent labels. If the same thing appears as "North," "NORTH," and "north," or "Acme Corp" and "Acme Corporation," any grouping you run will treat them as three different things. Ask the tool to standardize a column: "make every entry in the Region column use the same capitalization and the same name for the same region." Second, blank rows and duplicates. A blank row or a duplicated row quietly inflates or skews counts. Ask it to find and report them before removing anything, so you see what it thinks is a duplicate before it deletes it. Third, text that should be numbers. Ask it to convert a column to real numbers and to flag any cell it could not convert, because those are exactly the rows that would have been dropped from a sum. Fourth, dates. Ask it to make every date a real date in one format. Microsoft's guidance flags this one explicitly: dates stored as text, like "2017-01-01," get analyzed as text and can break trend and time analysis. [^1]

The rule that runs through all four: you ask the tool to clean, but you look at what it changed and you keep the changes visible. Do not let it silently rewrite your sheet. Have it report the fixes, review them, and only then accept them.

## Formulas: let it write, you verify

Most of us are not formula experts, and we do not need to be. An AI tool is genuinely good at translating a plain-English request into the formula that does it. You can say "give me a formula that totals the Sales column for rows where Region is North" and get back a working SUMIF, or "average the Amount column, ignoring any rows with no amount" and get a working AVERAGE with the right arguments. That is a real time saver, and it is the part of this lesson you will use most days. [^3]

Two cautions. First, read the formula it gives you before you paste it. You do not need to write formulas, but you should be able to read a simple one, to see which columns and rows it references, because a formula that points at the wrong column is worse than no formula, it is a confident wrong answer. Second, run the formula and check the result against what you can see. Does the total look about right for the numbers in front of you? If you expect roughly fifty thousand and the formula returns five million, something is wrong, and the wrong thing is probably that it summed a column of text as if the numbers were one cell, or referenced the wrong range. [^4]

The tools that generate formulas do not know your data. They know spreadsheet syntax. So the division of labor is clean: the tool supplies the syntax, you supply the judgment about whether the number makes sense for your actual rows. Keep that division and you can use formula generation all day without importing the tool's errors.

## Analysis: ask the question, check the answer

Once the data is clean, you can ask the tool to analyze it, and this is where it earns the most. Instead of hunting through filters and pivot menus, you ask questions in plain language: "what is total revenue by region," "which product had the biggest month-over-month jump," "show me the trend in new signups by quarter." Excel's Analyze Data feature is built around exactly this: you ask a question in natural language and it returns a summary, a chart, or a pivot table, no formula writing required. [^1] The same pattern exists in the other major tools in one form or another.

The habit from every previous lesson applies here with extra force. The tool's analysis is a draft, not the truth about your data. When it gives you a number you will act on, trace it. Open the rows it is describing and add them yourself, or at least spot-check the biggest contributors, because a claim you will act on deserves the same verification you gave the sources in lesson 13. [^5] The tool will also surface patterns you did not ask about, and some of those are real and some are statistical noise. Treat a surprising pattern as a lead to investigate, never as a conclusion to present. [^4]

## Worked example: one messy sheet

Put the whole method together in a scene you will recognize. You run a small regional sales team and you have a spreadsheet of last quarter's orders. It is the way these sheets actually arrive: Region is spelled "North," "north," and "N"; the Amount column has real numbers in most cells but a few entries came in as text with a dollar sign; two orders are duplicated because someone re-pasted a row; and the Date column mixes "2026-05-14" with "May 14, 2026." You want one number: total sales by region, so you can brief the leadership meeting.

Your first instinct is to ask the tool directly: "total sales by region." Watch what happens. The tool splits Region into three groups it thinks are different, drops the text amounts from the total without telling you, counts the duplicates twice, and hands you a neat table. Every number is specific. Two of them are wrong. [^4]

So you run the method. First, clean. You ask the tool to standardize the Region column, convert Amount to real numbers and flag any cell it cannot convert, remove duplicate rows and report how many it found, and make every Date a real date. It comes back and you review the report before accepting anything. The two text amounts get fixed or flagged. The duplicates are named. Now the table is trustworthy in a way it was not. [^1]

Second, write the formula. You ask for the formula that totals Amount by Region. The tool hands you a SUMIF. You read it, you see which column it sums and which range it matches, and you confirm both are right. Then you run it. [^3]

Third, verify, and this is the step that saves you. Before you brief anyone, you add up the North column by hand, or at least the five biggest North rows. The tool's North total matches. Then you check the total across all regions against the raw sheet, and it matches too, because you cleaned it first. The number you walk into the meeting with is real, because you saw it in the cells, not just in the tool's summary. [^5]

That is the whole lesson in one scene. The tool cleaned the messy sheet, wrote the formula you did not want to write, and produced the total. You looked at what it changed, read the formula, and checked the number against the rows. Speed came from the tool. Truth came from you checking it. [^4]

## Try it

Take a spreadsheet you actually use, even a small one, and give this fifteen minutes. It works best if the sheet has at least one text column and one numeric column, which almost all of them do.

First, before touching anything, write down one question you want the sheet to answer, "total by region," "average deal size by month," "which rep has the most open items." Second, ask the tool to clean the data and report what it changes: standardized labels, converted amounts, removed duplicates, fixed dates. Read its report and question anything that looks off. Third, ask it for the formula that answers your question, read the formula to confirm the right columns, and run it. Fourth, verify the result by adding up a subset by hand, the biggest contributors or one full group, and confirm the tool's number matches.

Here is the answer to the exercise, what you should notice. The cleaning step probably changed more than you expected, which is exactly why it comes first. The formula step probably saved you the most time, because the tool remembers the syntax you never could. And the verification step is the one that keeps the whole thing honest, because it is where you catch the text-amount rows and the duplicates that would have corrupted your total. If the tool's number did not match your hand-check, you found the whole point of this lesson. That mismatch is not a tool failure to be annoyed by. It is the check working. [^4] [^5]

## What's next

You can now take a messy spreadsheet, clean it, write the formula, and verify the number before it reaches a decision. That is the numerical half of day-to-day AI work, and it pairs with everything before it: bring your real data, ask in plain language, and check the result against the source. The next lesson moves from numbers back to people. It is about meetings: agendas, transcripts, action items, and follow-ups, how to use a tool to prepare for a meeting, capture what was actually said, turn the conversation into decisions and owners, and make sure the follow-up actually happens. [^3] For now, hold the habit from today: clean before you analyze, let the tool write the formula, and never brief a number you have not seen in the cells. That is how a spreadsheet becomes evidence instead of a guess.
