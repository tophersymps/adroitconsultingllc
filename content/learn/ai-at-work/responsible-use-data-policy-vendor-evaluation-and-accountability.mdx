# Lesson 25: Responsible Use: Data Policy, Vendor Evaluation, and Accountability

The last few lessons built the machinery of a working AI practice. Lesson 22 gave you a shared prompt library. Lesson 23 added a quality bar and a review. Lesson 24 showed you how to measure whether the whole thing is earning its place. That machinery serves a person, or at most a small team. This lesson is the step up, where the same good habits become something an organization depends on and therefore has to govern.

Here is the difference you have to sit with before anything else. When you used AI alone, the risk was yours. What you pasted in, what you trusted, whether you checked the output: those were personal calls, and a mistake cost you. The moment a company standardizes on a tool and builds real workflows around it, those same personal calls become organizational ones. More people use it. The data it touches is shared. Somebody chose the tool, and somebody has to answer when it produces a bad outcome. That is not bureaucracy arriving to spoil a good thing. It is the honest price of relying on the tool for work that involves clients, contracts, and other people's data.

Responsible use at this scale rests on three things, and they are the three parts of this lesson: a **data policy** that says what is allowed into the tool, **vendor evaluation** that says how you choose the tool in the first place, and **accountability** that says who answers when something goes wrong.

## Why good individual habits are not enough

You might read that and think you already did the first part. Lesson 5 taught you what to keep out of an AI tool: no client personal data in a consumer chatbot, anonymize before you paste, use the approved enterprise tool for real work. Those habits are the foundation, and they are genuinely good. But they were aimed at a single person protecting their own work. [^1]

A policy is what happens when those habits have to be true for forty people who do not all share your caution, your judgment, or your memory of the training. The habits travel with you. The policy has to travel with the team, which means it has to be written down, and it has to be specific enough that a reasonable person can follow it without a lawyer. That is the first lesson of governance: the individual skill scales only if you convert it into a rule other people can apply without you in the room.

There is a second reason the personal version stops being enough, and it is the part people miss. Once a team leans on a tool for real work, the tool stops being a personal choice and starts being an organizational purchase, whether or not anyone formally bought it. People found it on their own, signed up with a work email, and started pasting real client work into it because it helped them. That is how a company ends up depending on tools nobody evaluated and data nobody vetted. The fix is not to scold the people who did it. The fix is to give them a better, approved path and to make the rules clear. [^2]

## A data policy says what is allowed in

A **data policy** is the written rule for what your organization permits people to put into an AI tool and under what conditions. Think of it as Lesson 5, upgraded from a personal habit to a shared standard. It answers a handful of questions in plain terms, and if you have ever wondered what your own employer allows, you already know why the answers matter. [^1]

A workable policy covers a few specific things. Which tools are approved, because "use AI" is not an instruction a person can follow, but "use this approved tool for client work, this one for general questions" is. What categories of data are off limits entirely: client personal data, health and financial details, credentials, unreleased plans. What must happen before anything sensitive goes in: anonymize it, or use the approved tool that treats inputs as confidential under a data processing agreement. And who to ask when the rule does not cover a situation, because there will always be a gray case and the policy should point at a person, not leave you guessing.

You do not need a policy document with legal weight to start. You need a short, written answer to the question your whole team faces daily: what can I put in, and what stays out. If your organization already has one, your job is to read it and follow it. If it does not, a short one you write and get approved is worth more than an unwritten understanding, because people follow rules they can see. [^1]

## Vendor evaluation is how you choose the tool

A **vendor** is the company that makes and runs the tool, and **vendor evaluation**, sometimes called due diligence, is the process of asking that company the questions you need answered before you trust it with your team's work. Most people never do this. They pick a tool because a colleague mentioned it or because it is the famous name, and they only discover the problems later, when the tool turns out to train on their data, or keep it longer than they expected, or have no clear answer for what happens when they leave.

The questions worth asking fall into a short set, and they are the same whether you are evaluating a full AI product or deciding whether to let your team keep using the free tools they found on their own. [^4]

**Is our data used to train the model, and can we opt out?** This is the first question, because it is the one with the biggest consequence. Many vendors use customer data to improve their models by default. The answer you want is not a vague "we take privacy seriously." It is a specific commitment, ideally in the contract, that your data will not be used to train the model, and a clear statement of what happens to the data you put in. [^1] [^4]

**Where is our data stored, and how long is it kept?** Data residency and retention are not abstract concerns. They determine whether the tool even complies with your company's obligations and the laws it operates under. If the answer is vague about where the data sits or how long it is kept, that is a reason to pause, not a detail to wave through. [^4]

**Can we test it on our own data before we commit?** A tool that performs well on generic demos may fail on your actual work. A vendor that resists letting you try it on real, appropriately cleaned data may be hiding a performance gap. A trial period on your own material is the most reliable test there is. [^4]

**What security certifications does the vendor hold, and who can access our data?** Look for independent security certification such as SOC 2, and ask for the audit report rather than just the badge. Ask which parts of the vendor's staff can see your data, including support and any subcontractors. [^4]

**How often is the model updated, and what happens when it changes or is retired?** AI models change behavior when they are updated, sometimes without warning, and they reach end of life faster than traditional software. You want advance notice of updates and a real answer for what happens when the version you rely on is deprecated. [^4]

For most people reading this lesson, the evaluation happened once, at adoption, or it happened for you by your employer when it picked an approved tool. That is fine. You do not need to re-litigate every vendor decision. You need to know the questions exist, so you can ask them the next time someone proposes bringing in a new tool, and so you can tell a good answer from a hollow one. When you evaluate, ask the questions in writing, and treat the vendor's written answers as part of the deal. Verbal assurances are hard to enforce later. [^4]

One more note on how evaluation interacts with the earlier lesson. Lesson 5 pointed you to the distinction between consumer tools and enterprise tools, where an enterprise agreement often comes with a commitment not to train on your data. Vendor evaluation is how you check that the promise is real. A vendor that will not put its data promises in writing has effectively told you the answer. [^1]

## Accountability is who answers when something goes wrong

**Accountability** is the clearest answer to the question "if this causes a problem, whose job is it to deal with it?" It is not the same as blame. The international guidance on responsible AI draws a careful line between three related words. **Responsibility** is the sense of duty that leads a person to act well. **Liability** is the legal exposure that follows from harm. **Accountability** sits in the middle: the expectation that a person or organization will ensure a system works properly, will be able to explain the decisions behind it, and will act to make things better when they do not. [^3]

That framing matters because it tells you what a company actually has to build. Not a guilt list for when things break. A structure: someone is named as owning each workflow, someone can explain how a decision was made, and there is a path for fixing what goes wrong and learning from it. The organizations that design accountability seriously do not leave it vague. They document who owns each system, they assign responsibility for the risks, and they make the escalation route known. [^2] [^3]

For you, in your day-to-day work, accountability has a concrete shape, and it connects to a lesson you already learned. In Lesson 17 you saw that when a task needs your judgment, your context, or your standing with a client, you stay answerable for the result even when a tool did the work. Accountability is that same truth, now applied at the level of a whole workflow. [^5]

So the practical question for any AI-assisted task is not "did a human check this?" It is "which human was accountable for the outcome, and could that person explain the decision if asked?" A draft the tool produced and a person reviewed is an accountable process, because there is a person who can stand behind the result. A draft the tool produced and nobody looked at is not, because there is no one to explain it or correct it. When a tool gets something wrong, the failure is not that the tool was wrong. Tools are wrong all the time, and Lesson 3 taught you to expect it. The failure is if nobody was in a position to catch it. That is the accountability gap, and it is where responsible use breaks down.

A useful mental model for the whole lesson: good habits protect one person, a good vendor protects the data, and good accountability protects everyone else. Each one fails if the layer above it is missing, and all three together are what let you rely on the tools without pretending they are infallible.

## A worked example: Imani puts all three in place

Imani is an operations lead at a mid-sized professional-services firm. For the past year her team of twelve has used AI ad hoc. People signed up for free accounts with work email, pasted whatever they needed into whatever tool they liked, and it mostly helped. Then two things happened. A client asked what happens to the data from a draft that went through an AI tool, and Imani could not answer. And she found out three different tools were in use across her team, none of them evaluated, all of them holding client material.

Management told her to fix it and gave her a clear brief: decide on the rules, choose what the team standardizes on, and make sure someone is answerable. She treats it as three jobs, matching this lesson.

**The data policy.** Imani writes down the rules her team can actually follow. She starts from the categories Lesson 5 established. Client personal data, financial details, and anything unreleased go only into the approved enterprise tool, never a free account. Anything pasted into a general tool must be anonymized first. Credentials and passwords never go in anywhere. She lists the approved tool by name, so nobody has to guess what "use AI" means. She puts the one-page policy where everyone works, and she names herself as the person to ask in gray cases. [^1]

**The vendor evaluation.** Imani shortlists the enterprise tool her company already licenses against the tool her team has been using on its own. She sends both the same questions in writing. Does our data train your model, and can we confirm that in the contract? Where is our data stored and how long is it kept? Can we trial it on our own cleaned data? What security certification do you hold, and can we see the report? How do you notify us of model updates? The answers split cleanly. One vendor commits in writing that business data does not train its models and gives her a trial on her own material. The other is warm on the phone but vague on retention and will not put its data promises on paper. Imani picks the one that answered, and she keeps the written answers on file. [^4]

**The accountability.** Imani names a single owner for each workflow the team runs through the tool, including herself for the client-comms workflow that started the whole problem. For each owner she writes one line that answers the question from earlier in this lesson: which human is accountable for the outcome, and could that person explain the decision if asked? She sets a simple rule that every client-facing output is reviewed by its owner before it goes out, which is the accountability structure from Lesson 17 applied to the whole team. [^2] [^5]

The result is not a stack of paperwork. It is one page of rules, one written evaluation that justifies the approved tool, and a named owner per workflow. When the same client asks again what happens to their data, Imani can answer with the vendor's written commitment. When a draft goes out with an error, there is a person whose job it was to catch it, and she can ask what the review missed instead of wondering who to blame. That is the whole difference between using AI responsibly and using it by accident.

## A review habit that stays cheap

You do not need to build this from scratch if your company has already done it. Read the policy, use the approved tool, and know who owns the workflows you touch. The review habit for a whole team comes down to three checks that stay cheap.

**Review the policy when the tools change.** The moment someone proposes a new tool, or a tool your team uses changes its terms, the policy and the evaluation need a fresh look. Tools and their data practices change faster than the policies written about them. [^2]

**Ask the questions before you adopt, not after.** Evaluation is cheap before you are committed and expensive after, when the data is in the tool and the team depends on it. Run the short question set before you standardize, not when a client asks. [^4]

**Keep the accountability answer current.** When someone leaves, or a workflow changes hands, update who owns it. An owner who no longer exists is not accountability, it is a gap dressed up as a structure. [^2]

## Try it

Pick one AI tool your team uses, or that you are considering adopting, and run a short version of the evaluation on it. Answer three questions honestly, in writing. Is our data used to train the model, and is that stated clearly somewhere we can point to? Can we trial it on our own cleaned data before we commit? If it produced a harmful or wrong result, who on your team would be accountable for catching it, and could that person explain the decision?

If you want a second opinion on your evaluation, use this prompt in any tool:

> I am evaluating an AI tool for my team. The tool is [name or describe it], and my team will use it for [describe the task]. I need to decide whether to approve it. Answer these as a skeptical procurement reviewer: what are the specific questions I should ask the vendor about whether my data trains their model, where and how long my data is stored, security certification, and what happens to my data if we cancel? Which answers should count as red flags, and which are acceptable with a written commitment? Also help me write a short data policy for my team that says which categories of data can go into this tool and which must stay out. Keep it plain enough for a non-technical person to follow, and do not invent facts about the tool. Tell me what to verify with the vendor directly.

Read the answer the way this series has taught you. The tool is a useful second pair of eyes with no knowledge of your vendor or your team. Take the structure and the questions you agree with, verify the facts against the vendor's actual documentation, and apply your own judgment about your stakes. [^6]

If your employer already has a data policy and an approved tool, skip straight to the accountability question. Find out who owns the workflows you touch, and if nobody owns one, that is worth raising, because an owned workflow is one that can be explained and fixed, and an unowned one is a risk nobody is watching.

## What's next

You now know what responsible use looks like at the level of a team: a data policy that says what is allowed in, an evaluation that justifies the tool you rely on, and a named owner who is accountable for each workflow. That is the right frame for a single team, and it points at the next, larger question. Once you have proven the approach on one team, how do you run it across a whole company without losing the controls this lesson gave you? The next lesson answers that. Lesson 26, The manager's guide: running a company-wide rollout, is about how you lead adoption across an entire organization rather than a single team: sequencing the rollout so it does not outrun the governance, training people who do not share your context, and keeping the data policy and the accountability structure intact as the number of users and workflows multiplies. It is the natural next step, because the controls in this lesson protect one team, and a rollout is how you protect a thousand people with the same rules instead of a hundred separate versions of them. For now, run the three-question check on one tool your team uses, and write down who is accountable for the workflows you touch. That is a small step, and it is the whole shape of the responsible use this lesson is about.
