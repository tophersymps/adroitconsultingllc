# Lesson 22: Building a Shared Team Playbook and Prompt Library

Imagine for a moment that your whole team is doing what Lesson 19 asked you to do alone. Each person saved their own prompts into their own notes. Each one refined a version of the same status email, the same summary, the same support reply. Nobody shares theirs, or they share them in a chat thread that scrolls away. Ten people, ten slightly different prompts, ten different tones of output, and every one of them rebuilt separately from scratch. [^2]

That is the ordinary state of AI use on most teams. This lesson is about the fix, and the fix is not a tool. It is a habit with two parts: a shared playbook that says how the team uses AI, and a shared prompt library where the good prompts live once, for everyone. Together they turn a pile of private experiments into a common resource the whole team draws on and keeps improving together. The payoff is not more AI. It is consistency, faster ramp-up for new people, and a quality bar the team sets together instead of each person guessing. [^5]

This lesson sits where the series moved to the team. Lessons 18 through 21 gave you the personal skills: spotting the tasks, saving the prompts, running the workflows, and deciding what to automate. [^1] [^2] [^3] Now you take those hard-won prompts and hand them to the team as a shared thing. Everything you already know applies. The only new work is agreeing on the container they all live in.

## The words we are using

Keep the terms plain, because the difference between them is the difference between a library that gets used and a folder that gets ignored.

**A prompt library** is a shared, organized collection of reusable prompts, kept in one place everyone on the team can reach. The key word is shared. A personal library from Lesson 19 is yours. A prompt library is everyone's, which means it needs rules to stay coherent. [^2]

**A prompt entry** is one prompt inside the library, with the extra fields that let someone else use it: a name, a when-to-use line, the prompt itself with placeholders, a what-to-check note, and an example of the output. Without those fields, a shared prompt is just an orphaned wall of text nobody trusts. [^2]

**A team playbook** is the part that is not prompts. It is the written agreement on how the team uses AI at all: which tone the outputs should hold, which kinds of tasks the team does and does not hand to a tool, where data may and may not go, and who checks what before anything leaves. The playbook is the standards. The library is the working examples of those standards. [^4]

**An owner** is the person answerable for a prompt entry staying correct: that its when-to-use is right, that its placeholders still work, and that it gets retired or updated when the task changes. A library with no owners is a library that quietly goes stale. [^3]

**A version** is a numbered change to a prompt entry, so a team can tell who changed what and roll back to the previous one if a change made things worse. This matters more the more people touch a shared prompt, because the failure mode is real: one person tweaks it for their scenario and breaks it for everyone else, and nobody can get the old one back. [^3]

## Why shared beats thirty private versions

The case for a shared library is not a warm feeling about collaboration. It is a measurable waste of effort and a drift in quality.

Start with the waste. If ten people each maintain their own prompt for the same recurring task, the team is paying for the same work ten times, forever. Every improvement one person makes is invisible to the other nine. Every new person who joins rebuilds the wheel from memory or starts cold. A shared library makes a good prompt a one-time cost that the whole team compounds, the same way a personal library compounded for you in Lesson 19. [^2]

Then the drift. When each person asks the same tool to do the same job with their own wording, the outputs come back different. Different length, different tone, different things emphasized. For a customer-facing team that means the company sounds different depending on who answered. The tool is not the cause. The tool does what each person asks. The variation is in the prompts, and the fix is to make the prompts one thing instead of ten. [^5]

The ramp-up is the quieter win. A tested prompt with a one-line note on when to use it gets a new hire producing usable output on day one. Without it, they spend the first weeks relearning what the team already learned. The library is the team's shared memory, which is why it has to be findable and current rather than hoarded. [^5]

## The shape of a good prompt entry

Before the example, the five fields every entry should carry, so you can spot them (or add them) when you build yours. These come straight from why a shared prompt fails when it lacks them: someone else cannot safely use a prompt they do not understand. [^6]

**Name.** A short verb-plus-noun, so the list is scannable. "Draft a discovery follow-up email" beats "email prompt v3 final."

**When to use it.** One sentence on the exact situation. "After a 30-minute discovery call where the prospect named two or more pain points." Without this, people guess whether a prompt fits their case, and mis-apply it.

**The prompt.** The reusable instruction with bracketed placeholders instead of the real details baked in. This is what Lesson 9 taught for handling data: the template stays clean and the real content gets added at run time. [^2] Write the prompt to be clear and specific, name the persona, the task, the constraints, and the output format, because the tool produces what it is asked to produce. [^6]

**What to check.** The judgment note from Lesson 6, written down for this specific prompt: which names and numbers to verify, what a wrong answer looks like, what must come from your own notes and not the tool's imagination. [^4] This keeps the human-in-the-loop check from being improvised every time.

**An example output and an owner.** One sample of what a good result looks like, so everyone has a shared target. Plus the named owner and a version so changes are trackable and rollback-able. [^3]

## A worked example: Marcus moves from personal to shared

Marcus is the customer-support lead from Lessons 19 and 20. He has a personal library with a handful of prompts he trusts: a polite-but-firm reply for frustrated customers, a weekly status update, and an internal summary prompt. [^2] Today he decides the team should stop each person building their own and start sharing one set.

He starts small, with one task every support agent handles: the reply to a frustrated customer. Three of the four agents already have their own version. Two are fine and one is sharp enough to come across as rude. Rather than pick his favorite and dictate it, Marcus holds the team to the practice the playbook calls for: he walks through the good prompts together and agrees on one.

The entry they write looks like this.

**Name:** Draft a reply to a frustrated customer.

**When to use it:** When a customer has escalated, over email, and the issue needs a firm-but-kind response a real person will send.

**Prompt (template):** "You are a senior customer-support specialist writing for [company]. The customer is frustrated about [the issue]. Draft a reply under 120 words that does three things: acknowledges the frustration without over-apologizing, states clearly what I can do and when, and ends with one concrete next step or question. Do not invent facts, policies, or promises. Use a calm, respectful, direct tone. I will review and edit before sending."

**What to check:** The tone is calm and not sycophantic. Every claim matches our actual policy. There is no invented promise or timeline. Read it out loud before sending.

**Example output and owner:** [one short sample], owned by Marcus, version 1.

Marcus writes this into the shared document with a one-line when-to-use note, and everyone starts from it. [^5] When an agent finds it runs long or lands too stiff, they do not edit it in place and send. They add a note in the team channel, and Marcus reviews and bumps the version with the change and the reason. The prompt is a shared, owned thing, not a free-for-all. [^3]

## How to build yours

You do not need a rollout plan or a fancy tool. You need a shared place and a small standing agreement. Five steps, most of it done in one session.

**Step one: pick one shared home.** Choose the place your team already opens daily, where a prompt drops straight into the chat box with no copy-paste tax. A shared doc or a team channel pinned at the top both work. The exact tool matters less than the rule: every team prompt worth keeping lives there, not in private notes or scroll-away chat. [^5]

**Step two: seed it from what already works, not from a blank page.** Take the prompts people already use and trust, starting with the recurring tasks from the Lesson 18 audit. [^1] Two or three solid entries beat a wall of fifty speculative ones. The library is built by harvesting what already proved itself in real work. [^2]

**Step three: agree on the five fields and write them onto every entry.** Name, when to use it, template, what to check, example and owner. Enforce the fields from the start. An entry missing its when-to-use and what-to-check is a prompt nobody else can use safely, however clever it is. [^4] [^6]

**Step four: name owners, and keep it to a handful of people.** Every entry has one named owner. The more people can edit a shared prompt freely, the faster it degrades. Owners review changes, bump versions, and retire entries that stopped working. [^3]

**Step five: set a review cadence, and make it as ordinary as the Monday standup.** The library that never gets looked at becomes a graveyard of outdated instructions, because the tools and the tasks both change. [^6] A monthly pass where each owner confirms their entries still work is enough to keep it alive. Retire more than you add.

## Where it helps, and where it does not

A shared playbook and library earn their place on the tasks the team repeats in a stable shape: the recurring emails, summaries, replies, and formatting jobs that every lesson in this series has pointed you toward. [^1] They are the work where a consistent prompt saves the most and a good one is worth the whole team using. That is their lane.

Do not stretch them into the judgment work. A prompt the whole team uses to draft the standard reply is a good prompt. A shared prompt meant to decide which customer gets a fee waived, or to set the tone of a difficult negotiation, is a trap, because that is where your context and accountability live. [^4] The library stores the drafting; the human stays on the deciding. Lesson 17 made this line explicit and it holds here with extra force, because a shared, trusted prompt makes it easier to stop thinking. [^7]

Three honest risks, so you can head them off. **Gatekeeping:** a handful of people who hold the prompts and treat the library as their thing, which kills adoption. The answer is not to make everyone an editor, it is to make the process for proposing and reviewing new entries open and fast. **Staleness:** prompts left running long after the task changed, which quietly produces wrong output that keeps looking legitimate. The monthly owner review is the cure. [^3] **Scope creep:** a library that grows into an unsearchable dump, which nobody trusts and everybody ignores. Keep it curated. Fewer, owned, working entries beat many orphaned ones.

## Try it

The whole exercise is one meeting and one shared document.

First, do the one-prompt starter. Pick the single recurring task your team does most often through a tool, the one you are most tired of each person rebuilding. Collect the two or three versions people already use. Then, together, write the five fields for one shared entry: the name, the when-to-use line, the template with placeholders, the what-to-check note, and an example plus an owner. Put it in a shared place everyone can reach. That is a real library, even at a size of one.

Second, get a second opinion on the design, using this prompt in any tool with your task list and the prompts you have:

> Here is a list of tasks my team repeats: [your list]. Pick the three that would benefit most from a shared prompt, and for each propose the five fields of a shared entry: a short name, a one-sentence when-to-use, a reusable template with bracketed placeholders, a what-to-check note, and a one-line description of a good example output. Keep the templates clear and specific. Then suggest the two or three people who should own these entries and one review cadence that will actually stick.

Read the answer the way earlier lessons taught you. [^4] The tool is a helpful colleague with good instincts and no knowledge of your actual team, your policies, or who people trust. Its owner and cadence suggestions are a starting point, not a decree. Take the useful structure and apply your judgment about your own people.

## What's next

You now have the team side in place: a shared playbook that says how you use AI, and a shared library of prompts everyone can reuse. That raises the natural next question, and the next lesson answers it. Lesson 23, Human-in-the-loop: quality standards and review, is about how a team keeps a shared quality bar once the prompts are common: what standard everyone checks against, who reviews the output, and how the check itself stops being an afterthought. It is the direct follow-on, because a library only produces consistently good work if the team agrees on what good means and enforces it together. For now, build the one-entry starter. Pick the task, gather the versions, write the five fields, put it somewhere shared, and name an owner. You are not building bureaucracy. You are giving the team's best work a common home, so the good prompt gets reused instead of rebuilt, and the quality bar gets set together instead of guessed. [^2]
