# Lesson 9: Fixing the Failure Modes: Vague Asks, Invented Facts, and Wrong Formats

The last three lessons handed you the toolkit. Lesson 7 gave you the anatomy of a good prompt: role, task, context, format. Lesson 6 gave you the judgment habit: read output as a draft and verify the claims that matter. Lesson 8 gave you the loop: refine, rephrase, ask for alternatives. Here is what none of them named out loud. When the output is still wrong, it is usually failing in one of three specific ways, and each way needs a different fix. This lesson is the diagnostic.

Think of it like a car that will not start. You do not just keep turning the key and hoping. You check the obvious causes one at a time, and you fix the one that is actually broken. AI output works the same way. The three failure modes are vague asks, invented facts, and wrong formats. Name which one you are looking at, and the fix is almost mechanical. Guess wrong, and you will spend four rounds polishing the wrong thing.

That last part is why this lesson comes after the others. Iteration is only useful if you know what to change. Give a vague ask to a tool and then iterate, and you get a more polished version of a vague answer. Iteration sharpens whatever is already there. It does not fix a request that was incomplete, a fact that was fabricated, or a shape that was never specified. [^6] Diagnosis has to come before iteration, and that is the whole skill in this lesson.

## The words we are using

Let me pin the terms down, because each one names a specific breakdown.

**A failure mode** is a recurring way that output goes wrong, with a recognizable symptom and a fix that addresses it. Naming the mode matters because it tells you where to spend your effort.

**A vague ask** is a request that leaves the tool to guess what you want. It misses the role, the task, the context, or the format, so the model falls back on the most generic version of anything you might mean. OpenAI's own guidance says it plainly: prompts should be clear, specific, and provide enough context for the model to understand what you are asking, and you should avoid ambiguity. [^2] A vague ask is ambiguity you did not notice.

**An invented fact** is a statement the model produces that sounds true and is not. IBM defines hallucinations as outputs that sound plausible but are factually wrong, irrelevant, or entirely fabricated, and it names the mechanism: the model presents fake facts, invented studies, nonexistent URLs, or incorrect details about real things as if they were factual. [^3] The word that matters is "as if." It has all the furniture of a real fact and none of the reality.

**A wrong format** is output that has the right content in the wrong shape. You wanted a table and got a paragraph. You wanted three bullets and got an essay. You wanted a one-line summary and got a page. The content is not wrong. The container is.

**Diagnosis** is the act of looking at a bad result and deciding which of the three it is before you change anything. It is the step everyone skips, and it is the step this whole lesson trains.

## Why these three, and only these three

Here is the clean way to hold the whole lesson. The tool needs three things from you to produce usable output, and each failure mode is one of those things going missing.

First, it needs a request it can act on. That is the vague ask. Second, it needs facts it can use, and because it has no access to truth, it will invent them when they are missing. That is the invented fact. Third, it needs to know what the finished thing should look like, because otherwise it defaults to the most ordinary shape. That is the wrong format.

Those three map exactly onto the four parts of the prompt from lesson 7. [^5] A vague ask is role, task, and context going missing. A wrong format is the format part going missing. And an invented fact is not a prompt problem at all, it is a reality problem, which is why no amount of prompting fixes it, only verification does. That last distinction is the most important one in this lesson, so I will come back to it.

## Failure mode one: the vague ask

You will recognize this one because the output is not wrong, it is just useless. It is a wall of generic paragraphs that could be about anything. It answers a question you did not ask. It is stuffed with filler. When you read it, you think "that is not what I meant," and you are right, because you never said what you meant.

Here is the mechanism. The model predicts the most likely completion of your text. If your text is "help me write an email," the most likely completion is the most generic email anyone could write, because that is what most of the examples in its training look like. [^2] It is not being lazy. It is being statistically normal, and normal is not what you wanted. Anthropic's best-practice guidance gives you the sharpest test for this, which it calls the golden rule: show your prompt to a colleague who has minimal context on the task and ask them to follow it. If they would be confused, the model will be too. [^1]

The fix is to complete the brief. Add the role, the task, the context, and the format from lesson 7. [^5] A vague ask turns into a workable one when you say who is talking, what exactly to produce, what facts to use and who will read it, and what the answer should look like. The fix is not more words. It is the specific words. Two sentences of the right kind beat a paragraph of the wrong kind.

A quick test to tell this failure mode apart: is the output generic, or is it specific? Generic output that could apply to any task is a vague ask. The moment you add the missing parts of the brief, the generic wall turns into something aimed at your actual situation. If it does not, you are looking at a different failure mode.

## Failure mode two: the invented fact

This one is the dangerous one, because it does not look like a failure. The output is specific, confident, and complete. It gives you a number, a name, a date, a citation, and it says all of it in the same even voice it uses for the things it is sure about. That confidence is exactly what makes it dangerous. IBM is blunt about why: a generative model does not know what is true or false, it only knows what fits the pattern, and it optimizes for plausibility, not for correctness about the world. [^3] When information is sparse, it does not say "I do not know." It makes up details based on patterns it has seen. [^3]

The classic case is the 2023 US lawyer who used a chatbot to help draft a court filing and asked for case law to support his arguments. The tool returned a list of authoritative-looking cases with case names, docket numbers, and quotations, all formatted like genuine legal precedent. None of it was real. The courts never issued those opinions, and the quoted passages did not exist anywhere. [^3] That is the invented fact failure mode at full strength, and it is the reason this series keeps hammering on verification. [^4]

The fix here is not a better prompt. It is verification. When the output makes a claim that matters, you check it against a primary source, the actual report, the company's own page, the document you are summarizing. You break the answer into its individual claims and check the ones that would embarrass you if they were wrong. [^4] Lesson 3 covered why these tools fabricate, and lesson 6 gave you the four checks and the habit of lateral reading. [^8] [^4] Everything in those lessons applies here. Nothing in lesson 8's iteration loop applies here. Iteration makes a fabricated number sound better. It does not make it true. [^6]

The most expensive mistake people make is treating this failure mode like a vague ask and trying to fix it by rephrasing. You cannot rephrase a false fact into a true one. The only move is to verify it, find the source, and replace the invented figure with the real one. Macquarie University documented exactly this failure in an early experiment: the tool produced a reference list full of entries that did not exist, with authors and journal details assembled from plausible parts. [^7] The protection costs a minute. When a citation matters, search for it. If you cannot find it, that is your answer.

## Failure mode three: the wrong format

This one is the easiest to fix and the most annoying to hit, because the content is right and the container is wrong. You asked for a summary and got an essay. You wanted a table comparing options and got a paragraph that lists them. You wanted three bullets and got a page with a summary at the top. The model understood you. It just picked the shape, and it picked the most ordinary shape by default.

That default is the problem. If you do not say what you want back, the model picks the most statistically likely structure, which is usually a wall of paragraphs. It has no reason to guess you wanted a table, so it does not. Anthropic's guidance is direct about the fix: be specific about the desired output format and constraints, and when the shape really matters, show the model an example of what you want, because examples are one of the most reliable ways to steer output format, tone, and structure. [^1] Naming the format is half the fix. Showing it is the other half.

So instead of "summarize this," you say "summarize this as a table with columns for decision, owner, and due date." Instead of "tell me what you think," you say "give me three options, each with one line on the tradeoff." Instead of "explain this," you say "explain this in three short bullets, no more." Each of those tells the model the shape as well as the content, and the shape is the part it would otherwise guess.

The cheapest habit that prevents this whole failure mode is to state the format in the prompt before you hit enter, and when the format is unusual or important, paste a small example of exactly what you want. That single move does more to cut your editing time than any other part of prompting. [^1]

## A diagnostic: which one are you looking at?

Here is the three-question checklist to run before you touch anything, because it decides which fix applies.

Is the output generic, vague, or off target? That is the vague ask. Fix it by completing the brief: add the role, the task, the context, and the format. [^5]

Is the output specific but wrong, or does it cite things you cannot find? That is the invented fact. Fix it by verifying against a primary source, not by rephrasing. [^4]

Is the output correct in substance but the wrong shape? That is the wrong format. Fix it by naming the format and, when it matters, showing an example. [^1]

One line to remember it: generic means you were vague, specific-but-unverifiable means it invented, wrong-shape means you never said the shape. Run the checklist and you will stop guessing which round to spend.

## Worked example: one scene, all three fixes

Put it together in a scene you will recognize. You need a short briefing for a leadership meeting about a customer rollout, and you want it to be honest about a slip in the timeline. Most people start with the vague ask:

> Tell me about the rollout.

The tool answers with two paragraphs of general reassurance. Everything is progressing, the team is engaged, we remain committed to a strong launch. It is not wrong. It is useless, because it could be about any project at any company. You have just hit failure mode one. [^2]

So you complete the brief. You say: "Act as a program lead. Write a 150-word update on the Phoenix rollout for the leadership team. The delivery date moved from September 15 to October 1 because the data migration ran three weeks late. We recovered two of those weeks. Lead with the new date, be factual, no blame." Now the answer is aimed at your actual situation. Good. That was the vague ask, fixed.

But read the new version carefully, because it slipped a number in: "customer adoption is currently at 87 percent." You did not give it that number, and the tool has no access to your adoption data. It produced 87 percent the same way it produces everything, by predicting a plausible figure. [^3] This is failure mode two, and notice how quietly it arrived, buried in an otherwise correct paragraph. You catch it by holding the output against what you already know. You open your own dashboard and read the real number. Whatever it is, that is the number you use. The fabricated 87 comes out, the verified figure goes in. That is the fix. [^4]

Now you look at the shape. The tool handed you a solid paragraph, but leadership wants a briefing they can skim, with the decision up top and the dates in one line. So you specify the format: "Now format this as three short sections with headings: Status, Reason for the slip, New date and confidence. Keep each section to two sentences." The same content, now in the shape the room can actually use. That was failure mode three, fixed by naming the format. [^1]

One scene, three fixes, each aimed at the specific thing that was broken. That is the whole lesson, and it took about a minute of typing instead of four rounds of hoping.

## Try it

This exercise takes about ten minutes and builds the diagnostic into your hands. Take a real task you have asked an AI tool to do recently, something with a clear deliverable.

Round one, deliberately vague. Run your request exactly as you would have before this series, something like "help me with X" with no role, no context, no format. Write down what comes back. It will be generic. That is failure mode one showing itself.

Round two, complete the brief. Add the role, the task, the context, and the format from lesson 7. [^5] Run it again and note how the output tightens. If any specific number or name appears that you did not supply, that is your signal to check failure mode two. Verify it against a source you can see before you use it. [^4]

Round three, fix the shape. Look at the format of the best output you got and decide whether it is actually the shape you wanted. If not, say so explicitly: "put this in a table," "give me three bullets," "one-line summary." Run it and compare.

Here is the answer to the exercise. The vague round shows you how much of the output's weakness is your own missing context. The verification check shows you how silently an invented fact can hide in an otherwise good answer. And the format round shows you that the model will not volunteer the shape you want, so you have to name it. If you noticed yourself reaching for a rephrase when the output was specific-but-wrong, that is the instinct this lesson exists to replace. Verify instead. [^3] [^4]

## What's next

You now have the diagnostic that ties lessons 6, 7, and 8 together. Vague asks get the completed brief, invented facts get verification, wrong formats get a named shape, and iteration runs after you know which one you are fixing, not before. The next lesson moves from fixing individual exchanges to working across a real task end to end, and it introduces the one technique that collapses most of these fixes into the first round: giving the tool an example of exactly what you want, so it stops guessing your context, your facts, and your format. [^1] For now, hold the habit from today. When output misses, do not rephrase on reflex. Run the checklist. Generic means you were vague. Specific and unverifiable means it invented. Wrong shape means you never said the shape. Name the failure, apply the fix, and only then iterate. That is how you stop fighting the tool and start steering it.
