# Lesson 7: Anatomy of a Good Prompt: Role, Task, Context, and Format

The earlier lessons built your foundation: you know the machine predicts the next token, that it can invent facts, and that its sweet spot is drafting rather than deciding. That knowledge is useless until it changes how you actually type. This lesson is where it does.

Lesson 5 and 6 covered the basics of talking to the tool. Now it is time for the difference between a prompt that gets you a usable draft on the first try and a prompt that leaves you editing a pile of fluff. The gap is not magic. It is structure.

Think of a prompt the way you would brief a new employee who is brilliant but has never met your team. You would not hand them a vague "sort this out." You would tell them who they are acting as, exactly what to do, what they need to know, and what the finished thing should look like. A prompt is the same brief, and it has four parts.

## The four parts of a prompt

Ask any experienced user why a prompt worked and the reasons keep falling into the same four buckets. The Prompt Engineering Guide, one of the standard references for this material, names them as the core elements of any prompt: an **instruction** (the task), **context** (external information that steers the model), **input data** (what you hand it to work on), and an **output indicator** (the format you want back). [^1]

Those four reduce to a memorable four-part anatomy you can hold in your head:

- **Role.** Who is the tool acting as? A fresh pair of eyes, a skeptical reviewer, a plain-language explainer, a writing coach.
- **Task.** What exactly do you want done? One clear verb, with a concrete deliverable.
- **Context.** What does the tool need to know to do it well? Your audience, the purpose, the constraints, the facts it should use.
- **Format.** What should the answer look like? A paragraph, a table, three bullets, a list with headings.

That is the whole structure. Everything else is refinement. Let me walk each one and show what it actually does, because the payoff of each part is specific.

## Role: who is talking

A role is a single instruction about the stance the tool takes. It matters more than people expect. Anthropic's prompting guidance is blunt about it: setting a role focuses the model's behavior and tone, and even a single sentence makes a difference. [^2]

Here is why it works. The same text, asked from two different stances, gets shaped by a different set of assumptions. Ask for "a summary of this report" and the tool writes a neutral recap. Ask the tool to act as "a skeptical auditor looking for weak spots in this report" and the same text gets read for gaps, contradictions, and unsupported claims. You have not changed the document. You have changed the lens it is read through, and the lens changes what gets surfaced.

Role is cheap to add and it rescues you from the most common failure of all, which is a tool that answers a question nobody asked. Tell it who it is and it stops being a generic answer-machine and starts being the specific expert you wanted. You are not role-playing for fun. You are pointing the lens.

## Task: one clear verb

The task is the heart of the prompt, and the most common mistake here is asking for two things at once. "Summarize this and tell me what to do about it" is two jobs, and the tool will split the difference, doing neither well.

Anthropic's best-practice guidance puts it in terms you can use immediately: be clear and direct, and when the order of steps matters, list them as numbered or bulleted steps. [^2] The Golden Rule they give is the sharpest test in this entire lesson: show your prompt to a colleague who has minimal context on the task and ask them to follow it. If they would be confused, the model will be too. [^2] That single habit catches most of what makes prompts fail.

So write the task as one concrete verb with a clear deliverable: "Draft," "Summarize," "Rewrite," "List," "Compare," "Explain." If the task genuinely has steps, number them. Vague tasks produce vague output because the model has to guess which of the many things you might mean is the one you want.

## Context: what it needs to know

Context is the part that separates a professional from a tourist. The tool knows a lot about the world and nothing about your company, your customer, your deadline, or your standards. Every relevant detail you leave out, it has to guess, and its guesses default to the most generic version.

Anthropic names this directly: add context to improve performance. Explaining why something matters helps the model target the response, because it no longer has to infer your purpose. [^2] The example they use is instructive: telling the model the reasoning behind a formatting preference gets you a smarter result than just stating the preference, because the model can generalize from the reason. [^2]

Practical context includes: who will read this, what the decision or goal behind it is, any facts or numbers it must use, and any constraints (tone, length, things to avoid, things to keep). When you paste a document, separate it from your instructions so the model can tell your command from your source material. OpenAI's own guidance shows this: wrap the context in clear markers so the instruction and the material do not blur into each other. [^3] Your context is fuel. Feed it the right fuel and it aims at your target; feed it nothing and it aims at the most common thing anyone could mean.

## Format: what you want back

Format is the most underused part, because people assume the tool will "figure out" the right shape. It will not. It will pick the most likely shape, which is usually a wall of paragraphs with a summary at the top. That is rarely what you want.

OpenAI's guidance is explicit that the model responds better when you specify the format and, even better, show it with an example. "Articulate the desired output format through examples" is the advice, because models respond better when shown specific format requirements than when told abstractly. [^3]

So decide what you want back before you hit enter. A decision summary with three options? Ask for a list with the tradeoffs of each. A meeting recap? Ask for decisions, owners, and due dates as separate sections. A comparison? Ask for a table. Naming the format is not a courtesy to the tool. It is a specification you already had in your head, and putting it in the prompt is the only way the tool can honor it.

## Putting it together: the four-part prompt

Here is the whole structure in one line you can reuse for almost any task:

> You are **[role]** . **[Task]** . Here is the context: **[facts, audience, purpose, constraints]** . Format your answer as **[format]** .

That is not a magic incantation. It is just the brief, in order, so nothing gets lost. When you forget a part, you can feel which one is missing by looking at the output. Too generic? You skipped context. Rambling and unfocused? You skipped the task or the role. Wrong shape? You skipped the format. The anatomy gives you a diagnostic, not just a template.

## Worked example: the same request, two ways

Here is a realistic scene. You need a short note to send to a project stakeholder who is worried the timeline is slipping, and you want it to be honest without being alarmist. Your first attempt is the way most people prompt:

> Write something about the project timeline.

See everything that is missing. No role (is this a peer, a manager, an apology?), no real task (something about what?), no context (what project? what is the new date? what is the worry?), no format (a sentence? an email? a bullet list?). The tool will guess all of it and hand you a generic, slightly nervous paragraph about how things are "progressing as expected."

Now the four-part version:

> You are a project lead writing a brief, honest update to a stakeholder. Write a 100-word update on the Phoenix rollout. The delivery date has moved from September 15 to October 1 because the data migration took three weeks longer than estimated. We have recovered two of those weeks and expect no further slippage. Keep it factual, no blame, and end with the new date and one sentence of confidence. Format it as a short paragraph, then a one-line summary.

Compare what each prompt gets you. The second one told the tool who it is, what to produce, what facts to use, the tone, the length, and the shape. The output will read like a specific person writing about a specific project, because the model no longer has to invent any of that. The first prompt gave it nothing to work with and got exactly what it asked for.

That is the whole lesson in one contrast. The tool did not get smarter. The brief got complete.

## Try it

This exercise takes about five minutes and will change how you draft prompts. Take a real task you have asked an AI tool to do this week (an email, a summary, a draft, a plan, anything).

First, run your original prompt exactly as you wrote it. Note what is missing or generic about the result.

Then rewrite it using the four-part structure:

> You are **[role]** . **[task with one clear verb]** . Context: **[audience, purpose, key facts, tone]** . Format my answer as **[format]** .

Run the new prompt. Compare the two outputs side by side, and write down which part of the structure changed the result the most. For most people it is context, because that is the part they were leaving out.

Now push it one step further and test the Golden Rule. [^2] Read your new prompt out loud to a colleague with minimal context on the task and ask, "Would you know what to do from this?" If they hesitate, the tool did too, and you now know exactly which part to add.

## What's next

You now have the skeleton of a good prompt: role, task, context, and format. The next lesson takes you from getting a good first draft to getting the right final answer. You will learn how to refine a prompt when the first try is close but not quite, how to give the model an example so it matches your exact style, and how to know when the tool needs more than a better prompt to be reliable. For now, hold the habit from today: before you hit enter, check the brief. Do you know who is talking? Is there one clear task? Does the model have the context it needs? Is the format specified? Answer those four and the first draft stops being a disappointment you edit and starts being a starting point you use.
