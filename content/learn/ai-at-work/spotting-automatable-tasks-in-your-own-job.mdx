# Lesson 18: Spotting the Automatable Tasks in Your Own Job

The last lesson taught you when to leave the AI tool switched off. This one is the mirror image. It shows you how to find the work in your own week that deserves to be automated, the repetitive slices you can hand to a tool and get back real hours.

Most people start with the wrong question. They ask "is my job automatable?" and the answer feels like no, because their job has parts that need judgment, relationships, and context. That question is too big to be useful. The question that actually pays is narrower: "which of the tasks in my job are automatable?" A job is not one thing. It is a bundle of dozens of small recurring tasks, and those tasks are not equal. Some need your judgment. Some just need to happen, the same way, every week. The skill is telling the two apart, and it is a skill you can learn in an afternoon.

The research backs this up. The McKinsey Global Institute studied automation at the level of individual activities rather than whole occupations. Its 2017 report found that fewer than 5 percent of occupations could be fully automated with then-current technology, but that about half of all the activities people are paid to do had the technical potential to be automated. [^1] Almost every occupation is a mix. So the real question is never whether your job is automatable. It is which slices of it are, and how much time those slices eat.

## The words we are using

Before the method, the terms, kept plain.

**A task** is a discrete piece of work with a clear start and end. "Prepare the Monday sales summary" is a task. "Run operations" is not. The whole method depends on this difference, so be strict with yourself: if you cannot say where the piece of work begins and ends, it is not a task yet, it is a bucket.

**A recurring task** happens on a schedule: daily, weekly, monthly, or every time a certain trigger occurs. A new hire joins, so you send the onboarding pack. That is recurring even though the date moves around.

**A rule-based task** is one where you could write the steps down in order and someone else, or a tool, could follow them without asking you a single question. The steps can be a checklist, a template, or a prompt. What matters is that the procedure exists and you are not making it up fresh each time.

**A judgment call** is the opposite. It is a decision that depends on context, values, relationships, or consequences you personally own. "Should we waive this late fee for a good client?" is a judgment call. "Send the standard late-fee reminder" is a rule.

**Automation** here does not mean robots or writing code. It means any setup where a tool does the repeatable part and you do the checking. That can be a saved prompt that drafts your weekly report, a template that formats an email, or a script that merges a spreadsheet. The tool changes, the idea does not: you find the repeatable shape, hand it over, and keep the parts that need you.

## The four signals of an automatable task

Run any task past these four questions. A task that scores high on all of them is a candidate. A task that fails one of them hard is probably not worth automating, at least not whole.

**Signal one: it happens more than once.** Automation is an investment, and frequency is the return. A task you do twice a year is rarely worth the setup. A task you do every week is a different story. When in doubt, count the occurrences per year and put a number on it. The number cuts through the feelings.

**Signal two: you can write the steps down.** Try it. Open a blank note and write the procedure for the task in order, the way you would hand it to a careful assistant. If you can write ten steps and the tenth produces the result, the task is rule-based and automatable in principle. If you stall halfway because the next move depends on something you only know when you see it, that part is judgment, and it stays with you.

**Signal three: the input and the output are clear.** "Pull the numbers from this sheet and produce the summary in this format" has a defined input and a defined output. That clarity is what a tool can work with. Tasks where the input is a vague sense of what the boss needs are harder to automate, because the tool cannot see the input either.

**Signal four: a mistake is cheap or checkable.** Wrong output on a low-stakes internal draft costs you five minutes of reading. Wrong output on a client invoice costs you a relationship. The cheap ones are safe to automate freely. The expensive ones are still automatable, but only with a check you actually run, which is the draftable and decidable split from Lesson 6: the tool drafts, you decide, and the deciding part never leaves your hands. [^2]

Notice what the four signals do. They separate the mechanical shell of your job from the judgment core. That split is the whole game. The mechanical shell is bigger than most people think, and the judgment core is more valuable than most people spend it on.

## A worked example: Priya, the operations coordinator

Priya runs operations for a 40-person company. Her week is a pile of recurring tasks, so she is a perfect test case. Here is her actual list, with rough time per week.

- Monday sales summary: pull numbers from the CRM and the spreadsheet, format them into the leadership template, add a note on what changed. Two hours.
- New hire onboarding pack: assemble accounts, links, and the handbook, send the welcome email. Happens two to three times a month, about an hour each.
- Expense report reconciliation: match receipts to the card statement, flag anything odd. An hour and a half.
- Supplier questions: pricing, delivery dates, contract details. Unpredictable, several a day, some quick and some involved.
- Drafting the monthly internal newsletter: collecting wins, writing the intro, formatting. Three hours once a month.

Now run the four signals.

The Monday sales summary: happens 52 times a year. She could write the steps in order (open the CRM report, pull the week's numbers, drop them into the template, check the totals, add a note). Input is the CRM and spreadsheet, output is the filled template. A wrong number is embarrassing but caught by the second reader, and she can check it in five minutes. That is four for four. Prime candidate.

The onboarding pack: recurring, rule-based (the steps are a fixed list), clear input and output (new hire details in, pack out). A wrong account link matters, but it is checkable. Four for four. Also a candidate, though lower volume.

The expense reconciliation: recurring, mostly rule-based, and the flagging step is the judgment part. "Match receipts to statement" is mechanical. "Flag anything odd" is a call. Candidate, but only the mechanical half.

The supplier questions: recurring in count but not in content. The steps depend on the question, and a wrong answer to a client-facing supplier costs trust. Fails signals two and four. Not a candidate.

The newsletter: recurring, and the formatting is rule-based, but the intro is writing with a voice and a judgment about what matters this month. Half and half, same as the reconciliation.

Priya starts with the strongest one, the Monday summary. Here is the math. Two hours a week is 104 hours a year. She spends one afternoon building the automation: a saved prompt that knows the template, a short checklist of where the numbers come from, and one test run. That is about four hours of setup, plus thirty minutes a month to keep the prompt current when the template changes. After the setup, the task takes her thirty minutes a week instead of two hours: she pulls the numbers, lets the tool draft the summary, then checks every figure and writes the judgment note. She saves an hour and a half per week, which is 78 hours a year. The setup and maintenance cost her about ten hours in the first year. Net gain: 68 hours, and the setup pays for itself in under three weeks.

The numbers are worth sitting with. 68 hours a year from one task, and she did not touch the tasks that need her. She still answers supplier questions, still makes the call on odd expenses, still writes the newsletter intro herself. She automated the shell and kept the core. [^3]

## How to run your own task audit

You can do this on a train ride. Here is the whole method.

**Step one: list the tasks.** Do not try to remember your job from scratch. Take your calendar, your inbox, and your to-do list from the last two weeks, and write down every discrete piece of work you actually did, with an estimated time per occurrence. Twenty to thirty tasks is normal. If a piece of work does not have a start and end, skip it for now.

**Step two: score each task on the four signals.** Use a simple scale: 1 for no, 2 for partly, 3 for yes. Frequency (how many times a year), writable steps, clear input and output, cheap or checkable mistakes. A task scoring 10 or higher out of 12 is a candidate. A 12 is a gift, automate it first.

**Step three: count the hours.** Multiply time per occurrence by occurrences per year. You are looking for the tasks where the annual total is big: 50 hours, 100 hours, more. Small scores with big hours are the hidden treasure of most jobs.

**Step four: estimate the setup cost.** How long would it take you to write the prompt, template, or script, and test it once? Be honest. Most single-task automations cost between one afternoon and a day of setup. If the setup is bigger than the annual savings, stop. That is not a failure, that is the calculation working.

**Step five: do the smallest version first.** Pick one task, build the simplest thing that handles it, and run it for two weeks. Count what it actually saved you, not what you hoped. Then decide whether to keep it, improve it, or throw it away. [^4] One working automation is worth more than a folder of plans.

## Try it

This exercise takes about thirty minutes and gives you a real target.

First, do the audit: list your last two weeks of tasks, score each on the four signals, and find your one best candidate. Second, do the payback math for that candidate: annual hours now, estimated setup hours, estimated hours after, and weeks to payback. Third, pick the single task and describe, in five sentences, what the automated version would look like.

If you want a second opinion, paste this into any AI tool, along with your task list. The prompt is deliberately tool-agnostic.

> Here is a list of tasks I do at work, with approximate time per occurrence and how often each happens. For each task, score it 1 to 3 on four questions: how often it happens in a year, whether the steps could be written down in order, whether the input and output are clear, and whether a wrong result would be cheap to fix or catch. Then list the three tasks with the highest combined scores, estimate the annual hours each one eats, and suggest the smallest possible automation for the top one: what the tool would do, what I would still check, and how long the setup would take.

Read the answer the way Lesson 6 taught you. [^2] The tool is a helpful colleague with good instincts and no idea about your actual workflow. Its suggestions are a starting point for your own judgment, not a verdict.

## What's next

You have found the tasks worth automating. The next step is making that automation stick, and the next lesson is exactly that: building a personal prompt library. Once you know which tasks deserve a tool, you need the tool to do them the same way every time, without you rewriting the instructions from scratch each week. Lesson 19 shows you how to save, name, organize, and reuse the prompts that work, so the task you spotted today becomes a repeatable tool you can run in minutes instead of a prompt you rebuild every Monday. That is where the hours you found in this lesson actually stay saved.

For now, run the audit on your own week. List the tasks, score them, count the hours. The point is not to automate everything. It is to find the handful of recurring shells in your job, hand those to the tool, and spend your judgment where it actually matters. [^1] [^4]
