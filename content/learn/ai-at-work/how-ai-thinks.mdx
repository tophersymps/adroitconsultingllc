# Lesson 2: How AI Thinks in Plain Language

Lesson 1 gave you the one sentence underneath everything: an AI assistant predicts the next token, over and over. This lesson makes that concrete. What is a token? How does the model decide what comes next? And why does it sometimes seem to forget what you told it ten minutes ago?

The answers to those three questions explain most of what feels strange or frustrating when you use these tools at work. Once you know how the machine actually reads, you stop being surprised by it, and you start prompting it on purpose.

## What the model reads: tokens, not words

A large language model does not see your sentence the way you do. You see words. The model sees a sequence of **tokens**, which are chunks of text. A token is usually close to a word, but not exactly. [^1]

OpenAI's own documentation defines it plainly: tokens "can be thought of as pieces of words," and roughly 100 tokens equal about 75 English words. [^1] So a token is, on average, about three quarters of a word.

The surprising part is that tokens do not line up with words at all. Some words are one token. Some are two or three. Here is a real example. I ran a common tokenizer over the sentence "The meeting is at 3pm in room 4B." and it split into 13 tokens:

```text
The | meeting | is | at | 3 | pm | in | room | 4 | B | .
```

Notice what happened. "3pm" became two tokens, "3" and "pm". "4B" became "4" and "B". The period is its own token. The machine cut the text into pieces by what it had seen in its training data, not by your idea of a word.

Even more revealing is a word like "understanding". That single word is two tokens: "under" and "standing". [^1] The model learned those fragments as separate pieces and reassembles them. This is why a token is "almost a word, but not quite." [^3]

Why should you care? Two reasons. First, token count is the currency of these tools: your prompt and the reply both get counted, and limits are set in tokens, not words. [^1] A 500-word email is not 500 tokens. It is roughly 375, and sometimes your own message is longer than the reply you asked for. Second, knowing that the model reads in fragments explains why a small typo can derail it, and why it treats a name or a product differently depending on how it got tokenized.

## How it writes: one token at a time

Here is the mechanical heart of the whole thing. The model does not compose a full answer and hand it to you. It writes one token, then uses that token plus everything before it to guess the next one, and repeats. [^4]

Microsoft's engineering documentation describes the loop directly: the model predicts a value for the next token, picks the most likely one from its vocabulary, appends it to the text so far, and uses that as the input for the next step. [^3] Each new token becomes part of the input for the token after it.

So when you ask it to write an apology email to a customer, the model is not pulling a finished email off a shelf. It is guessing the first word, then the second given the first, then the third given the first two, all the way to the end. That is why the results are probabilistic: give it the same prompt twice and it can pick different likely tokens. It is also why long replies drift. Every guess carries a small chance of steering the text, and after a thousand guesses those small chances add up. The first half of a long answer is usually tighter than the last half.

This is not a flaw you need to fix. It is the mechanism. You work with it by keeping your request focused, because everything you say before and during the request is fuel the model uses to choose the next token.

## The context window: the slice it can see

Now the third idea, and the one that explains the most confusion. The model can only consider a limited amount of text at any one time. That limit is called the **context window**, and it is measured in tokens. [^2]

IBM's definition is the cleanest: the context window is "the amount of text, in tokens, that the model can consider or remember at any one time." [^2] Everything inside that window, your instructions, the document you pasted, the earlier turns of the conversation, is what the model has to work with. Everything outside it does not exist to the model in that moment.

Here is the part most people miss. When the model answers you, it is not remembering your whole chat. It is looking at whatever fits in the window right now. A conversation is not a memory; it is a moving slice of recent text. [^2] If the slice fills up, the oldest text falls off. That is why a long conversation slowly loses the details you mentioned at the start. The tool is not being careless. The start is simply no longer in the window it can see.

Context windows have grown a lot, and some current models can handle a huge amount, over a million tokens on the largest offerings. [^2] But in everyday use most tools run with a smaller effective limit, and your total is your prompt plus the response. You can still blow past it if you paste an entire annual report and then ask for a rewrite of the whole thing.

## Why this matters at work

Three practical consequences fall out of all this. Keep them in mind and a lot of confusing tool behavior stops being confusing.

**One: keep the important stuff near the end.** Since the model works with the most recent tokens in its window, put your key instruction late in the prompt, not buried in the first paragraph. If you are pasting a big document, put the question after the document, not before it, so the question is the freshest thing the model sees. [^2]

**Two: if a tool seems to "forget," restate the essentials.** You do not need to repeat everything, just the load-bearing details. Instead of "as I said earlier," say "the contract is with Meridian, the deadline is Friday, draft the closing paragraph." That puts the fact back in the window where the model can use it. [^2]

**Three: watch your total length.** Because your prompt and the reply share the same token budget, a very long document plus a request for a long output can hit the ceiling, and the tool will either truncate, refuse, or drop the earliest part of what you gave it. [^1] If you need a summary of a huge file, you often get better results by asking for it in stages than by demanding one giant pass.

## Worked example: the disappearing detail

Say you are building a status report with an assistant. You open a chat and give it three sentences of setup:

> Project Helios is running two weeks late. The database migration is done. The client wants a weekly update every Friday at 9am.

Then you have a long back and forth about the migration. Forty turns later, you ask "remind me what the client asked for." If the earlier turns filled the context window, the model may answer vaguely, or it may invent a detail that was never said, because it is guessing what the most likely answer looks like, not reading what you wrote in turn three. [^2] [^4]

That is not a broken tool. That is the context window doing its job. The fix is not to be annoyed. The fix is to re-anchor: "The client wants a weekly update every Friday at 9am. What did I commit to in the last update?" Now the important fact is in the window again, and the answer snaps back to being useful.

## Try it

This one takes two minutes and changes how you read these tools forever.

Open any AI assistant you have (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, or an internal tool, they all behave this way). First, ask it to count the tokens in a sentence of yours, then ask a follow-up that makes you check your own mental model:

> Count the tokens in this sentence: [paste a sentence of about 15 words]. Then explain which words took more than one token and why.

Then run the context-window test on a real piece of work:

> Pretend we are in a long conversation and I gave you this instruction at the very start: [paste one clear instruction]. I have now sent you [N] follow-up messages. Restate my original instruction as best you can, and tell me honestly whether you actually remember it or are reconstructing it from context.

The second prompt is the honest one to sit with. Watch how the model answers. Most of the time it will reconstruct rather than truly recall, because that is what a next-token predictor does. [^4] Naming that out loud is the skill.

## What's next

Now that you know the model reads in tokens, writes one token at a time, and sees only a limited window, the next lesson turns to what that machinery gets wrong. Lesson 3 is about hallucinations, the confident inventions these tools sometimes produce, why they happen, and how to catch them before they cost you. Prompt it with that picture in mind and the results stop being a mystery.
