# Lesson 4: What AI Is Genuinely Good At, and Where It Fails

By now you know the machine underneath these tools predicts the next token over and over, and that it occasionally turns a confident guess into a made-up fact. Lesson 3 gave you the habits for catching those hallucinations. This lesson steps back and asks the question you have probably wanted to ask since lesson 1: given all that, what is the tool actually good for?

The honest answer is not "everything" and not "nothing." It is a specific, learnable split, and it is the single most useful thing you can take from this entire series. Get the split right and you get the speed without the damage. Get it wrong and you either trust it too much or throw out a genuinely useful tool out of frustration. Both mistakes cost you.

## The one sentence to hold onto

AI is a fast, confident drafter. It is excellent at producing language, and it has no built-in truth check.

Those two halves matter together. The drafting half is why the tool saves you so much time. The no-truth-check half is why you can never hand it the final word on anything that has a right answer. Every "is AI good at this" question in your head reduces to the same test: am I asking for a draft, or am I asking for a decision?

That framing is not my invention. Researchers at the MIT Center for Collective Intelligence reviewed more than a hundred studies of humans working with AI, and they found the split holds up in the data. Human-AI combinations performed worse on tasks that involved decision-making, but better on tasks that involved creating content. [^1] Same tools, same people, opposite results, depending on whether the task was deciding or producing.

## What AI is genuinely good at

Start with the good news, because it is a lot. Every one of these is a task where a next-word predictor shines, and where you should hand it work without guilt:

**Summarizing and condensing.** Give it a wall of notes, a long email thread, a dense report, and it will hand back a tight version that hits the main points. This is the most reliably useful thing these tools do, and it works across every assistant on the market. [^2] It is also the reason people report the biggest time savings from the very first week.

**Drafting and first passes.** Blank pages are expensive. A tool that produces a reasonable first draft of an email, a job description, a project update, or a proposal gets you past the blank page in seconds instead of an hour. You are not done, you are started, and that is the point.

**Rewriting and re-voicing.** Have a paragraph that is technically fine but reads flat? Ask it to make it friendlier, more formal, shorter, or punchier. This is where the tool genuinely earns its keep, because it can shuffle language fast while you decide whether the new version says what you mean.

**Brainstorming and exploring.** When you need options rather than an answer, the tool is excellent. Name a problem and ask for ten ways to approach it. Most will be ordinary, one might be useful, and the act of generating them gets you thinking. Generative AI is built for exactly this kind of iterative back-and-forth. [^1]

**Explaining and translating for an audience.** Ask it to explain a complicated thing to a new hire, or to translate a technical paragraph into plain language, and it will often do a better job of the plain-language version than a hurried expert will.

Notice what these have in common. They are all language-production tasks. They are about generating, reworking, condensing, or reframing text, with no single correct answer to hit. The tool does not need to be right, it needs to be useful, and on those tasks it is.

## Where it fails

Now the other half, and this is where people lose money and trust. The tool fails precisely where its core design stops working:

**Anything with a single right answer.** Exact calculations, precise figures, dates, prices, counts, and facts. A next-word predictor does not compute, it guesses what a correct answer looks like, and the guess is usually close and occasionally wrong. [^3] That is a terrible combination for anything where being "mostly right" is the same as being wrong.

**Current or private information.** The model knows what it learned in training, and no matter what tool you use, it does not know what happened yesterday, what is happening in your building, or what your company actually decided. It will not say "I do not know." It will fill the gap with its most likely guess. [^3] Ask it for your own internal policy and you are asking it to invent one.

**Decisions with stakes and accountability.** This is the one the research is most blunt about. Human-AI combinations did worse on decision-making tasks. [^1] When a choice has consequences, when someone will be held accountable for it, when the cost of being wrong is real, the tool has nothing to contribute but speed, and speed is not what you need. The MIT team put it directly: humans excel at contextual understanding and emotional intelligence, while AI excels at repetitive, high-volume, data-driven subtasks. [^1] Judgment stays with you.

**Knowing when it is wrong.** The tool has no internal alarm for its own errors. It cannot tell you "I am not sure about this part" the way a careful colleague will, because it has no sense of what it knows versus what it guessed. [^2] That means the responsibility for catching mistakes never leaves your hands. This is not a bug you can patch around. It is the design.

The common thread on this side: these are tasks where being wrong is costly and where there is a fact or a judgment underneath that the tool cannot verify.

## The mental model: draftable vs decidable

Here is the practical rule that ties it together. Before you ask the tool to do anything, sort the work into two buckets.

**Draftable work** has no single right answer and the cost of imperfection is low. Summarizing, drafting, brainstorming, rewording. Hand this to the tool freely, then skim the result before it ships.

**Decidable work** has a right answer, or a stake, or both. Numbers, policies, current facts, high-stakes decisions, anything with accountability attached. Keep this for yourself. The tool can be a starting point, never the final word.

Most real tasks are a mix, and that mix is where the skill lives. A status report is mostly draftable, except the figures, which are decidable. An email to a frustrated customer is mostly draftable, except the apology's commitment, which is decidable. The tool drafts, you decide, and the two of you split the work at the right line.

## Worked example: the meeting notes

Here is a realistic scene. You just came out of a long project meeting, you have a mess of typed notes, and your manager wants a summary email by the end of the day.

You paste your raw notes into an assistant and ask for a clean summary of the decisions and next steps. This is where the tool earns its keep. In under a minute it hands back an organized email: decisions in one section, action items in another, owners and due dates pulled from your notes, and a tone that reads like you on a good day. The drafting is genuinely good. This is not a gimmick. It saved you the half hour of untangling your own notes.

Then you read the draft, and you look at the numbers. The notes said "roughly 40% of the rollout is done." The draft says "over half the rollout is complete." The model did not misread your notes. It saw a percentage and rounded it up to something that sounded more confident, and it did it in a voice indistinguishable from the rest of the email. [^3] If you had skimmed for tone instead of checking the figures, that wrong number would have gone to your manager.

The skill is not "never use AI for this." The skill is that you used it for the draftable half, the organization and the wording, and then you checked the decidable half, the numbers, against the one source you trust. That division is the entire lesson. The tool turned your chaos into a draft faster than you could, and you did the part it cannot do, which is verify that the draft is true.

## Try it

Take one real piece of work you have done this week and run it through the two-bucket test.

First, write the task down, then separate it into its draftable parts and its decidable parts. Be specific. "Summarize the meeting" is draftable. "State that we are 40% complete" is decidable. Most tasks break into a mix, and naming which parts are which is the skill.

Then test the failure mode on purpose. Give any assistant you have this prompt:

> Here is a short piece of real data from my work: [paste a few facts and a number]. Summarize it, then tell me which numbers you kept exactly as I gave them and which ones you changed or rounded, and why.

Watch what it does with the numbers. If it rounded or "corrected" a figure, you have just seen, in your own work, exactly why the decidable half stays with you. [^2] [^3]

## What's next

Lesson 5 turns from knowing what the tool is good at to getting it to do that reliably. Now that you can sort work into draftable and decidable, the next lesson is about how you talk to the tool so it stays on its strengths and surfaces its limits early, instead of hiding them. For now, hold the split from today: draftable work you delegate freely, decidable work you keep, and on everything that mixes the two, you are the one who decides where the line falls.
