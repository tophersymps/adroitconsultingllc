# Lesson 20: Simple Repeatable Workflows You Can Run Weekly

The last lesson left you with a small library of prompts you already made work. Good. Now comes the part that turns that collection into saved time on autopilot. A single saved prompt saves you the typing of that one task. A weekly workflow saves you the deciding, too, because you stop re-planning the same small batch of tasks every single week.

Here is the difference, kept plain. A prompt is one request you send to a tool. A workflow is a fixed sequence of steps you run in order, toward a recurring result. When those steps are prompts from your library, run the same way on the same day, you get a routine you can start almost without thinking. That is the payoff this lesson is after: not more automation, but a small, repeatable habit that runs itself with you checking the output.

This lesson sits directly on the two before it. Lesson 18 showed you how to spot the recurring tasks in your own job that are worth handing to a tool. [^1] Lesson 19 showed you how to save the prompts that handle them into a library you can find again. [^2] A workflow is what you get when you take the tasks from the first lesson, the prompts from the second, and run them in a fixed order on a schedule. If you have not done those two yet, this lesson still works, but it works better once you have even a handful of saved prompts to draw on.

## The words we are using

Keep the terms plain so the method stays simple.

**A workflow** is a fixed sequence of steps run in order to produce a recurring result. It is not a single request. It is several, arranged so each step hands its output to the next. "Every Friday I pull the week's numbers, condense my notes, draft the update, and check it before sending" is a workflow. "Write me a status update" is a prompt.

**A step** is one discrete action inside the workflow. In a workflow that uses AI, most steps are one saved prompt from your library, run with the input it needs. [^2]

**A weekly workflow** is a workflow you run on a fixed schedule: the same steps, the same order, the same day, roughly the same time. The schedule is what makes it a habit instead of a to-do you re-remember each week.

**A handoff** is the output of one step becoming the input of the next. The numbers you pull in step one feed the draft in step three. When the handoffs are clean, each step starts ready to go and the workflow runs smoothly end to end.

**A check** is the final step where you verify the output before it goes anywhere. Lesson 6 established the split that runs through this whole series: the tool drafts, you decide. [^3] The check is where "you decide" happens for a workflow. It is the step you never skip, because it is the step that keeps a fast routine safe.

**A timebox** is a fixed amount of time you give the workflow, like 45 minutes on Friday afternoon. The timebox matters because a workflow you can finish in a set window actually runs every week, and one that balloons into an afternoon gets skipped.

## Why a fixed order beats improvising every week

Most people run their recurring tasks the improvisational way. The task rolls around, they think about it fresh, they decide what to include this time, they write a prompt from scratch or from memory, and they re-make the same small choices they made last week and the week before. None of those choices is hard on its own. Added together, they cost you a few minutes and a few decisions every single week, forever.

A fixed order removes most of that. You decide the order and the inputs once, and then the weekly question shrinks from "how do I do all of this" to "run the routine and check the result." That is a real saving, and it compounds the way the library from Lesson 19 did: the first week costs you a little extra to set the order, and every week after is a little faster and a little more consistent. [^2]

Consistency is the quieter win. When you improvise, the third version of a task comes out sloppier than the first, because you are rewriting your own instructions from memory. A workflow holds the order fixed, so the quality does not drift week to week. The same prompts, the same inputs, the same check. You are not getting the model to be smarter. You are getting yourself to stop re-planning work you already planned.

## The shape of a good weekly workflow

Before the example, the five things a good weekly workflow has, so you can see them when they appear.

**It is bounded.** Three to five steps, and under an hour. A workflow is a habit, and habits survive when they are small. A 12-step, half-day workflow is a project, and it will get skipped.

**It runs on a fixed day and time.** Not "sometime this week." Friday at 3:00. The schedule is what turns the workflow into a routine you do not have to re-decide.

**Each step feeds the next.** The output of step one is the input of step two. When a step needs no output from a prior step, it does not need to wait, but in a well-built weekly workflow most steps hand something forward.

**It ends with a check.** The last step is you reading the output, verifying the facts against your own source, and only then letting it leave your hands. [^3] A workflow with no check is not automation, it is delegation to something you do not verify.

**It is built from prompts you already trust.** You are not writing new prompts on the spot. You are pulling ones from your library that already produced output you used and were happy with. [^2]

## A worked example: Marcus's Friday closeout

Marcus is the customer support lead from Lesson 19. Last week he built a starter library with a few prompts he already used and liked: a weekly status update for leadership, a polite-but-firm reply for frustrated customers, and a clean internal summary he makes from a jumble of support notes. [^2] Today he turns the first and last of those, plus one more, into a fixed weekly routine.

Here is the routine he designs. Every Friday at 3:00, he blocks out 45 minutes and runs four steps in order.

**Step one: pull the week's numbers.** This is not a prompt yet. It is Marcus opening the ticket system and the spreadsheet and pulling the numbers for the week: tickets opened, tickets closed, and the handful of ones still open. He writes them into a blank note. This is the raw material the rest of the workflow runs on. It takes him five minutes, and it does not need a tool, because the tool cannot see the ticket system.

**Step two: condense the notes.** He takes the pile of support notes he has been jotting all week and runs his saved summary prompt, the one that turns a jumble into a clean internal summary for the team. The input is his raw notes. The output is a tight paragraph or two. [^2] He reads it to make sure it captured the important cases, and it becomes the backbone of the next step.

**Step three: draft the status update.** He runs his saved status-update prompt, feeding it the numbers from step one and the summary from step two. The prompt asks for an update under 150 words that states the tickets closed, flags the two most important issues, and ends with one clear question he wants answered. This step does the writing he used to do from memory every Friday. [^2]

**Step four: the check, then send.** Now the workflow reaches the step that matters. He reads the draft against his what-to-check note from the library: every number must match the data he pulled, the two flagged issues must come from his notes and not the tool's imagination, and there must be no invented trend or positive spin his notes do not support. [^3] He fixes anything wrong, adds the judgment note he wants leadership to see, and sends. Five minutes, and it is the step that makes the whole thing safe.

That is the entire workflow: pull the numbers, condense the notes, draft the update, check and send. The handoffs are the point. Step one feeds step three directly. Step two feeds step three too. By the time Marcus starts step three, he is not thinking about his week at all. The tool is doing the drafting, and he is doing the checking.

The result is worth the numbers. Before, that Friday update took him the better part of an hour of careful writing and re-reading, and it came out different quality week to week depending on how much time he had. Now it takes him 45 minutes total, and most of that is the check, because the drafting is a saved prompt he already knows produces a shape he approves. [^2] The setup cost was the fifteen minutes it took him to write the order down once. It paid for itself the first Friday.

## How to build your own weekly workflow

You do not need to design anything clever. You need five steps, and the first three take one sitting.

**Step one: pick a day and a slot.** Choose the day the bulk of your recurring work lands and block a timebox, 30 to 45 minutes. Friday afternoon works well for most people, because it is when the week's summaries and closeouts happen. The schedule is the whole game, so be specific: "Friday 3:00" beats "at some point this week."

**Step two: choose two or three recurring tasks you already run through a tool.** Look at your list from the Lesson 18 audit and your library from Lesson 19. Pick the tasks that are rule-based, have clear inputs and outputs, and already have a saved prompt or are close to it. [^1] [^2] Two or three is plenty to start. A workflow you actually run beats a perfect one you skip.

**Step three: order them so each feeds the next.** Arrange the steps so the output of one becomes the input of the next. Pull the data first, then run the tasks that use it. Write the order down as a checklist, with each step's input and output named. This written order is what you will follow every week without re-deciding.

**Step four: write the check for the end.** Add one final step that is you verifying the output: which numbers to confirm, which facts must come from your own notes, what a wrong answer looks like. This is the what-to-check note from your library, applied to the whole workflow. [^2] [^3] Write it down. It is the step that keeps the routine safe.

**Step five: run it once, time it, then adjust.** Do the full workflow this week and count what it actually took, not what you hoped. If it ran over the timebox, cut a step or tighten a prompt. If a handoff was unclear, fix it. If a step produced nothing useful, drop it. The workflow is a living thing, like the library, and the first run is where you find out what it should actually be. [^2]

## Where a workflow helps, and where it does not

A weekly workflow is the right tool for a specific kind of work, and knowing the boundary keeps you from forcing it where it does not belong. It shines on the recurring, rule-based tasks that make up the mechanical shell of your job: weekly summaries, meeting-note condensation, status updates, repetitive writing, pulling and formatting the same numbers every period. Those have stable shapes and clear handoffs, which is exactly what a fixed sequence can handle. [^1]

It does not help with the tasks that need fresh judgment every time. A workflow that automates the judgment part of your job is a trap, because the judgment is where the value lives and where the mistakes are expensive. The supplier question that depends on context, the call on whether to waive a fee, the intro paragraph that sets the tone for a newsletter, those do not belong in a fixed routine. [^1] Lesson 17 made this point for AI in general, and it applies to workflows with extra force: a workflow hides the judgment inside a tidy-looking sequence, so it is easy to forget the check. [^4]

Two more honest limits. A workflow is not automation that runs without you. You are still in the loop at the check, and that is the point, not a failure. [^3] And a workflow should never carry sensitive data. The prompts in the sequence are templates with blanks, exactly as Lesson 9 taught, and the real data gets filled in at run time and stays where your company's rules say it belongs. [^5]

## Try it

This exercise takes about thirty minutes and gets you a real, runnable workflow.

First, build a two-step starter. Pick a day and a 30-minute slot. Take one recurring task you already run through a tool and one saved prompt you already trust, and write a two-step order: the step that prepares the input, then the prompt that uses it. Add a written check for the output. Run it once this week and time it.

Second, get a second opinion on the design. A good workflow is an opinionated order, and it is easy to miss a step or a handoff when you design it alone. Paste this into any tool, along with your list of recurring weekly tasks and their rough order:

> Here is a list of tasks I repeat weekly, roughly in the order I do them: [your list]. For each task, tell me whether it needs a human check on the output or can run on its own. Then propose a simple weekly workflow that puts two or three of these in a fixed order, naming for each step what the input is, which of my saved prompts it uses, and what I should check before the output leaves my hands. Keep it under five steps and under an hour.

Read the answer the way earlier lessons taught you. The tool is a helpful colleague with good instincts and no idea about your actual week. Its order is a starting point, and the check steps it suggests are where your judgment belongs. [^3] Keep only what fits your real routine.

## What's next

You now have a small weekly routine that runs itself with you at the check. The next step is deciding how much of it to hand over entirely, and the next lesson is exactly that: low-risk ways to start automating, and when to stop. Lesson 21 shows you where it is safe to let a tool run a step without you watching every time, what to automate first so the risk stays low, and the signs that you have automated too much and should pull a step back. That is the natural follow-on, because a workflow you run weekly is the perfect test bed for deciding which of its steps earn their own automation and which should stay with you.

For now, build the starter workflow. Pick the day, choose the two tasks, order them so they feed each other, write the check, and run it once. You are not adding automation for its own sake. You are taking the tasks you already identified, the prompts you already saved, and giving them a home in your week where they happen the same way every time, leaving your judgment for the one step that matters.
