# Lesson 12: Editing and Reviewing AI's Writing: Human-in-the-Loop

By now this series has given you the pieces: what the machine is, how it invents facts, how to judge its output, how to write a good brief, and how to iterate on what comes back. This lesson is where those pieces turn into a daily habit. It is the moment the draft is on the screen and you have to decide what happens to it. The answer to that decision is one idea: you are the editor, the reviewer, and the reason anything ships.

Think about the name of this lesson. Human-in-the-loop is a technical term, but it describes something you already do every day. The tool produces. You decide. The "loop" is the cycle between them, and you are the loop. When the machine drafts and a human checks, that is the pattern working the way it is supposed to. When the machine drafts and nobody checks, the pattern has failed, and nobody named a single person who is responsible for the result.

This lesson gives you two distinct jobs to do with any AI draft, editing and reviewing, and shows you why confusing them is how mistakes get through.

## The words we are using

Define the terms first so the two jobs stay crisp.

**Human-in-the-loop (HITL)** is the name for a system where a human actively participates in the work of an automated one. IBM defines it directly: in the context of AI, it means humans are involved at some point in the AI workflow to ensure accuracy, safety, accountability, or ethical decision-making. [^1] Notice what the definition is really about. It is not about doing the work for the machine. It is about being present so that accuracy and accountability have an owner. The goal, as IBM puts it, is to get the efficiency of automation without sacrificing the precision, nuance, and ethical reasoning of human oversight. [^1]

**Editing** is the pass where you fix what is on the page. The draft exists, it is mostly right, and your job is to make it better: tighten a sentence, fix a tone, cut a section, correct a number, sharpen a claim. Editing assumes the draft is close and you are refining it.

**Reviewing** is the pass where you check before anything ships. You are not improving prose, you are asking whether this is safe to send, true to verify, and appropriate to release. Reviewing assumes the draft might be wrong in ways that matter, and it is where errors get caught.

**Generative and transformative content** are the two things AI tools do with language, and they set up both jobs. Generative content is new text created from a prompt, like "write a sonnet about a cat." Transformative content modifies or improves existing text, like summarizing, translating, or rephrasing a paragraph into a different tone. [^2] Editing and reviewing apply to both, and they apply differently depending on which one you are looking at.

**Provenance** is where a piece of information came from. It matters because the hardest thing to review is a claim with no origin, a number with no source, a fact with no home. When you cannot say where something came from, you cannot check it, and you are back to trusting the tool's confident voice.

Hold those words. Human-in-the-loop is the posture, editing and reviewing are the two jobs, and provenance is what you look for while you do them.

## The core idea: you are the loop

Here is the shift this whole lesson rests on. The tool is not the author of the thing you send. You are. The machine produces a draft, and you take responsibility for what happens to that draft, because you are the human in the loop, and accountability is the whole point of having one. [^1]

That sounds like a platitude until you feel what changes. When you treat the AI as the author, your job is to forward what it made. When you treat yourself as the author with a fast drafting assistant, your job is to own the result, to know what it says, to be able to defend it, and to be the one who checks it before it goes anywhere. That second framing is the difference between someone who uses AI well and someone who lets AI make their mistakes for them.

The tool supports this posture if you use it right. IBM points out that AI content tools can brainstorm, draft, and produce at speed and scale, and that the same content "can lack originality, creativity and emotional depth," which is why guidelines and careful oversight have to be in place. [^2] That oversight is you. The speed is the tool's gift. The depth, the accuracy, and the decision about whether it is good enough are yours, and those are the parts that do not transfer.

## Two jobs, and why mixing them up hurts

Editing and reviewing are both part of being in the loop, and people mix them up all the time. They read a draft, fix a sentence here and a phrase there, feel good about "editing" it, and ship it. That was editing only. The reviewing pass never happened, and the error that mattered sailed through underneath the sentence they tightened.

Keep the two jobs separate on purpose, because they have different goals and different failure modes.

**Editing is a craft pass.** You are making the draft better. You cut the wordy opener, you fix the passive sentence, you align the tone with the audience, you drop in the verified number. Editing improves quality. It does not establish safety.

**Reviewing is a gate.** You are deciding whether this thing should go anywhere at all. You check the facts, you check the claims against what you know, you check whether the draft answers the actual question, and you check whether it represents your organization responsibly. Reviewing establishes safety. It does not improve prose.

A draft can be beautifully edited and completely wrong. A draft can be rough and entirely correct. The first one is the dangerous one, because it looks done. That is why the reviewing pass has to come after the editing pass, and why it has to be a separate act of attention, not a skim that happens while you are already proud of the fixes.

## The editing pass: making the draft better

Editing is the friendlier job, so do it first. You have a draft, you have decided it is the right approach, and now you make it read the way you would write it.

Start with substance, then structure, then sentences. Substance first because it is the most important and the easiest to skip. Does the draft contain everything it needs and nothing that should not be there? Cut the filler, add the missing point, correct the wrong emphasis. Then structure: does it open where the reader needs it to, flow in a sensible order, and end with the right note? Then sentences: are they varied, specific, and in your voice rather than the tool's flat, even rhythm?

For tone, the tool is an easy lever. You can ask it directly to rewrite in a warmer, more formal, or more direct register, and it will comply. [^2] But remember that a tone rewrite changes the surface, not the substance. The same IBM guidance that celebrates how easily the tool can rephrase also warns that AI-generated text can lack the depth and emotional nuance a human brings. [^2] So use the tool for the surface pass and supply the nuance yourself. If the draft reads slightly hollow, slightly too even, that is the cue to put some of your own voice back into it.

When you edit, change one thing at a time and keep track of what you changed, the habit from the iteration lesson. [^3] You want to know which edit moved the draft, because that is how you learn what the tool drafts badly and what you will always have to fix yourself.

## The reviewing pass: checking before it ships

Reviewing is the gate, and it is the job that protects you. Do it second, and do it as a deliberate pass.

Read the draft against your actual question first. The tool sometimes answers the question it guessed you meant, not the one you asked, and a well-edited answer to the wrong question is still the wrong answer. Then check the claims. Every number, name, date, and reference in the draft is a claim that could be wrong, and the tool is capable of producing specific, confident, invented ones. [^4] For anything that matters, find the source and read the number there, the lateral reading habit from the literacy lesson. [^5]

Check the provenance. For every claim you are about to ship, can you say where it came from? If it is a fact about your own company, your own project, your own customer, the tool does not have access to that world, and the claim is a guess unless you verified it. [^5] If it is a number, a regulation, or a named reference, it needs a real source you can point to. If you cannot say where it came from, it is not ready to ship.

Finally, check whether the draft represents you responsibly. Would you put your name on it? Could you defend every sentence if asked? This is the accountability part of being in the loop. [^1] If you would hesitate, fix it, or drop it, but do not ship it because the tool wrote it and it sounded sure.

## Worked example: the project update

Put both passes together in a scene you will recognize. You need to send a short project update to a stakeholder, and you have used an AI assistant to draft it. The draft reads well. It is the kind of thing that, in the past, you would have skimmed and forwarded.

Here is the draft: "The migration project is progressing well and remains on schedule. We have completed the core data transfer and are now in the final testing phase. Customer-facing impact has been minimal, and we expect to complete ahead of the September 30 target date."

Start with editing, the craft pass. The draft is decent, and it is also a little flat and a little generic. "Progressing well" is filler, "remain on schedule" does no work, and the whole thing reads like a template, which is exactly what it is, the statistically most ordinary version of "write a positive project update." [^2] You tighten it: cut the filler, name the phase you are actually in, and say the one thing a stakeholder cares about, which is whether the date holds.

Now the reviewing pass, the gate. This is where the draft becomes dangerous, because nothing in it is obviously wrong, and it is well written enough to forward on autopilot. Read it against what you actually know. Is the migration really on schedule? Do you have a source for "customer-facing impact has been minimal," or is that the tool's optimistic guess? What is the real status of testing? If you are not certain of those claims, they are not verified, and a confident sentence from the tool does not make them true. [^4]

So you check. You open the project tracker and confirm the phase. You check the customer-impact claim against the ticket log. You confirm the September 30 date against the plan, because the tool has no access to your project and can only predict what an on-schedule update sounds like. [^5]

You keep the sentences you tightened. You correct anything that does not match what you found. And you only ship what survived the reviewing gate, the parts you can defend. That is the whole lesson in one scene: the tool gave you a fast, confident, plausible draft, editing made it read better, and reviewing made it safe to send. The skill was never the drafting. It was you.

## Try it

This exercise takes about ten minutes and it forces you to do both passes separately, which is the point.

Take a piece of writing you genuinely need to produce this week, an email, a short report, a project update, anything with a few factual claims in it. Ask an AI assistant to draft it.

First, edit it. Do a full craft pass: tighten the prose, fix the tone, cut the filler, vary the rhythm. Do not check any facts yet. Just make it read better. Time yourself and write down how long the edit took and what you changed.

Then stop, reset, and review it. Read the edited draft as if you had never seen it and someone else was asking you to approve it. List every factual claim it makes. For each one, can you name the source? Go check the ones that matter, the numbers, the statuses, the names. Ask yourself whether you would put your name on the result and defend each sentence.

Here is the answer to the exercise, what you should notice. The editing pass feels natural and goes fast, because making text better is a skill you already have. The reviewing pass is where the work actually happens, and it is the pass people skip. Almost every dangerous AI error, the invented stat, the wrong status, the confident guess about your own project, survives editing and dies in reviewing, because editing never asked whether the claim was true. If your draft contained a claim you could not source, you found the exact point where being in the loop paid for itself. [^1] [^4] The habit to keep is the two-pass separation. Edit for quality, then review for safety, and never let a well-edited draft convince you it was also well-reviewed.

## What's next

You now have the two jobs that make every other AI skill safe to use. Editing is how you turn a draft into something that reads like you. Reviewing is how you keep a confident, plausible draft from shipping an error, and together they are what human-in-the-loop actually means, a human present to own the result. The next lesson moves one step earlier in the process. Instead of judging or fixing the tool's output, it is about using the tool to do the reading and comparing for you: research and synthesis, how to read sources, compare what they say, and extract the useful bit without importing the tool's errors. It is the same skill of keeping yourself in the loop, applied at the front of a task instead of the back. For now, hold the habit from today. Edit for quality, review for safety, keep the two passes separate, and never ship a claim you cannot point to a source for.
