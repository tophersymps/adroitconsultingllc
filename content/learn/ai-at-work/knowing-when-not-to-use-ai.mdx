# Lesson 17: Knowing When NOT to Use AI

By now this series has shown you the bright side of the tool: it drafts fast, it synthesizes reading, it cleans up spreadsheets, it stress-tests plans. Lesson 16 gave you the loop for making it a genuine thinking partner. This lesson is the mirror image, and it is the one people skip. Knowing when not to use AI is a skill, not a surrender. The person who gets the most out of these tools is not the one who uses them the most. It is the one who uses them on the right tasks and keeps them away from the wrong ones.

The reason this is a skill and not a rule is that the answer is never automatic. AI is not good or bad in the abstract. It is good or bad for a specific task in a specific situation. A tool that produces a brilliant first draft of a memo is the wrong tool for a decision that needs your accountability behind it. The same assistant that saves you an hour on a spreadsheet is a liability when you need a number that must be exactly right and you have no way to check it. The skill is the filter that tells you which case you are in, and it takes about fifteen seconds to run.

## The words we are using

Let me pin down the terms before the filter, because the whole lesson rests on a few distinctions.

**A task** is a piece of work with an outcome you want: write a status update, compare three vendors, decide whether to approve a request. Every task has two properties that decide whether AI fits: what the output is for, and how you will know it is right.

**Delegation** is handing a task, or part of a task, to something else to do. The Future of Life Institute's Human Control principle puts the rule plainly: humans should choose how and whether to delegate decisions to AI systems. [^1] The key word is choose. Delegation is not an accident and not a default. It is a decision you make on purpose, task by task.

**Accountability** is the answer to the question "who is responsible if this goes wrong?" It is yours, even when a tool did the work. The tool cannot be accountable for anything. It has no reputation, no job, no license, no relationship to protect. You do. So whenever a task ends in something you own, the accountability stays with you, and that single fact drives most of this lesson.

**Verifiability** is how easy it is to check that the output is correct. A task is verifiable when you can confirm the answer against a source you trust. It is unverifiable when you cannot, either because the thing does not exist yet or because you have nothing to check it against.

**Cost** is not just money. It is your time, the risk of a wrong answer, the effort of checking, and the downside if the tool is confidently wrong. The real question is never "can the tool do this?" It is "does the tool do this better and more cheaply than the alternative, once you count the checking?"

## Why the default is not "use it"

Here is the shift this lesson asks for. Most people start from "can I use AI for this?" and stop when the answer is yes. The better question is "should I?" The two are different, and the first one will mislead you, because the answer to "can I" is almost always yes. The tool can produce text or a list or a draft for nearly anything you describe. That tells you nothing about whether you should use it.

Start instead from what the tool actually is. It is a text predictor, not a truth machine. It has no memory of your company, no access to your files, no way to look anything up, and no stake in whether its answer is right. It produces the most plausible next words given what you typed. [^2] That is an excellent engine for some tasks and the wrong engine entirely for others.

So the useful framing is not "what can AI do" but "what does this specific task need, and does AI supply it?" When the task needs speed and range of ideas, the tool is hard to beat. When the task needs your private context, a verifiable fact, a decision you must own, or a human relationship, the tool is missing exactly what the task requires. The filter below is how you tell those apart before you waste the effort.

## The filter: four questions before you hand a task to AI

Run these four questions before you delegate. If any of them gives you pause, keep the task, or keep a human firmly in the loop. This is the whole lesson compressed.

**Does it need your context?** The tool does not know your customer, your project, your company's numbers, or your relationship with the person on the other end. If the task depends on any of that, the tool is working blind. It will produce a confident, plausible answer built from the average case, and your situation is never the average case. Lesson 6 showed you the danger: the tool states a figure about your own company with complete confidence, because a plausible answer about a healthy business has a rising number, and it cannot know what your actual number is. [^3] If the task needs facts or context only you have, the tool cannot supply the core of it.

**Does a wrong answer cost a lot?** Some tasks fail safely. A brainstorm that misses a few options is no disaster. A draft that needs light editing costs you a few minutes. But when a wrong answer is expensive, embarrassing, or dangerous, the bar for trusting the tool goes way up. The worst case is a task where being wrong is costly and the output is unverifiable: you cannot check it, and if it is wrong you pay a real price. That combination is a hard "do not delegate."

**Do you have to own and explain it?** If the outcome is a decision or a statement that someone can hold you to, the accountability is yours no matter who or what produced it. Stanford's research on AI in government agencies makes this concrete. Agencies have to explain their decisions, and the authors note that such explanations become increasingly difficult as algorithmic systems displace human judgment. [^4] Your manager and your client are no different. When you submit a decision or a commitment, you are the one who has to explain it. The tool cannot stand in front of your stakeholder. So when a task ends in something you own, keep the judgment in your hands even if a tool helped you think.

**Does it need a person?** Some work is not about producing output at all. It is about a relationship, trust, empathy, or judgment that a person has to deliver and a person has to receive. You can have a tool draft the words, and Lesson 11 showed you how useful that is for tone and structure. But the moment of delivering bad news, resolving a conflict, negotiating a sensitive point, or reading the room, that is yours. The human in the loop is not a formality there. It is the point of the task.

## The two cases where AI earns its place anyway

The filter has two important exceptions, and naming them keeps this honest instead of lopsided.

First, most real tasks are mixed. The deciding piece of a task may need you, while the surrounding work is exactly what the tool is good at. You should not hand the whole task to the tool, but you also should not refuse the tool entirely. The draftable part, the wording, the structure, the first pass, can go to the tool. The decidable part, the fact you must verify, the choice you must own, stays with you. Lesson 6 called this the draftable versus decidable split. [^3] Use the tool for the draftable half, keep the decidable half for yourself.

Second, delegation is not the same as handing over the outcome. You can use the tool heavily and still not have delegated the decision, as long as you check what matters. Lesson 6's habit is the model: trust the draft for the language, verify the claim, and only ship what survives the check. [^3] The tool can be a drafting engine, a research assistant, and a stress-tester, and you can still be fully accountable for the result, because you ran the checks. That is the healthy relationship: the tool works, you decide, you own.

## The quiet cost nobody counts: your time and attention

There is a cost of using AI that almost nobody puts in the ledger, and it is the one that tips many tasks toward "do not use it." Every time you hand a task to the tool, you spend time writing the prompt, reading the output, and checking it. For a big task that saves you hours, that overhead is trivial. For a small task, the overhead can exceed the task itself.

Consider a two-line reply you could write in thirty seconds. By the time you prompt the tool, read its version, decide whether it matches what you meant, and fix the bits that do not, you have spent longer than just writing the reply. The tool did not save you time. It charged you for the privilege of doing the work twice. The same logic applies to anything where you already know the answer and the answer is easy. AI is not a default. It is a tool with a cost, and when the cost is higher than the benefit, the right call is to skip it.

This matters more than people think, because it is where the tool quietly makes you slower without you noticing. The novelty makes the overhead feel worth it. It rarely is, for small tasks. The filter question is blunt: if I can do this reliably in the time it takes to prompt, check, and fix, the tool loses. Do not use it.

## Worked example: the request you have to own

Let me put the filter on a real scene so it stops being abstract. You work in operations, and your manager forwards you a request from a client asking whether your team can commit to a specific delivery date, and what the cost implication would be if the date slips.

Your first instinct is to ask an AI assistant to draft the response. So run the filter.

Does it need your context? Completely. The delivery date depends on your team's current workload, the open dependencies, the person who owns the next milestone, and the commitments already made. None of that is in the tool. It does not know your capacity or your pipeline. Anything it says about a realistic date is a guess dressed up as an answer.

Does a wrong answer cost a lot? Enormously. This is a commitment to a client. If the date is wrong, the client plans around it, and you miss it, that is a real cost to a real relationship. This is exactly the high-cost, and it is also partly unverifiable, because the "right" date is not a fact to look up, it is a judgment about your team.

Do you have to own it? Yes, fully. You are the one the client will hold to that date. The tool cannot attend the follow-up call and explain why it gave the date it gave. You will.

Does it need a person? It is a client relationship. The words matter, and so does the person delivering them, because a commitment is a trust exchange, not a string of text.

So the filter says: do not hand this whole task to the tool. But here is the second half of the skill, the draftable part. The tool can absolutely help you write the response well. You gather the real date and the real slip cost from your own sources, you decide what you can commit to, and then you can use the assistant to tighten the wording, structure the email, and make the tone land. You kept the decidable part, the date, the commitment, the judgment, and you used the tool for the draftable part, the language. [^3] The task gets the benefit of the tool and none of the risk, because the risk lived in the part you kept.

That is the mature use of AI: know which half of a task the tool can help with, use it there, and keep the half that carries the consequence. The tool is at its best when it is a writing partner on a decision you have already made, not a replacement for making the decision.

## The habit, not the rule

Here is what I want you to take from this lesson, and it is deliberately not a rule list. There is no universal "AI is bad for these five things" that survives contact with a real job, because the boundaries move. A task that needs a person today, like a delicate client conversation, can be sensibly drafted by AI and delivered by you. A task that seems safe, like a routine internal number, can be catastrophic if the tool invents it and you do not check.

So the habit is the filter, run fast and honestly, on every task you are tempted to hand over. Does it need context you have and the tool lacks? Does a wrong answer cost a lot? Do you have to own and explain it? Does it need a person? And the quiet one: is the overhead of using it more than the task is worth? When the answer to any of these is yes, keep the task, keep the judgment, or keep a human firmly in the loop.

This is also what it means to stay accountable. NIST's AI Risk Management Framework treats oversight and documented limits as core to using AI responsibly, and it emphasizes that organizations need clear policies and procedures for human oversight of AI systems. [^5] The same principle holds for one person deciding whether to use a tool on one task. The human decides how and whether to delegate, and the human stays answerable for the result. [^1] That is not a constraint. It is the thing that makes the tool safe to use at all.

## Try it

This exercise takes about ten minutes and makes the filter reflexive. Take the last five tasks you delegated to an AI assistant at work. For each one, write down the task, then run the four filter questions quickly, and note whether the answer to any of them was a clear yes.

Now the harder part. For each task where the filter said yes, answer two questions. First, what was the actual cost of using the tool, counting the prompt, the reading, the checking, and the risk? Second, what part of the task did you keep, and what part did the tool actually do? In most cases you will find the tool was doing the draftable shell, and you were doing the decidable core. That is good, that is the healthy split. [^3]

Here is the answer to the exercise, the pattern to look for. You will probably find one or two tasks where you delegated more than you meant to, where the deciding piece, the thing that carried the consequence, quietly went to the tool without you checking it hard enough. That is the task to reclaim next time. And you will probably find one small task where the tool cost more than it saved, the two-line reply you spent four minutes on. That is the one to stop delegating. The filter is not about being anti-AI. It is about being deliberate, so the tool works for you instead of you working for it.

## What's next

You now have the other half of the skill: knowing when the tool is the wrong tool, when the task needs your context, your accountability, or a human relationship, and when the overhead of using AI is higher than the benefit. The filter, plus the draftable and decidable split from Lesson 6, is how you keep the tool in its lane. The next lesson flips the question around and makes it practical. Instead of asking when not to use AI, it asks how to find the tasks in your own job that are genuinely worth automating: the repetitive, rule-based, high-volume work that an AI tool, or a small automation, can take off your plate for good. That is the skill that turns this whole series into saved hours, so you can spend your judgment where it actually counts. For now, hold the habit from today. When you are about to delegate a task, run the filter. If it needs context you have, a fact you must own, or a person on the other end, keep the judgment yourself, and use the tool only for the part it is good at. [^1] [^3] [^5]
