# Lesson 21: Low-Risk Ways to Start Automating, and When to Stop

Lesson 20 gave you a weekly workflow. You picked two or three recurring tasks, ordered them so they feed each other, and stayed in the loop at the check. The result ran on time and you kept your judgment on the one step that mattered. That is the safe shape for most AI use, and it is a good place to settle. But there is a bigger question underneath it, and this lesson is about that question.

When does a step earn the right to run without you watching every time? Not a workflow that waits for you, but a step that fires on its own, drafts the output, and leaves you a note. That is automation, and it is genuinely different from a workflow. A workflow is a sequence of tasks that you run and check. Automation is a task that runs and checks itself, with you verifying less often or not at all. The difference is where your attention has to go. In a workflow, you are always at the check. In automation, you are trying to find the few steps where you can safely leave the check.

This is the point where a lot of people go wrong, and they go wrong in both directions. Some never automate anything, so the tool never fully earns its keep. More common, and more dangerous, is the person who automates everything at once, watches it quietly work for a week, and stops reading the output. That person has handed their judgment to a text predictor. [^1] The path through the middle is narrow, and it runs on a few simple rules.

## The words we are using

Let me pin down the terms, because the whole lesson rests on three distinctions.

**Automation** is a step that runs without you starting it and produces output without you watching. The trigger might be a schedule, a new document, or a message arriving. The point is that you are not in the loop for that step. **Human-in-the-loop**, the opposite, is a step that produces a draft and stops for a person to check before anything happens. [^2] The mental shift this lesson asks for is: automation is a step you deliberately promote from human-in-the-loop, one at a time, and demote just as freely.

**Reversible** describes an action whose effect you can undo, or that changes nothing until you approve it. A draft in a folder is reversible. A note you formatted is reversible. Sending an email, changing a record, approving a payment, and telling a client a date are not, because the other side of the transaction has seen it and cannot unsee it. 

**Blast radius** is how much damage a wrong output can do. A wrong draft of a status summary costs you five minutes of fixing. A wrong message sent to fifty people, or a wrong figure in a quote, costs a relationship. The same tool can have a tiny blast radius on one task and a huge one on another, so the radius belongs to the task, not the tool.

**Automation creep** is the slow, quiet growth of automation without a decision. You start with one reversible step, it works, you add the next, and eventually you stop noticing what is running. Creep is not a red flag by itself. Every step added carelessly is. The skill is making each addition a decision, not a default.

## The one rule that carries the lesson

Here it is, in a sentence. **Automate the reversible, keep the human on the irreversible.** [^3]

That is the whole filter, and almost every low-risk decision follows from it. Find the steps in your work whose output you can inspect and discard without anyone else being affected. Those are your automation candidates. Keep a human checkpoint on any step whose output leaves your hands, changes a shared record, commits to something, or needs your personal judgment. The reason is not that the tool is untrustworthy. It is that the cost of it being wrong is asymmetric. A reversible step that is wrong costs a little time. An irreversible step that is wrong costs trust, and trust is expensive to rebuild. [^1]

Two more rules sharpen it. First, automation is a gradual promotion, not a binary switch. Even the safest step starts with you checking every output, then every other one, then spot checking, before it truly runs on its own. The step earns its autonomy with a track record, not by declaration. [^3] Second, when a step is wrong and it is your job to own the result, the human checkpoint is not a compromise. It is the part of the work that is actually yours. Lesson 17 made the accountability point for AI in general, and automation is exactly where it bites. [^4]

## A worked example: the weekly progress summary

Let me make this concrete with a task nearly every team runs, so you can see which steps are safe to automate and which are not.

Say every Friday you produce a one-page progress summary for your team: what shipped, what is blocked, what is happening next week. Right now it takes you about an hour. You have the pieces, in scattered places: this week's meeting notes, your to-do list, a couple of documents others updated. You honestly think the tool could help, because the task is half gathering and half writing.

Step 1: collect the week's sources into one place. This is rule-based. The sources exist, the names are stable, there is nothing sensitive beyond your ordinary working documents, and if the tool pulled in a wrong note you would catch it in seconds. High-scope example, but strictly reversible: nothing is sent, nothing is changed, a wrong pickup costs a little cleanup. This step is a candidate.

Step 2: draft the summary from what you collected, grouped by project, with blockers and next steps called out. This is assisted drafting, exactly what the tool is good at, and it is the same move Lesson 20 used for a workflow. [^2] The output is a draft in a folder. Reversible. Another candidate.

Step 3: check the draft. Are the names right, the numbers right, the tone neutral, the blockers actually blockers and not opinions? This is your judgment, and it is the step that carries your name. It is not automatable, and it should not be. If you automate this, you have stopped doing your job.

Step 4: send it to the team. Not reversible. Not up for automation. 

So the low-risk move is to automate steps 1 and 2, behind a checkbox on step 3, and leave 4 with you. You spend the hour less, because the gathering and the first draft happen while you are elsewhere, and your time goes to the check and the send. That is automation earning its place: it removes predictable work without hiding risk. [^3]

Notice what did not happen. Nobody connected the tool to the distribution list. Nobody let it decide which blocker matters. The irreversible parts and the judgment parts stayed human. That is the difference between automating the boring part of a job and automating the job.

## A scorecard for any step

You do not need to keep the worked example in mind to apply this. Run every candidate step through three questions, and the answer falls out. Paste this into any tool if it helps you think, but it is really just three questions.

1. Is the input stable and safe? Do the same inputs come in the same shape each time, free of sensitive or regulated data that should not be in the tool? A step fed by varied, opinionated, or protected input is a poor candidate. [^1]
2. Is the output easily checkable against a source I trust? Can I tell in a glance whether it is right, and if it is wrong, is it a quick fix? If checking takes as long as doing it by hand, automation has saved you nothing. [^3]
3. Could I undo it? If it is wrong, does it touch anyone else, change a record, or commit to something? The more irreversible, the more a human must stay in the loop. [^3]

Three yeses and a reversible output: automate it, still starting with a checkpoint and relaxing it on a track record. Three yeses but the output goes out or commits to something: automate the draft, keep the human on the action. Anything else, or any sensitive data: leave it human-in-the-loop.

## When to stop

The "when to stop" half is the half nobody teaches, and it matters more. Automation is not self-correcting. The tool does not know when its old assumptions no longer hold, and it will keep producing confident output long after the situation changed. That confident-wrong combination is the danger Lesson 6 warned you about, and automation makes it worse, because the output arrives without you asking and without you in the room. [^5]

Three signs tell you you have automated too much, and you should pull a step back.

First, you stop reading the output. This is the real name for automation bias: the human tendency to favour the machine's recommendation over your own judgment, which grows the longer the machine keeps being right. [^5] The cure is a forced check. If a step's output can go out without you actually reading it, that step is automated past the point of safety. The moment you catch yourself approving without reading, demote it back to human-in-the-loop.

Second, checking costs more than doing. If the step produces output you have to scrub, correct, or rework more than you would have spent doing it by hand, the automation is net negative. Measure the whole loop, not just the part that got faster. An automation that saves ten minutes of drafting but adds twenty of correction is not an improvement. [^3]

Third, the judgment crept in. You look at the output one Friday and realize the tool is deciding something you used to decide. It ranks the blockers, or picks the tone, or chooses which update leads. Each of those is a small decision, and they migrated without a vote. Any step where your call is the actual value is a step that should come back to you. [^4]

When any of these shows up, you do not have to tear the whole thing down. You pull back the one offending step. Automation should be as easy to demote as it was to promote. The people who get the most from these tools are not the ones who automate the most. They are the ones who automate the reversible parts, check the rest, and are willing to undo a step the moment it stops being worth it. [^1]

## Try it

Take the workflow you built in Lesson 20 and run its steps through the scorecard. Write each step as its own line, then answer the three questions for each: input stable and safe, output easily checkable, output reversible. Score them honestly, one of them is very likely a no on the third question.

Pick the step with the strongest score and a reversible output, and automate just that one, behind a checkpoint. The easiest safe shape is a scheduled run that drafts and stops. Here is a prompt you can adapt:

> Every [day of week] at [time], gather [list your sources] and draft [describe the deliverable]. Save it in [your folder] and send me a one-line summary of what you produced. Do not send it anywhere, do not share it, and do not take any action. Just draft and notify. I will review before anything is used.

Run it for one week and time yourself against your old way of doing it. Here is the number that matters, and be honest about it: does the check plus any corrections take less total time than doing the step by hand? If yes, the automation is earning its place, keep it and consider the next step. If no, or if you found yourself approving without reading, reverse it. You are not failing. You found a step that should stay with you, and that is the whole point of the exercise. [^3]

## What's next

You now have the personal side of automation, and the discipline: start reversible, keep the human on judgment, pull a step back the moment it stops earning its place. The next lesson moves from your desk to the team. Lesson 22 is about building a shared team playbook and a shared prompt library, so the working prompts you have refined on your own become a common resource the whole team uses consistently instead of thirty people each building their own. That is the natural follow-on, because once you trust a prompt on your own tasks, the question becomes how to trust it on everyone's. For now, score one step, automate it behind a checkpoint, and watch the number. Automation that saves you time you would have spent on the same task is good. Automation that lets you stop checking is how mistakes get quiet.
