# Lesson 57: The Optimization Loop: Turning Analytics into Prompt, Topic, and Action Changes

An agent goes live for a retail service team and the first week looks clean. By the third week the Quality Score has slid and escalations are climbing, so the platform team does the thing most teams do first: it rewrites the agent's instructions, broadly, everywhere, and waits. Nothing moves. The reason is not the wording. Performance Insights shows the failures cluster under a single intent, Agent Optimization shows the answer text is fine but the wrong Subagent handled it, and the instructions were never the problem. Three weeks of prompt editing went into a routing defect that a topic change would have closed in an afternoon.

That is the failure this lesson is built to prevent, and it is the most common one in production Agentforce work. Optimization is not the act of changing something. It is a loop that starts with reading what actually moved, then localizing it, then reading the session, and only then choosing the smallest change that fixes the observed defect. Teams that skip the reading step edit by feel and burn sprints. Teams that run the loop fix the right thing on the first pass.

This lesson serves requirement go-2c, "Explain agent analytics and agent optimization," in the Governance and Observability domain, which carries 10 percent of the exam. The exam does not ask you to name a dashboard. It hands you a symptom and asks which lever you pull, and the whole objective resolves on one habit: locate the failure in the trace, and the step type names the lever. By the end you can read Agent Analytics without confusing the metric families, localize a trend to a subagent or an intent, read a trace to find the failing step, choose between instructions, topics, and actions with a reason, and time your judgment to the pipeline instead of to the calendar.

## Deep Dive

### Optimization is a loop, not a single change

The loop has an order, and every step leaves evidence for the next. A metric moves against the prior period in Agent Analytics. You break that metric down to the subagent, intent, or action behind it in Performance Insights. You open the sessions for that segment in Agent Optimization and read the trace to find the exact step that failed. You choose one of three levers. You make the change. You retest the failing scenarios in Testing Center. You commit and activate a new version, which deactivates the old one, and then you watch the metric again. A scenario question on this objective hands you a symptom at one point in that loop and asks what you do next, so knowing the order is what keeps you from changing the wrong thing.

The loop closes on measurement discipline, and this is the part teams get wrong most often. After you change a lever, you retest the failing scenarios before you commit, then activate the new version. Then you wait. Sessions are processed and quality scored after they close, on the order of four to five hours, and intents are clustered by a weekly pipeline that forms a cluster only once a tag accumulates at least ten matching intents. Judging an optimization the day it ships is how a team concludes that a good fix failed, because the data that would show the improvement has not finished processing yet. A change you cannot measure is a guess, and the dashboard is the only thing that turns a guess into a fix. Alongside the dashboards, Agent Health Monitoring watches for silent failures such as spiking error rates and dropping response rates, and its alerts are evaluated continuously against a recent window so a spike reaches you before it compounds into customer impact.[^5]

### Read the metric before you touch the mechanism

Agent Analytics aggregates performance across all sessions and surfaces trends in dashboards powered by Tableau Next, and its tabs group metrics by the question they answer.[^1] Effectiveness covers deflection, escalation, abandonment, engagement, success, and task resolution rates. Usage covers unique sessions, interactions, users, and average interactions per session. Quality Scores cover the Quality Score itself along with Answer Faithfulness, Answer Relevance, and Context Relevance. Health covers agent interaction latency, session duration, and error rate. Trust covers Adherence Response Rate and the average agent toxicity score. Voice covers interruption rate for voice-enabled agents, and User Satisfaction covers thumbs up and thumbs down on employee agents.[^2] Your first move is always to confirm which dimension and which metric actually moved, and against what baseline. Worth knowing before you build: installing an analytics app does not configure optimization, and the two are set up and permissioned separately, which is why a team can have populated dashboards and still open an empty session view.[^4]

Different metric families point at different levers, which is why reading the metric comes first. Success Rate measures whether interactions completed their action steps without errors, so a fall there implicates actions. Adherence Response Rate measures whether the agent follows instructions and stays inside content bounds, so a fall there implicates instructions. Quality Score, Answer Faithfulness, Answer Relevance, and Context Relevance come out of the retrieval and feedback layers, so a quality-only fall with clean action steps usually implicates grounding or wording rather than integration. Read the metric family and you have already narrowed the lever before you open a single session.

![A table mapping each Agent Analytics tab to what it measures and the lever it points at, from Effectiveness and actions through Trust and instructions](</diagrams/day-57-go-2c-the-optimization-loop-turning-analytics-into-promp/3-metric-family-to-lever.png>)

Hold the period-over-period view while you are here, because a metric with no baseline is not a signal. Analytics compares each card against the prior period, so a Success Rate of ninety-four percent means one thing when it was ninety-six percent the week before and something else entirely when it was eighty percent. The first question a consultant asks is not whether the number looks good, it is whether the number changed, by how much, and in which segment. A dashboard reviewed without a baseline produces opinions rather than investigations, and an investigation is the only thing that leads anywhere useful.

### Narrow the trend to a subagent or an intent before you open a session

Performance Insights is the step between a trend and a session, and its whole job is to keep you from reviewing sessions at random. In its Breakdowns area you select Subagents, Intents, or Actions, choose a segment and a metric, and compare the data across segments.[^2] If Escalation Rate is up, the breakdown tells you whether escalations cluster under one subagent or spread across all of them. A cluster is a scoped defect and points at one topic or one action. A spread usually points at a systemic instruction or knowledge problem shared by every path.

Treat that breakdown as triage. You are deciding where to spend your session-reading time, and a populated product will always have more sessions than you can read. The breakdown tells you which handful matter. A single intent carrying most of the bad outcomes is a different problem from every intent carrying a little, and it leads to a different lever.

When a breakdown points at an intent rather than at a subagent, the fastest next step is to filter the session list by that intent and then by its cluster. Cluster tags group the several ways users phrase the same request, so once a tag accumulates enough matching intents the platform groups those sessions together, and you can see every conversation where users asked for the same thing in different words. That is the difference between fixing one session and fixing a pattern, and a pattern is what actually moves a headline metric. Reviewing sessions one by one will always be slower and will always miss the systematic failure hiding behind three phrasings of the same question.

### The trace names the lever before you change anything

Agent Optimization gives you the session-level view that Analytics cannot. Processed Sessions carry an extracted intent, a cluster tag, and a quality score, and the platform makes them available several hours after session close. Unprocessed Sessions are available immediately, and so is the Trace tab, so you never have to wait for intent processing to start debugging.[^1] The Interaction Summary tab for a selected intent lists the subagents triggered, the actions triggered, the quality score, and an LLM-generated explanation of why that score was assigned, which often states the defect in plain language before you open the trace. The Trace tab itself is the time-ordered record of every processing step: subagent identification, action selection, routing, variable updates, retrieval, and response generation, each with a status indicator and timing, with nested steps you expand when a subagent is delegated to in a multi-agent design.[^1] Those tools all sit in one workspace: Agent Analytics, Agent Optimization, the Interaction Summary, and the Trace view share the observe and optimize area of Agentforce Studio, so the path from a trend to a step is short by design.[^3]

When two levers could explain the same symptom, the trace settles it, and this one fact answers most of the scenario questions on the objective. A wrong subagent with a good answer is a topic fix. A right subagent with a weak answer is an instruction fix. A right route and a fine answer with a failed step is an action fix. Ask what exactly failed in the step list, and the step type names the lever. That reasoning is what the exam rewards over any product name, because it is the judgment a consultant actually applies when an agent underperforms in production.

![A decision tree that reads the trace: wrong subagent with a fine answer points to topics, right subagent with a weak answer points to instructions, and a failed or stalled step points to actions](</diagrams/day-57-go-2c-the-optimization-loop-turning-analytics-into-promp/2-trace-to-lever.png>)

Walk a concrete case end to end. Escalation Rate climbs, and the breakdown shows the increase is concentrated in one subagent that handles order status. You open a handful of those sessions and read the Interaction Summary: the quality score is low and the reasoning names a missed instruction. You open the trace for one of them and the step list is clean, with the correct subagent identified, the correct action selected, and no error, but the response generation step produces an answer that omits a detail the instruction block asked for. Nothing in the step list failed, which rules out the action lever, and the route was correct, which rules out the topic lever. The evidence is a correct route plus a weak answer, so the lever is that subagent's instructions. You adjust the wording, retest the failing scenarios in Testing Center, commit the new version, and then give the pipeline its several hours before you check whether the metric moved. That sequence is the loop in miniature, and it is the sequence a scenario question is asking you to reconstruct.

### Lever one is instructions: fix the wording, not the route

Instructions are the lever when the interactions show the correct subagent firing but the answers are off in tone, detail, or adherence. A falling Quality Score with correct retrieval, or a falling Adherence Response Rate, points here. The fix is to edit that subagent's instructions and retest the wording in Testing Center before you commit. What makes this lever easy to misuse is that it is the most familiar: editing text feels like progress. So the discipline is negative. Do not widen a topic to fix a wording problem, because the routing is already right and widening it can pull in requests that belong elsewhere. Do not rewrite the prompt to fix an action that returns an error, because the prompt is not what failed in that case. The evidence that you are in the instruction lever is a correct route plus a weak answer.

### Lever two is topics: fix the scope the router reads

Topics, by which the platform now means the subagents and the scope you give them, are lever two. If sessions escalate because the agent never routes a request to the subagent that owns it, or routes it to the wrong one, that is a classification and scope problem rather than a language problem. The fix is to add a subagent, sharpen its description so the router selects it, or move an action under the correct topic. The tell is a cluster of untagged or misclassified intents. The signal is the Interaction Summary showing the wrong subagent selected while the answer text itself is fine, which is the mirror image of the instruction case and the thing the opening scene of this lesson got wrong.

### Lever three is actions: a failed step is not a conversation failure

Actions are lever three, and the trace is unambiguous about when you are here. If Success Rate falls or Error Rate rises and the trace shows an error step or a stalled step inside an action, the problem is the action or its inputs rather than the conversation. An action runs under the running user's security context, so a missing permission fails it at run time regardless of how good the instructions are.[^1] Check the action's inputs, its backend, and the running user's permissions, then optimize by tightening the input mapping or fixing the integration. Do not tune the prompt to cover for an action failure, because a wording change cannot grant a permission or repair an integration. The error step in the trace is your evidence that the action, and not the language, is the cause.

## Exam Traps

Three traps dominate this objective. The first is believing any quality dip is a prompt problem. A failed action step or a wrong retrieval needs an action or a knowledge fix, and editing the instructions hides the real cause while leaving the defect live, so the correct answer is the lever the failed step points at rather than the lever that feels most familiar. The second trap is believing an optimization can be judged the day it ships. Sessions are processed and scored after they close and intents are clustered weekly, so the dashboard lags the change, and an answer that declares a fix successful on day one has misread a pipeline delay as a result. The third trap is believing an action failure is a conversation failure. An action runs under the running user's security context, so a missing permission fails it at run time regardless of how well the instructions are written, and the answer that rewrites the prompt is treating a permissions problem as a language problem.

## What a consultant should walk away with

Carry the loop and the trace rule as one habit. Read the metric family first, because Success Rate points at actions, Adherence Response Rate points at instructions, and the quality and retrieval metrics point at grounding or wording. Localize in Performance Insights before you open sessions. Then read the trace and ask what failed in the step list: a wrong subagent means topics, a weak answer means instructions, and a failed step means actions. Make the smallest change that fits, retest the failing scenarios in Testing Center, commit and activate a new version, and then give the pipeline its several hours and its weekly clustering pass before you judge whether the metric moved. A consultant who runs that sequence fixes the right defect once. A consultant who edits by feel fixes the wrong defect repeatedly.
