Data governance gets a bad reputation in architecture conversations, and most of that reputation is earned. Asked to "stand up a governance program," teams have a habit of buying a tool, printing a policy, and calling the job done. The result is a data catalog nobody updates and a steering committee that never steers. The Salesforce Platform Data Architect exam runs in the opposite direction, and this lesson (requirement gv-2a) is where it draws the line. It asks you to compare and contrast the approaches and considerations for designing and implementing an enterprise data governance program, including the operating framework, stewardship roles, policies, standards, and metrics.[^1]

The single idea that anchors everything in this lesson is this: data governance is not a tool, and it is not a team. It is a set of decision rights and accountabilities for data. It answers four questions that every org has to answer eventually: who decides, who is accountable, who does the work, and under what rules. A tool can support that answer. A governance council can enforce it. But the substance is the answer itself: the explicit assignment of who may decide what about data, who carries the accountability when it goes wrong, and who does the day-to-day work of keeping the data fit for its purpose.

Hold that definition and the rest of the lesson clicks into place. When the exam asks which artifacts are core to a governance framework and offers a published set of policies and standards, defined roles with decision rights, a single reporting dashboard, or a Big Object archive, the framework's substance is the policies and standards plus the defined roles with decision rights. Dashboards and archives are supporting tools at most. They report on the program or store a copy of the data, but neither one decides, neither one is accountable, and neither one does the governance work.

This lesson builds the program piece by piece: what a framework contains, who the roles are, how the operating model can be organized, what the policies and standards have to cover, what the metrics look like, and how governance plugs into the delivery lifecycle so it becomes a gate instead of a department. Lesson 26 gave you the enforcement mechanisms, the GDPR control surfaces of consent, retention, encryption, and deletion. This lesson gives you the ownership and policy structure that keeps those mechanisms maintained, audited, and non-negotiable. The two lessons are the yin and the yang of the Data Governance domain: lesson 26 was the what to do, this one is the who decides and under what rules.

## What data governance is, and what it is not

Start by fixing the definition, because the exam rests on it and so does every defensible design.

Data governance is the set of decision rights and accountabilities for data. It assigns who decides what data means, who decides how it is named and classified, who decides how long it is kept, who decides who may see it, and who is accountable when any of those decisions are wrong or ignored. Those decisions happen in every org whether anyone names them or not. An org with no written governance still decides, implicitly and inconsistently, who gets to create a field, what "active customer" means, and whether a data quality problem gets fixed. The difference governance makes is that those decisions become explicit, assigned, and auditable instead of accidental.

Two consequences follow, and the exam is careful to test both.

First, governance is not a tool. A data catalog, a lineage tool, a profiling suite, and a quality dashboard are all useful, and a mature program uses several of them. But the tool does not decide, and it is not accountable. If you install a catalog and it sits empty because nobody owns the metadata, the tool did not fail, the governance did. The decision rights that would have made the catalog worth using were never assigned. The exam treats "buy a tool" as the naive answer whenever the scenario is really about who owns, who stewards, and what the rules are. Tools are enablers of a governance decision; they are never the decision itself.

Second, governance is not a team. A governance council and a data office can be part of the program, and in large orgs they usually are. But a governance team with no authority is a meeting. The decisions the team makes have to be binding, and binding means there is a mechanism, an executive sponsor or a council with decision rights, behind them. The exam's trap here is a council that exists on paper but has no executive sponsorship and no enforcement, so its decisions are advisory and everyone ignores them.

That is the frame. Government is not about the organization chart on its own, and not about the software. It is about the accountable assignment of decisions over data, made real by roles with authority and standards that carry weight.

## The operating framework: the building blocks

The exam asks you to compare and contrast approaches for designing a program, and the first thing it wants you to be able to name is what a governance framework contains. The framework is the structure that organizes the decision rights, and it has five recurring building blocks in the exam's model.[^2]

Scope and domains. Governance is not one undifferentiated blob; it is organized around domains, which are the business areas whose data is governed as a unit. The classic domains are customer, product, and finance, but any org defines domains that match how its data is actually used. Scoping the program means deciding which domains are in scope and, crucially, which domains get governed first. This is where the maturity path starts, and the exam is explicit that you start with a pilot domain rather than trying to govern everything on day one.

Policy. Policy is the layer of principles and rules: what the org's position is on data classification, on retention, on who may access what, on the consequences of non-compliance. Policy is high-level, written, and relatively stable. It is the "why" and the "shall" of the program. A policy says the org treats customer personal data as confidential and will protect it accordingly; the standards say how, field by field.

Standards. Standards are the concrete, enforceable rules that implement the policy. Naming conventions for objects and fields, the classification scheme and what each class means, the quality thresholds a field must meet, the retention periods per data category. Standards are where policy becomes something a machine or a reviewer can check against. A standard that cannot be checked is not really a standard; it is a wish.

Processes. Governance needs operating processes: how a data issue gets logged and resolved, how a change to a standard is proposed and approved, how a new field request is reviewed. Without processes, the decisions have no path to be made, and the roles have no workflow to run. The two processes the exam names most are issue management and change control.

Roles. Finally, the framework assigns who does what. This is the accountability layer, and the next section is devoted to it. The framework's roles are the owner, the steward, and the custodian, backed by a governance council and an executive sponsor.

A framework that covers these five holds the decision rights in a structure. A framework that skips any of them leaves a gap. Stock a framework with policies but no roles and nobody is accountable. Staff it with roles but no standards and the roles have no rules to enforce. The exam's Q1 is really asking whether you can recognize the two artifacts that are the framework's substance, meaning the policies and standards on one side and the defined roles with decision rights on the other, as distinct from the two supporting tools, the dashboard and the archive.

## The roles: owner, steward, custodian

The accountability layer of the framework is expressed in roles. The exam expects you to distinguish three of them precisely, because conflating them is a classic distractor.

The data owner is the business leader accountable for a domain. The owner is not a technician; the owner is the person, usually a senior business leader, who carries the accountability for the data in their domain and who has the authority to make decisions about it. If the customer domain's data is a mess, the customer data owner is the person who answers for it. The owner sets the policy direction for the domain, approves the standards that apply to it, and resolves escalations that stewards cannot decide. Accountability is the owner's defining trait.

The data steward is the person who does the day-to-day quality and issue-resolution work for a domain. Where the owner is accountable, the steward is operational. The steward maintains the domain's data definitions, applies the standards, resolves data quality issues, manages the exceptions, and works with the business to keep the domain's data fit for purpose. If the owner says "customer data must meet this quality bar," the steward is the one who runs the quality reports, chases down the bad values, and drives the fixes. Stewards can be business analysts, data analysts, or domain specialists; the point is they are the everyday hands of governance for their domain.[^3]

The custodian is the technical operator of the systems that store and move the data. The custodian is not accountable for the quality of the data, and does not decide the policy. The custodian operates the technical environment: the database or org where the data lives, the integration jobs that move it, the APIs that serve it. The custodian keeps the systems running, applies the technical controls, and implements what the owner and the steward decide. Account and system administrators on Salesforce are custodians in this sense.

Outside the three named roles sit the two authority structures. Executive sponsorship gives the program its teeth: a C-level or SVP sponsor who has the authority to make governance decisions binding and to hold the owners accountable. The governance council, or steering body, is the forum that makes the cross-domain decisions: it ratifies standards, resolves conflicts between domains, approves the program's roadmap, and reviews its performance. A council is only as strong as its sponsorship; without an executive sponsor behind it, a council's decisions have no enforcement, and that is exactly the trap the exam warns about.

The mental model to carry into the exam is a chain: the executive sponsor authorizes the council, the council oversees the owners, the owners are accountable per domain and set the direction, the stewards do the day-to-day quality and issue work, and the custodians operate the technical systems. Confusing the owner with the custodian, or the steward with the owner, drops you into the wrong answer. Owner decides and is accountable to the business. Steward executes day to day. Custodian operates the technology.

## The operating models: centralized, federated, hybrid

The next comparison the exam wants is the operating model: how the program is organized across the enterprise. There are three canonical models, and they differ in where the decision authority sits.[^4]

Centralized. In a fully centralized model, one team governs all data. A single central data-governance group owns the policy, the standards, the roles, and the decisions for every domain. The strength is consistency: one set of rules, applied the same way everywhere, so there is no difference between how the finance domain and the marketing domain are governed. The weakness is the bottleneck. Every decision, every standard change, and every escalation funnels through one team, which slows down a large enterprise and stalls when the central team cannot keep up. Centralized also tends to disconnect the standards from the business realities of each domain, because the central team is further from the day-to-day work.

Federated. In a fully federated model, each domain governs itself within an agreed enterprise framework. Domain owners and stewards make their own governance decisions for their domain, bound by a shared set of enterprise-level ground rules. The strength is agility and relevance: each domain sets standards that match how it actually works, and decisions are made close to the work. The weakness is inconsistency. Without strong central standards holding the line, domains drift: the customer domain and the product domain end up with different naming conventions, different classification schemes, and different ideas of data quality, and the org loses the ability to compare and integrate data across domains.

Hybrid, which the exam names the common enterprise choice, keeps the enterprise standards central while the domains execute the day-to-day governance. The center sets the non-negotiable, org-wide standards: the classification scheme, the naming conventions, the retention framework, the quality threshold floor. The domains run their own governance within those standards: their own stewards resolve their own issues, their own owners set domain-relevant policies, their own reviews happen at their pace, as long as they conform to the central standards. Hybrid gets the consistency of centralized for the things that must be consistent, and the agility of federated for the things that vary by domain, while avoiding the bottleneck of pure centralization and the drift of pure federation.

The exam's Q2 asks this directly: which operating model keeps enterprise standards central while domains execute day-to-day governance? The answer is the hybrid model. Centralized is wrong because it bottlenecks all decision through one team and does not leave day-to-day execution to the domains. Federated is wrong because it lets the domains define the standards themselves, risking inconsistency. Hybrid is the model that pairs central standards with domain execution, and the exam is explicit that it is the common enterprise choice. Carry the trade-off rather than just the labels: centralization buys consistency at the price of agility and bottleneck risk, federation buys agility at the price of consistency, and hybrid splits the difference by centralizing only what must be consistent.

## Policies and standards: what has to be defined, and what makes a standard survive

The framework's content, the part that actually governs, is the set of policies and standards. The exam wants you to know both what they cover and what makes them meaningful, because a standard that says noble things but cannot be enforced or audited is not doing governance.

The policies and standards the exam names, and that any defensible program defines, are these.[^5]

Data classification and handling. A scheme that assigns each data element a sensitivity class, public, internal, confidential, restricted, or a PII/PHI/PCI tagging scheme, and states how each class must be handled: who may see it, whether it is encrypted, how it is masked, how it is retained. Classification is the hook that the protection controls from lesson 26, FLS, encryption, masking, retention, all hang on. A field tagged confidential triggers the handling rules for confidential data automatically.

Naming conventions. The rules for naming objects, fields, and records consistently across the org. Consistent naming is what makes data discoverable and what makes integration predictable. The exam treats naming conventions as governance, not cosmetics, a thread it first picked up in the metadata lessons. A naming convention is a standard because it is a rule that can be checked and enforced.

Retention and archival. The rules for how long each category of data is kept and what happens when the period ends, purge or archive. This is the standardized, org-wide version of the retention policy lesson 26 made operational. Governance sets the policy; the scheduled purge jobs are its enforcement.

Quality thresholds. The rules for what counts as acceptable data quality, expressed as measurable thresholds per field or per object: completeness (a field is populated for X percent of records), validity (values conform to the domain and format), timeliness (data is refreshed within Y hours). Quality thresholds are the standards that the metrics later measure against.

Access control principles. The high-level rules for who may read and write what data, the principles that the sharing and security model implements. Access principles are the governance layer above FLS and sharing rules: the rule exists because the governance said how data access should be decided, and the platform enforces it in the concrete configuration.

Issue and escalation management. The rules for how a data quality or governance issue is logged, triaged, assigned, resolved, and escalated. Issue management is the process that keeps governance from being reactive chaos, and it is a standard that can be audited.

Standards carry weight only if they are enforceable and auditable. Enforceable means there is a mechanism to check whether the standard is being met and a consequence for not meeting it; a naming convention nobody checks is a suggestion. Auditable means there is a record that the check happened and the result, so the org can demonstrate, to an auditor or to itself, that its standards are real. A standard without enforcement or auditability is the difference between governance and a decorative policy document. The exam rewards architects who insist on this: a standard that cannot be checked and verified is not a standard worth counting as part of the program.

## Metrics: making governance a managed program

This is the piece that separates a governance program from a governance aspiration. Policies and roles establish intent; metrics make intent measurable, and the exam is explicit that metrics turn governance from an aspiration into a managed program.[^6]

The core metrics are the data quality dimensions, measured and reported over time. The exam names six that should be second nature: accuracy (does the value match the true real-world fact), completeness (are fields populated where they should be), consistency (does the same fact agree across records and systems), timeliness (is the data current enough for its use), uniqueness (are there no unintended duplicates), and validity (do values conform to the expected domain and format). These six dimensions are the vocabulary of data quality, and a governance program measures them against the quality thresholds it set in its standards.

Around the quality dimensions sit two families of program-level metrics. The first is adoption and coverage: how much of the governed data is actually covered by the program. Coverage asks what share of objects and fields have defined owners, stewards, standards, and quality thresholds. Adoption asks how consistently the governed teams follow the standards, how many data issues are being logged and resolved through the process rather than around it. A program can have perfect quality numbers on a sliver of its data and still be failing; coverage and adoption tell you whether the program reaches the whole estate.

The second is issue aging, how long data issues go unresolved. Issue aging is the operational health gauge of governance. Issues are logged, and a program tracks how many are open, how long each has been open, and how many aged past the target resolution time. An org whose data issue backlog is growing, and whose issues age past target, is a program whose stewards are over capacity or whose processes are not working, no matter how nice the policy document reads.

Metrics matter for a reason the exam wants you to state: they turn governance into a managed program. Without metrics, governance is a set of good intentions that cannot be evaluated, improved, or defended to the business. With metrics, governance becomes something a leader can review on a dashboard, see a problem in, and act on: the completeness of the customer domain dropped last quarter, the issue aging in the product domain crossed the target, coverage of the finance domain never started. That is manageability, and it is what earns governance a seat at the table. Metrics are not the program, but they are what makes the program real.

## Embedding governance in the SDLC

The last major comparison in this lesson is the one the exam's Q3 tests, and it is the most practical. Governance has to be connected to delivery. It has to be embedded into the software development lifecycle as a gate in projects, not run as a parallel team that is consulted after the fact.

The reason is enforcement. When governance is a separate team that reviews projects after they are built, its standards are whatever a developer happens to remember, and the governance team can only comment on finished work. When governance is embedded in the SDLC, its standards become gates that a project must pass to be done: the design review checkpoints that a data model change goes through, and the Definition of Done that a project must meet before it ships, which includes conforming to the naming, classification, and quality standards. A standard becomes enforceable the moment it is a gate, because the project cannot move forward until it conforms, and the rework cost of shipping non-conforming work makes the gate stick.

The exam's Q3 asks why governance should be embedded in the SDLC rather than run as a parallel team, and the answer is that embedding standards as delivery gates, in the design reviews and the Definition of Done, makes them enforceable and prevents the drift and rework that parallel review cannot stop. The wrong answers are the three that treat embedding as a way to drop the program's pieces: it reduces the need for metrics, it removes the need for data owners, it eliminates the need for stewards. All three are false, and the reasons matter.

Embedding governance in the SDLC does not reduce the need for metrics, because gates still need to be measured and the program still needs its quality and coverage and issue-aging numbers to run. It does not remove the need for data owners, because somebody still has to be accountable for the domain and its standards and to resolve escalations; a gate cannot be accountable. And it does not eliminate the need for stewards, because somebody still has to do the day-to-day quality work and resolve the issues the gates surface. Embedding is a way to make the standards bite; it is not a way to shrink the accountability structure. The program keeps all its pieces, owners, stewards, and metrics, and adds the enforcement that makes them real.

The frame is this: governance should be a gate in projects, not a parallel bureaucracy. A gate stops non-conforming work and forces conformance at the point where it is cheap. A parallel bureaucracy reviews work after the fact, when conformance is expensive and easy to skip. The exam rewards the architect who designs governance into the delivery pipeline, the review checkpoints and the Definition of Done, because that is where the standards actually take effect.

## The maturity path: prove value before expanding

Governance is not a big-bang project, and the exam is explicit about the anti-pattern. Attempting enterprise-wide governance on day one, chartering a council for every domain, writing standards for every object in the org, almost always stalls. The scope is too large, the momentum is spread too thin, and the program dies before it demonstrates any value.

The maturity path the exam endorses starts with a pilot domain, most commonly customer. Stand up the governance structure for that one domain: charter the council, name the customer data owner and stewards, define the customer domain's standards and metrics, and run the pilot through a full cycle. Prove value where it can be seen: a real quality problem in the customer domain measured and fixed, an issue backlog that gets logged and resolved through the process, a standard that prevented a bad field from being created. Then expand, using the pilot as the template, into the next domain.

The pilot-first path wins for two reasons. It proves the framework works on a bounded scope before it is asked to govern everything, so the program earns credibility and adopters instead of imposing on them. And it surfaces the rough edges of the framework, the gaps in the process, the poorly worded standard, while the blast radius is still one domain, so the second domain is smooth. The exam rewards the architect who recommends starting small, proving value, and expanding, and it penalizes the ambitious enterprise-wide launch that stalls.

## Worked example: the retail governance program

Here is the applied scenario the exam is shaped like, and the reasoning it wants to see.

A retailer has customer, product, and finance data spread across Salesforce, an ERP, and a data warehouse. Data quality varies wildly by domain and by system, nobody can agree on what "active customer" means, new fields appear with inconsistent names, and a compliance audit flagged that retention and access are not consistently enforced. Leadership wants a data governance program, and one faction argues for buying an enterprise data catalog tool, another for a standing committee, and a third for governing the whole company starting immediately.

Score each proposal against the definition of governance. Governance is decision rights and accountabilities, so the catalog tool is an enabler, not the program; buying it without assigning owners and stewards will leave it empty and useless. A standing committee is a forum, but it is only as strong as its sponsorship; a committee with no executive authority can advise but cannot bind. Governing the whole company immediately is the stall-prone big-bang that the maturity path warns against.

The defensible design runs the framework, the roles, the model, and the maturity path together. Scope the customer domain as the pilot, the classic first step, and charter a governance council backed by an executive sponsor with the authority to make decisions binding. Name a customer data owner, the business leader accountable for the domain, and customer data stewards to do the day-to-day quality work, and identify the custodians who run the CRM, the ERP, and the warehouse. Choose a hybrid operating model: a central set of enterprise standards for classification, naming, retention, quality thresholds, and access, with the customer and later the product and finance domains executing their own governance within those standards. Publish the policies and standards, and make the two load-bearing ones concrete: a naming convention that every new field must conform to, and classification tags that drive the protection controls. Instrument the metrics: measure the six quality dimensions on the customer domain, track coverage and adoption as the program reaches new objects, and watch issue aging on the steward backlog. Embed the standards into the delivery lifecycle so a new field or object that violates the naming or classification standard cannot pass the design review or the Definition of Done.

The result is a program with teeth and a start that can finish. It made the decisions: who is accountable, who stewards, what the standards are, how the program is organized, and how it will know if it is working. It gave the accountability real authority through the executive sponsor. It embedded the standards as delivery gates so they actually bite. And it started with one domain so it can prove value and expand, rather than stalling on an enterprise-wide launch. That is the exam's idea of a good answer.

## Config walkthrough: standing up an enterprise data governance program

Here is the numbered walkthrough the exam expects you to be able to reason through.

1. Define scope and domains, and charter the governance council with executive sponsorship. Decide which domains are in scope, start with a pilot domain such as customer, and charter the governance council that oversees the program. Secure an executive sponsor with the authority to make the council's decisions binding, because a council with no enforcement is a meeting. This is the authority layer that makes every later step stick.

2. Assign data owners and stewards per domain, and document the decision rights. For each domain, name the data owner, the business leader accountable for the domain, and the data stewards who do the day-to-day quality and issue-resolution work, and identify the custodians who operate the systems. Document the decision rights: who decides, who is accountable, who does the work. The documented decision rights are the substance of the program.

3. Publish policies and standards. Define and publish the classification and handling scheme, the naming conventions, the retention and archival rules, the quality thresholds, the access control principles, and the issue and escalation rules. Make the standards enforceable and auditable: enforceable through a mechanism that checks conformance and a consequence for non-compliance, and auditable through a record that the check happened. Without enforcement and auditability, a standard is a suggestion.

4. Instrument the metrics and build the dashboards. Pick the data quality dimensions to measure, the six of accuracy, completeness, consistency, timeliness, uniqueness, and validity, and set the thresholds. Build the coverage and adoption metrics so the program knows it reaches the whole estate. Track issue aging on the steward backlog so issues cannot sit unresolved forever. Metrics turn governance from aspiration into a managed program.

5. Embed the standards into the SDLC and run the pilot domain. Add the governance checkpoints to the design reviews and the Definition of Done so non-conforming projects cannot pass, making the standards gates instead of recommendations. Run the pilot domain through a full cycle, prove the value, then expand to the next domain. Start small, prove value, and scale.

## The three exam traps

Trap one, treating governance as a tooling project. Governance is decision rights, roles, policies, and metrics; tools are enablers. Buying a catalog or a quality tool without assigning owners, stewards, and enforceable standards leaves the tool empty and ungoverned. When the scenario asks who decides and who is accountable, the answer is never "install software."

Trap two, naming a council with no authority. A governance council or steering body is only as strong as its executive sponsorship. A council whose decisions are not binding, because it has no sponsor with enforcement authority, produces advice that everyone ignores. The exam flags a council that exists on paper but cannot enforce anything.

Trap three, attempting enterprise-wide governance immediately. Chartering the council, writing standards, and assigning roles for every domain on day one usually stalls, because the scope is too large and the program never proves its value. The path is a pilot domain, prove value, then expand. Promoting the big-bang launch, or expecting a single governance structure to span every corner of the org from the start, is the mistake the maturity path was built to avoid.

## Try it for yourself

In a sandbox or a scratch org, run a mini governance exercise on a test domain. Define one standard, a simple naming convention for a set of custom fields, and document the six data quality dimensions and their thresholds for those fields. Then pick a small quality problem, for example an optional field that is populated only 40% of the time when the org's completeness threshold is 95%, and walk the issue through the escalation path you defined: log it, assign it to the steward, set a target for resolution, and track its age. This makes concrete why coverage and issue aging are the metrics that reveal whether a governance program is actually managing anything.
