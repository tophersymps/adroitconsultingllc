Metadata is the layer of the data model that most implementations leave until the end and then regret. Fields get created with empty descriptions, terms like "customer" mean different things in different departments, and nobody can answer the auditor's question about where a reported number came from. Requirement dm-3a is the lesson that fixes that, and it asks you to compare and contrast the techniques for capturing and managing business and technical metadata: the business glossary and data dictionary, data lineage, taxonomy, and data classification. [^1]

The exam weight is light relative to the data-modeling lessons, but the concepts are tested with precision. The highest-frequency mistake is conflating artifacts that the exam treats as strictly distinct. The business glossary is not the data dictionary. Lineage is not a glossary. Classification is not a taxonomy. Each one answers a different question, and a candidate who can say which artifact answers which question will clear the quiz in this lesson without hesitation.

The practical reward is just as real as the exam reward. An org that keeps the two halves of metadata linked, that can trace a value to its source and transformation, and that drives protection from classification, is an org that survives an audit and an integration project without drama. This lesson builds that picture term by term, then shows you how to make it real on the platform.

## Deep Dive

### Metadata has two halves that must stay linked

Metadata is data about data. On the platform it splits into two halves, and the split is the entire foundation of this topic. [^2]

Technical metadata describes the systems: object and table names, field data types, field lengths, keys, and API endpoints. It is the language of the schema, and it is what you read out of Object Manager and the Metadata API. [^2] Business metadata describes meaning: business definitions, data owners and stewards, the domain a piece of data belongs to, its sensitivity, and the business rules that constrain it. [^2] Both halves are necessary, and neither substitutes for the other. Knowing that the field is a Text(10) with a unique flag tells you nothing about whether it holds a government entitlement number that is regulated. Knowing that it has a sensitive classification tells you nothing about its storage type or length.

The reason the pair is so often tested together is that the platform stores the technical half natively and the business half only where you choose to put it. Field types, lengths, and API names are always there. Business definitions, owners, and sensitivity are there only if someone wrote them down, and that is the part projects skip. [^2] The discipline this lesson teaches is to keep both halves, and to keep a mapping between the two, so that a business term can be resolved to the object and field that implement it, and a field can be resolved back to the meaning and custodian that govern it. [^1][^2]

### The business glossary and the data dictionary are not the same artifact

Here is the distinction the exam tests most aggressively. A business glossary, also called a business data dictionary, defines business terms in business language. An example entry defines "Active Customer" as "a customer with at least one paid order in the trailing twelve months." [^1] It is written for business users and auditors, not for developers. A data dictionary documents the technical objects and fields: the object names, the field names, the data types, the keys, the validation constraints, and the relationships. [^1] It is written for the people who build and integrate.

The two are distinct but inseparable. The exam distractor is to offer one where the other is meant. When a question asks which artifact holds the business definition of "Active Customer", the answer is the business glossary, not the object data dictionary, because the dictionary documents structure and the glossary documents meaning. [^1] When a question asks which artifact documents the structure of an object, the answer flips.

The professional practice is to derive both from the same governance core so they cannot drift apart. The glossary term and the technical field should point at each other, with the glossary owning the meaning and the dictionary owning the structure. On the platform, the field Description and Help Text are where the in-system pieces of this mapping live, while the richer glossary usually lives in an external catalog or registry that holds the master definitions. [^1][^2]

![Comparison table of the business glossary and the data dictionary across the question each answers, the audience each is written for, what each holds, an example entry, and where each lives in the org](</diagrams/day-05-dm-3a-business-and-technical-metadata-business-dictionar/2-glossary-vs-dictionary.png>)

### Data lineage: answering where a number came from

Data lineage traces a data element from its origin source, through the transformations that changed it, to its destination and its consumption. [^1] Lineage is the artifact that answers the auditor's question directly: which systems and transformations produced this revenue value, and which reports or applications consume it? The glossary defines the term, the taxonomy classifies it, the dictionary documents its structure, but only lineage captures the flow. [^3]

Think about what a revenue figure on a dashboard actually is. It might originate in an ERP as a raw order value, be transformed by a currency conversion in an integration layer, be loaded with a filter in a data pipeline, and finally be consumed by a revenue report. Lineage records each of those hops so that when the number looks wrong, someone can walk from consumption back to origin and find the step that broke. [^3]

The exam wants you to know two things about lineage. The first is that it is a documentation artifact: the source-to-target field mappings, the transformation rules, and the job names that move data are the raw material of lineage, and they must be maintained. [^1] The second is that lineage is increasingly a live capability in integration tooling, not just a spreadsheet you retire at launch. Data movement tools surface lineage as a first-class feature, and modern auditors may demand live lineage rather than a hand-maintained document. [^1] On the platform, lineage can be inferred from your integration definitions in middleware such as MuleSoft Anypoint or Data Pipelines, from the field transformations those jobs apply, and from the object-to-object mapping documents you maintain, each traceable back to the job and the connection credentials it runs under, and that is one more reason those artifacts deserve to exist. [^2]

![Lineage trail for one revenue figure: ERP raw order value, currency conversion in the integration layer, a pipeline load with a filter, and consumption in a revenue report, with the two exam points that lineage is both a maintained document and a live integration capability](</diagrams/day-05-dm-3a-business-and-technical-metadata-business-dictionar/3-lineage-flow.png>)

### Taxonomy: the controlled hierarchy underneath category data

A taxonomy is a controlled, hierarchical classification of a domain, for example a product tree or a customer-type hierarchy. [^1] Its purpose is to standardize categories so that every system calls the same thing by the same name. Where the glossary defines individual terms, the taxonomy defines the arrangement of a category domain into a consistent tree. [^1]

The exam frames taxonomy as the skeleton on which classification and reference data hang. A product taxonomy that runs Hardware, then Computers, then Laptops, then a specific model, is what lets a marketing system and a finance system group the same records the same way. [^1] Without a taxonomy, each system invents its own categories, "category data" stops being comparable, and integration work doubles. With one, the shared reference data of the enterprise has a stable home. [^1]

Taxonomy and classification are easy to blur, and the exam separates them this way: a taxonomy is a controlled hierarchy of categories, whereas classification is the assignment of a sensitivity label to data. Taxonomy organizes what a thing is; classification tags how sensitive it is. [^1][^4]

### Data classification: sensitivity that drives protection

Data classification tags data with a sensitivity label. Common schemes run Public, Internal, Confidential, and Restricted, and regulated contexts add PII, PHI, and PCI categories. [^4] The point of classification is not the label itself; it is that classification drives protection. The correct protection for a field depends on what it holds, and the classification decides it. [^4]

Consider the mechanisms classification can drive. Field-level security controls which users see which fields. Encryption at rest protects the data from unauthorized reads of the underlying storage. Masking hides data in interfaces and dev environments. Retention determines how long it is kept, and sharing determines who is allowed to reach it at all. [^4] None of those is chosen in a vacuum. You decide the sensitivity first, and the protection follows from it.

This is the point of this lesson's third quiz question, and it is a precision question. A team wants any field tagged Restricted to be masked for certain profiles. The classification-driven approach is to maintain the classification metadata and drive field-level security and masking from it. [^4] The decoys each fail on a specific technical ground. A validation rule cannot block reads, because validation rules evaluate on save and govern what may be written, not who may read an existing value. Moving restricted data to a Big Object solves a volume problem, not an access problem, and forbids none of the sensitive access. Blanket-encrypting the whole org with Shield Platform Encryption alone protects at rest but does not handle masking or per-profile access. [^4] Classification is the mechanism that lets the protection scale with the data.

On the platform, the native metadata capture points are the field Description and Help Text, the object Description field, and the Field Usage or Where-is-this-used tooling that shows where a field is referenced. [^2] The sensitivity label itself is usually kept in the external catalog or a custom registry, mirrored into the field-level security and masking decisions. [^4]

### Metadata is itself governed

Good metadata does not stay good on its own. It needs owners, change control, and a review cadence, and without all three it drifts from reality within a release cycle or two. [^1] A glossary entry that still says a field is used by one team after that team retired the process is not just stale; it is dangerous, because downstream decisions will be made from it.

This is the stewardship requirement hiding inside an otherwise conceptual lesson. Someone must own each domain of the glossary, approve changes to definitions and classifications, and reconcile the external catalog against the live org on a schedule. [^1] When a field is added, its description and classification must be populated at creation. When a field is deprecated, the glossary and lineage must reflect it. Metadata management is the operational arm of data governance, and this lesson is where that link is established before the next lesson builds the tooling around it. [^1][^2]

## Configuration Walkthrough

Do this in a sandbox or a dev org; it takes minutes and makes the concepts concrete.

1. Populate in-system business metadata. In Setup, open Object Manager, select an object, and open a field. Use Edit to write a meaningful Description and Help Text that state what the field means and who owns it. Repeat for the object's Description field. [^2]
2. Read in-system technical lineage. Open the same field and use the Field Usage or Where-is-this-used action to see which validation rules, page layouts, formulas, and integrations reference it. This is the native view of the field's dependencies and tells you the blast radius of any change. [^2]
3. Stand up the glossary. In a spreadsheet or cataloging tool, create an entry per business term with four attributes: a business definition, its owner or steward, the object and field that implement it, and its classification. This is the master glossary, and it is the artifact that resolves synonyms. [^1]
4. Record integration lineage. For each source-to-target mapping in your middleware, write down the job name, the transformation rule, and which target field each source field feeds. Attach the job identifier to the target field so the flow is walkable. [^1][^3]
5. Build a classification field. Add a picklist field, for example Sensitivity with values Public, Internal, Confidential, and Restricted, on your metadata registry, and map its values into the field-level security and encryption decisions so the label actually governs access. [^4]

Run the steps and you will have both halves of metadata for one object, plus a lineage trail and a sensitivity label, the complete set this lesson asks you to distinguish.

## Exam Traps

**Trap one: conflating the business glossary with the technical data dictionary.** The glossary defines terms in business language; the dictionary documents technical objects, fields, types, and keys. [^1] When a question asks which artifact holds a business definition such as "Active Customer", the answer is the glossary. Offering the object data dictionary is the distractor, and it is wrong because the dictionary documents structure, not meaning.

**Trap two: assuming data lineage is only a documentation artifact.** Lineage traces a value from origin through transformations to consumption, and modern integration tooling surfaces it as a live capability, which auditors may require. [^1][^3] When an auditor asks which systems and transformations produced a revenue figure and which reports consume it, the answer is lineage, and it may need to be maintained as part of the running integration, not as a retired spreadsheet.

**Trap three: treating field descriptions as sufficient metadata without ownership, classification, and review.** Native descriptions are a starting point, but a field with a description and no owner, no sensitivity label, and no review cadence becomes stale and misleading after the first release. [^1][^2] The exam's underlying message is that ungoverned metadata, however complete it looked at launch, is not trustworthy metadata.

## Try It

Build the full artifact set for one field and then quiz yourself on the distinctions. Pick a dated field that feeds a report, such as an order date or a revenue amount.

Write its technical metadata: object name, field API name, data type, and length. Write its business metadata: a one-sentence definition of what the field means, its steward, and whether it is Public, Internal, Confidential, or Restricted. [^1][^2] Then trace one value: note the source system it arrived from, the transformation that touched it, and the report that consumes it, and confirm that walking that trail answers "where did this number come from". [^3] Now ask yourself which artifact you just built for each: the first two paragraphs give you the dictionary and the glossary, the third gives you lineage, and the sensitivity label is classification. [^1][^4] When you can build all four for one field and say which is which, you have the entire conceptual map this lesson covers, and the quiz in this lesson is designed to confirm exactly that.
