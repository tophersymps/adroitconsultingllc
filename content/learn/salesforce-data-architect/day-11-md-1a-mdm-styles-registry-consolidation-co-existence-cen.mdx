Before you read this lesson, make sure you can answer the opening question the exam will put in front of you: an enterprise carries the same customer as an Account in Salesforce, as a business partner in SAP, and as a party in a billing system, and each record disagrees on the address. Requirement md-1a asks you to compare and contrast the techniques, approaches, and considerations for implementing master data management solutions, including the four MDM implementation styles of registry, consolidation, co-existence, and centralized, and to say when each is appropriate. [^1] That is the whole of what you will learn here: the four ways to reconcile that one customer record, and how to pick among them.

Master Data Management, MDM, is the discipline of creating and maintaining a single, authoritative, reliable version of critical business data across an enterprise's systems. [^3] When Salesforce, SAP, an e-commerce platform, and a customer service system all reference Acme Corporation, true MDM means they all reference the same golden record with consistent, authoritative attributes. [^3] The implementation style is the architectural choice that determines how you source, maintain, and govern that master data: where the data lives, how changes flow, and who controls the truth. [^2] Pick the wrong style for your domain, your technical baseline, or your organizational maturity and you fight the architecture for the life of the program. [^2]

The single idea that ties the whole lesson together is that the four styles form a centralization spectrum. [^2] On one end the hub barely does anything; on the other it owns everything. Registry gives the identity only, consolidation gives the hub an analytics view, co-existence makes the flow bidirectional, and centralized makes the hub the author. [^5] Hold that spectrum and the four styles stop being four facts to memorize and become four increments of the same decision: how much authority are you willing to take away from the source systems?

## Deep Dive

### Registry style: the cross-reference and the global id, nothing more

Registry style is the lightest touch on the entire spectrum. The MDM hub does not create or own master records. It finds them, matches them, links them, and presents unified views. Every record stays in its source system; the registry is a map. [^2] Concretely, the hub stores the cross-reference between a master record and the source-system records, plus a global identifier, but it does not necessarily store the full attribute values. [^1] When an application needs customer 12345, the registry replies that this is really John Smith, who also exists as CUST-67890 in billing and ACCT-8234 in the call center, and it lets the calling application decide what to do with the ambiguity. [^2]

The SFVedas account of registry-style MDM on the Salesforce platform states it plainly: the registry maps the IDs across the systems. Salesforce Account ID 001xxx equals SAP Business Partner 10045 equals Oracle Party ID 55123. [^3] Each system keeps its own record and its own attributes. The registry enables cross-system identity resolution without requiring all systems to sync all attributes. [^3] That is the crucial exam point: registry gives you identity resolution and a unified key, and it does not centralize the attribute data. The sources stay the system of record for attributes.

Why does anyone choose registry? Because it demands the least change to existing systems. You do not ask the ERP to accept customer updates from the hub. You do not force a periodic batch reconciliation. You do not ask source systems to become read-only. [^2] The registry runs matching algorithms over name, address, phone, tax ID and publishes a survivorship rule stating that if this customer exists in both systems, these identifiers refer to the same legal entity. [^2] It shines in regulated environments where you cannot arbitrarily merge or update a record without an audit trail back to its origin, and in complex multi-tenant setups where no single system owns the relationship. [^2]

The trade-offs are real. The registry does not cleanse data; it maps dirty data to other dirty data, so its matching logic struggles when source systems hold garbage. [^2] Applications consuming the registry must handle ambiguity: if the match is 95% confident two records are the same, does the caller merge the views, ask the user, or route to a steward? [^2] And because the hub does not store attributes, it cannot improve data quality inside the source systems. [^3] On the exam, the trap is to read "registry" as if it copies attributes into a central store. It does not. Registry stores only the cross-reference and the identity. [^1] That is why, in a registry style, a record change in the ERP may never touch the MDM hub at all; the hub only notes that the ERP and CRM hold conflicting versions and presents both downstream. [^2]

### Consolidation style: a read-mostly hub for the analytics view

Consolidation style moves one step toward the center. The MDM hub extracts data from all source systems and builds a golden record in a central place, cleansing, standardizing, deduplicating, and matching as it does. [^4] The hub becomes the consolidated store for analysis and reporting. The defining characteristic is direction: consolidation pulls data in, and it does not push corrected data back out to the sources. [^1] Source systems are not updated from the hub; they continue operating independently. [^3]

Read that again, because it is the single most-tested distinction in this lesson. Consolidation produces a golden record for downstream analytics, yet the source systems are authoritative for transactions. [^2] An order still lands in the ERP. The customer's transaction history still lives in the transaction system. What lands in the hub is the curated master attributes, the customer segment, lifetime value, preferences, and risk score that marketing, analytics, and BI need. [^2] The hub is the reporting and analytics view, and the sources stay the authority for the day-to-day transactions. [^1]

The governance model is clearer than registry, which is why consolidation is so common. The hub owns the master once it has it. [^2] Stewards work in the hub or through its interface, changes follow a defined workflow with approval thresholds, and that clarity makes consolidation easier to govern than the match-and-link model of registry. [^2] Because it is non-intrusive to implement, consolidation is ideal for business intelligence and data warehouse use cases. [^4]

Consolidation is the right answer, and only consolidation, when an enterprise wants a golden customer view for analytics but cannot change the CRM or ERP to consume corrected data. [^1] Centralized would require the source systems to consume hub-authored data, which they cannot be changed to do. Co-existence needs bidirectional write-back, which the source systems cannot accept. Registry gives identity only and holds no full attributes, so it cannot build the golden reporting view. Consolidation alone builds a consolidated store for analysis without pushing anything back. [^1] That is a textbook scenario and it points straight at consolidation.

The cost of consolidation is synchronization overhead and staleness. You need periodic batch jobs or event-driven syncs to pull data from sources into the hub, transform it, and publish it outward. [^2] Typical refresh cycles run hours, and a consolidation hub cannot serve real-time operational lookups. [^5] If an address changes in the source, the analytics view reflects it only after the next sync. If the scenario demands that the corrected address show up in Salesforce immediately so sales sees the golden value, consolidation alone will not deliver it; that is the moment the design starts to need co-existence. [^3]

### Co-existence style: bidirectional flow with sources still running

Co-existence style, sometimes spelled coexistence, is the pattern most vendors mean when they say MDM: the hub and the source systems both hold data, and they stay in sync in both directions. [^2] Like consolidation, co-existence assembles a central MDM hub alongside the existing source applications. The difference is that the hub both consolidates data and publishes shared values back to the source systems. [^1][^4] Bi-directional synchronization keeps the hub and the sources consistent, so master data changes can originate in the MDM platform or in a source system and propagate to the other. [^4]

This is the first style that actually improves operational data quality in the source systems, and it is the most common enterprise pattern for a reason. [^1][^3] When the hub identifies that Salesforce and SAP disagree on Acme's address, the hub applies the golden record address to both systems. [^3] Co-existence supports bidirectional data flow while the source systems remain operational, which is why enterprises that cannot stop their CRM and ERP pick it. [^1] Source systems retain operational data and handle transactions, and the hub keeps the master attributes aligned across all of them. [^4]

The mechanism is worth understanding at the attribute level. Because edits can originate anywhere, you must define which system or the hub wins on each attribute, and model survivorship so a merge knows what to keep. [^2][^4] Ownership rules determine which system controls each attribute. [^4] Without predefined conflict-resolution rules before go-live, concurrent updates collide and race conditions corrupt the golden record. [^5] This is a governance decision as much as a technical one: every source-system owner must accept that the MDM is going to write to their tables, and every integration team must build two-way sync. [^2] That political cost is the price of co-existence.

Co-existence demands real-time or near-real-time infrastructure and careful matching, because systems push and pull continuously and resolve conflicts, and batch sync does not hold up when two systems modify the same attribute within the same window. [^2][^5] It is appropriate when you have a handful of mission-critical systems that must stay synchronized. [^2] On the exam, remember the two true statements about co-existence: the hub consolidates data and publishes values back to sources, and the flow is bidirectional while the sources stay operational. [^1] The cross-reference-only modeling belongs to registry, and the hub-as-sole-author belongs to centralized, neither of which describes co-existence. [^1]

### Centralized (transaction) style: the hub authors the master data

Centralized style sits at the far end of the spectrum. The MDM hub becomes the authoritative system for all master data creation and updates. [^3] Source systems no longer create or update customer records independently; they submit requests to the hub, which validates, deduplicates, and distributes the approved record. [^3] Applications read the master data from the hub on demand rather than maintaining their own copy. [^2] The hub is both the system of record and the only place that accepts updates. [^4]

This gives the strongest single version of the truth you can buy, and it is also the most invasive choice on the spectrum. [^1] Because every master record routes through the hub, maximum control, maximum consistency, and simpler governance and compliance follow. [^4] But the hub must perform like a mission-critical transaction system, and the source systems must be re-engineered to consume master data from it instead of authoring it. [^1][^2] Existing direct-create workflows in the ERPs have to be rebuilt. [^5] That is the requirement you must test first: centralized style requires application change, so it only fits when the source systems can actually be changed to consume hub-authored data. [^1]

Centralized is rarely a starting point. It is best suited to greenfield environments or organizations with advanced data governance maturity and modern cloud architecture, and it is justified for high-value domains where absolute consistency outweighs the disruption, such as regulated financial reporting or entity data that must be identical everywhere. [^2] It blocks self-service: all master data creation must go through the hub, so business teams that used to create customers directly in the ERP must learn the hub workflow instead. [^5]

### The spectrum and how to choose

Run the ordering once more, because the exam will ask you to arrange it from least to most invasive to the source systems: registry, consolidation, co-existence, centralized. [^1] Registry introduces only identity resolution and never touches source attributes. Consolidation adds a read and reporting hub that does not push back. Co-existence adds bidirectional write-back and genuine source-system participation. Centralized removes authority from the sources almost entirely and makes the hub the author. [^1] That ordering is the correct answer because it tracks the increasing disruption you force on the source systems. [^2]

Choice is a maturity question, not a preference. Consider the following before you commit:

- Latency requirements. If the business needs a real-time operational golden value for transactions, registry and consolidation cannot serve it, and co-existence may require sync infrastructure you do not yet have. [^1][^2]
- Whether sources can accept updates from the hub. Co-existence and centralized both require the sources to accept hub writes. If a legacy ERP physically cannot expose that path, consolidation is the ceiling. [^1][^3]
- Governance maturity. Registry and consolidation demand strong source-system governance, and co-existence demands sophisticated conflict resolution and stewardship that is meaningless without the organization to run it. [^2][^4]
- Data quality at the sources. Registry maps dirty data to dirty data, so it fails when source quality is poor; consolidation gives the hub a chance to cleanse centrally even if the sources stay dirty. [^2]
- Cost and complexity of integration. Registry is fast to deploy and cheap; centralized gives the most control at the most change and cost. [^1][^2]

Most organizations start lighter and evolve. A common path is to begin with consolidation, prove value to leadership, expand to co-existence as governance and steward maturity grow, and reserve centralized for specific high-value domains. [^4] Nobody jumps straight to centralized, and few stay forever at registry. [^2]

### Salesforce relevance: hub or participant

On the Salesforce platform, MDM is most often implemented declaratively rather than by standing up a separate enterprise MDM product. [^3] The building blocks are duplicate and matching rules, external IDs, and a canonical Account and Contact model. Matching rules define what a duplicate means, comparing fields with exact and fuzzy algorithms, and duplicate rules decide what to do when a match is found, whether to alert, block, or report. [^6] This keeps data clean at point of entry inside the org, and it is genuinely important hygiene, but on its own it is not enterprise MDM, because it governs data only within Salesforce, not across SAP, billing, and the website. [^3]

External IDs are how Salesforce participates in cross-system identity resolution. An external ID field holds the identifier from another system so that Salesforce can match records accurately, and you use it to upsert records by that external value instead of by the Salesforce Id. [^7] That external ID is the same cross-system key the registry maps, which is why it appears in so many MDM designs. [^3][^7] A canonical Account and Contact model, where Account is the named legal entity and Contact the people, gives every system a shared shape to conform to. [^3] For production scale, organizations often supplement with dedicated MDM tooling such as Informatica, Reltio, or Semarchy integrated with Salesforce as a consuming system, because standard duplicate management lacks probabilistic matching across cross-system identifiers. [^3]

The style choice maps cleanly onto whether Salesforce is the hub or a participant. When Salesforce is the hub, it plays centralized or co-existence, authoring or syncing the master it serves to other systems. When Salesforce is a participant, it plays registry or consolidation, contributing its record as one of several feeds to a hub elsewhere. [^1][^3] The honest test for whether Salesforce is truly the system of record is operational: when a customer calls to change a billing address, is it updated in Salesforce first with sync to SAP, or in SAP first with sync to Salesforce? The system updated first is the authoritative one. [^3]

### Hierarchy management across every style

Hierarchy management is part of the MDM consideration set in every style, not a separate feature you add later. You must model parent-child relationships consistently, deciding whether a given entity has a single parent hierarchy or multiple hierarchies, and keep them in sync when the source systems disagree. [^1] The Account hierarchy on Salesforce, the parent-child Account relationships across subsidiaries and parent companies, is a high-value starting point, and you make it durable with a clean external ID strategy that maps to the ERP party model. [^3] When each source carries a different view of how companies relate, the MDM layer, whatever its style, is what reconciles those trees into one that every downstream system can rely on. [^1][^3]

## Configuration walkthrough

Work the choice as an architect would, in five steps.

1. Decide the target style based on whether the hub must author data or merely reference it. [^1] If reporting needs a unified view but the hub must not write to sources, aim at consolidation. If the hub must publish corrections and the sources can accept them, aim at co-existence. If the hub must own the master outright, aim at centralized. [^1]
2. For a registry-style approach on the platform, define a cross-reference object that holds the source-system id plus a global id, and link it to the Salesforce record through an external ID. [^1][^7] Each source record keeps its own attributes; the cross-reference object records the identity mapping, which is the whole of the registry contract. [^3]
3. For consolidation, build the consolidated objects and reporting layer, with an ETL job that loads source data into the hub without writing anything back. [^1] Confirm the job is one-directional so the golden store can never accidentally corrupt a source. [^3]
4. For co-existence, enable bidirectional sync and define which side wins on each attribute, and model survivorship so merges keep the right value. [^1][^4] Publish the hub's mastered values back to the sources and make the hub accept their edits in return, with conflict rules in place before you switch it on. [^5]
5. For centralized, make the hub, whether Salesforce or an external MDM tool, the author of master records and configure the source systems to consume them through integration. [^1][^3] This is the step that demands application change, so confirm the sources can be modified to read, not author, master data. [^1]

## Exam traps

Three traps account for most of the missed questions in this requirement, and the design of the exam is built around them.

First, do not call consolidation centralized. Consolidation is a read and report hub that does not push corrections back to sources; centralized authors the data at the point of origin. If the scenario says a golden view is wanted for analytics and the sources cannot change, the answer is consolidation, not centralized. [^1]

Second, do not assume registry style copies attributes. Registry stores the cross-reference and the identity, not necessarily the attribute values. [^1] An answer that says the registry holds the full customer profile is describing a different style; the registry holds the map between the systems. [^2][^3]

Third, do not recommend centralized when the source systems cannot be changed to consume hub-authored data. [^1] Centralized requires application change, and if the ERP cannot be re-engineered to read from the hub, centralized is not viable; co-existence or consolidation is. [^1][^5]

## Try it

Work these on paper before you reach for Setup.

First, the classic scenario from requirement md-1a. An enterprise wants a golden customer view for analytics, but it cannot change the CRM or the ERP to consume corrected data. [^1] Name the style that fits and explain why each other one fails: consolidation builds the consolidated store without pushing back, centralized requires a consumption change, co-existence needs bidirectional write-back, and registry provides identity only without the full attributes. [^1]

Second, test the co-existence definition. State, in your own words, the two true statements about co-existence: the hub both consolidates data and publishes values back to sources, and it supports bidirectional flow while the sources remain operational. Then say which statements might tempt you and why they are wrong: cross-reference-only modeling describes registry, and the hub being the sole author describes centralized. [^1]

Third, arrange and defend the spectrum. Put the four styles in order from least to most invasive to the source systems: registry, consolidation, co-existence, centralized. [^1] For each boundary, name the new capability the next style adds to justify the jump in invasiveness, identity only to a reporting hub, then to bidirectional write-back, then to hub authorship. [^1][^2]

## What's Next

You can now take an MDM scenario and place it on the centralization spectrum: registry for identity mapping without attribute storage, consolidation for a read-mostly analytics hub, co-existence for bidirectional flow that keeps sources running, and centralized for full hub authorship and the most application change. [^1][^2] You can also justify the choice against latency, source-write acceptance, governance maturity, source data quality, and integration cost, and you can map any style onto whether Salesforce plays the hub or a participant. [^1][^3]
