Lesson 22 repaired a single view that was already broken, folding duplicate records into one survivor. Requirement sm-4a asks a bigger question: what do you do when the customer is spread across whole orgs, not just across records? The requirement frames it as: given a customer scenario, recommend a design to effectively consolidate and/or leverage data from multiple Salesforce instances, including org consolidation strategies, org-to-org integration, and Data Pipelines and Data Connect.[^1]

This is the reality most large enterprises inherit. A company does not usually plan to have six Salesforce orgs. It acquires a competitor and inherits their org. It opens a European division and stands up a separate instance to keep EU data local. Each business unit builds its own org because it was faster than coordinating. Before long the architect is staring at a map of orgs that all hold pieces of the same customer, none of them agreeing on what the customer looks like.

The exam wants you to be the person who makes a coherent decision out of that mess. Not "consolidate everything," which is a common but often wrong instinct, and not "leave it all alone," which is how the mess grew. The skill is knowing when to merge orgs, when to keep them separate and sync, and how to do either without breaking the data.

## The multi-org reality

Enterprises accumulate orgs for reasons that all made sense at the time. A regional office needs its own instance to satisfy data residency. A business unit runs a different sales process and does not want to fight the parent org's release train. An acquisition brings in an org that already has years of history and its own way of working. Each of these is a legitimate reason to have a separate org, and none of them is a failure of planning. The failure is when nobody ever steps back and asks whether the orgs should stay separate.[^2]

The architect's first job is to inventory that reality. Before you can recommend consolidate or integrate, you need to know what you are working with: what each org is for, what data it holds, how much of that data overlaps with other orgs, what processes run on it, how aligned its release schedule is with the rest of the company, and what residency or compliance constraints apply to its data. That inventory is the foundation of every decision that follows.

The core tension is simple. A single org gives you one customer view, shared processes, and one reporting truth. Multiple orgs give you autonomy, residency control, and the ability to run divergent processes. The architect's job is to decide, per entity and per org, which of those you actually need.

## Consolidate or integrate: the decision

The requirement names two broad strategies, and the exam expects you to know when each fits.

Consolidation means merging multiple orgs into one. You move the data, the users, and the processes into a single instance and retire the others. The drivers are the ones that make a single org attractive: a single customer view, shared processes across the business, reduced integration complexity, lower license and admin overhead, and one reporting truth. When every part of the company genuinely wants to work the same way on the same customer, consolidation removes the friction of keeping several orgs in sync.[^3]

Integration means leaving the orgs separate and syncing the data between them. You keep each org's autonomy and residency, and you move only the shared entities, the Accounts, Contacts, and Products that multiple orgs need. The drivers are the ones that make consolidation dangerous: divergent processes that would fight each other in one org, data residency rules that mandate separate instances, release-train conflicts, and the sheer risk of a large migration.

The exam trap here is the assumption that consolidation is always better. It is not. Data residency is the clearest counterexample. If EU customer data must stay in an EU instance, you cannot consolidate that data into a US org without breaking the law. Residency and compliance often force a multi-org design, and in that case you integrate or virtualize rather than consolidate, using a global key to stitch the customer view across instances.[^4]

The decision framework the exam rewards: consolidate when processes and sharing can genuinely unify and compliance allows it; integrate when you need autonomy, residency, or a phased approach; and prefer hub-and-spoke or data sharing when you need shared truth without merging everything.

## The three consolidation options

When you do consolidate, you have three ways to get there, and they differ in risk and cutover profile.

The first is to merge everything into a single existing org in one move. This is the big-bang approach. You pick one org as the target, migrate all the data and users from the others into it, and cut over all at once. It is the fastest to a single org and the highest risk, because a single failure during cutover affects the whole business at once. It also has the longest downtime window, since you are moving everything in one shot. Big-bang suits small orgs or orgs with little data and few users, where the blast radius of a failure is small.

The second is gradual consolidation, moving one division or business unit at a time. You migrate a division into the target org, verify it works, then move the next. Each cutover is smaller, so each failure is contained to one division and the downtime is shorter per move. The cost is that you run in a hybrid state for a while, with some divisions in the target org and others still in their source orgs, which means you need integration between the target and the not-yet-migrated orgs during the transition. Gradual consolidation suits large enterprises where a big-bang cutover is too risky.

The third is hub and spoke. A global org holds the master data, and the spoke orgs sync against it. This is not really full consolidation, it is a middle path. The hub is the system of record for the shared entities, and each spoke keeps its local data and its autonomy while pulling the shared master data from the hub. Hub and spoke gives you a single source of truth for the shared entities without forcing every org into one instance. It is the right choice when you need shared truth but the orgs cannot or should not fully merge, which is often because of residency or process divergence.[^5]

## Org-to-org integration

When you cannot merge, you sync. Org-to-org integration moves the shared entities between orgs so each one has the data it needs. The entities that usually need syncing are the ones every org touches: Accounts, Contacts, and Products. A support org needs to see the customer's account even if the sales org owns it.

The critical design decision in any org-to-org sync is which org is the master for each entity. You cannot sync bidirectionally without a per-entity master, because two orgs writing the same record will overwrite each other and create update loops. If the sales org owns the Account, then the sales org is the master for Accounts, and the support org treats its Account copy as read-only, updated from the sales org. If the support org owns Cases, then it is the master for Cases, and the sales org reads them. Every entity gets exactly one master, and every other org is a consumer of that master's data.[^6]

The mechanism for the sync is middleware, Salesforce-to-Salesforce, or Data Pipelines. Middleware is a separate integration platform that moves data between the orgs and can transform it in transit. Salesforce-to-Salesforce is a native sharing feature that lets two orgs share selected records. Data Pipelines is the managed option, covered below. Whatever the mechanism, the sync needs a shared key so the two orgs can agree on which record in org A corresponds to which record in org B.

That shared key is the external ID. A Salesforce record ID is org-specific, so the Account with ID `001A0000001` in the sales org is a different ID in the support org even if it is the same company. To sync, you give the shared entity an external ID field that holds a value both orgs agree on, usually the master org's record ID or a company-wide identifier. The consumer org stores that external ID and uses it to match the incoming record to its local copy. Without the external ID, the sync cannot tell which records correspond, and you end up with duplicates or lost links.[^7]

## Data Pipelines and Data Connect

The managed tools in this space are Data Pipelines and Data Connect, and the exam wants you to know what each does and when it fits.

Salesforce Data Pipelines is a high-performance data platform built into the CRM that can clean, transform, and enrich large volumes of data at scale, without taking the data outside the trusted Salesforce environment or round-tripping it through an external ETL tool. It is the native answer to the point-to-point ETL code you would otherwise write. You can use it to consolidate data scattered across systems into one place, including bringing in data from external services like Snowflake and Amazon S3, and to enrich and modify Salesforce data in place. When you want to move and transform data between orgs or between an org and an external store without building custom integration code, Data Pipelines is the managed path.[^8]

Data Connect provides zero-copy data sharing between Salesforce and external data stores. Zero copy means the data is not physically copied and stored in the destination; instead the destination queries the source data in place, or the source exposes it as a live view. This is the key tool when you want shared data without full consolidation and without the storage and sync cost of copying everything. A data warehouse can query Salesforce data as if it were local, and Salesforce can expose enriched data to an external platform, all without maintaining a duplicate copy. Zero copy is bi-directional, so external data lakes can be queried from Salesforce and Salesforce data can be queried from the external platform, with the source staying authoritative.[^9]

The reason these tools matter for the exam is that they change the consolidate-versus-integrate tradeoff. Historically, sharing data between orgs meant building and maintaining point-to-point ETL, which was expensive and fragile, so consolidation looked more attractive than it was. With Data Pipelines and Data Connect, you can share data between orgs and external stores with managed tooling and zero-copy sharing, which makes integration a much more viable option. When you need shared truth without merging everything, these tools are the answer.

## Worked example: the three-region company

Let me put this together with a concrete scenario, the kind the exam gives you.

A company has three regional orgs: Americas, EMEA, and APAC. Each org runs its own sales process and holds its own customer data. The company needs a global customer view for reporting, but EU customer data must stay in the EU instance to satisfy GDPR and local data residency rules.

The wrong answer is to consolidate all three into one org. That would move EU data into a single instance, breaking residency. The right answer is to integrate and stitch the orgs with a shared global customer key and residency-respecting sync.

Here is how that design works. Each org keeps its own data and its own process. You add a global customer key, an external ID field, to the Account object in every org. The key is a company-wide identifier, not an org-specific record ID, so the same customer has the same key in all three orgs. When a customer is created in any org, it gets a key, and the key is synced to the other orgs so they can match their local records to the same customer.

For the global view, you do not copy all the data into one place. You use Data Connect to expose each org's data to a central analytics layer, or you use Data Pipelines to bring the shared entities together, and you stitch them on the global customer key. The EU data stays in the EU org, but the analytics layer can query it in place through zero-copy sharing, so the global view exists without violating residency. The customer's identity is unified by the key, while the data physically stays where the law requires.

This is the design the exam's Q1 scenario is testing. Three regional orgs, EU data must stay in the EU instance, global customer view required. The answer is to integrate and stitch with a shared global customer key and residency-respecting sync, not to consolidate, not to delete the EU org, and not to use Big Objects to merge the orgs. Residency forces a multi-org design, and a shared key plus controlled sync gives the unified view without breaking the law.

## Config walkthrough: deciding and building the design

Here is the numbered walkthrough the exam expects you to be able to reason through.

1. Inventory all orgs. For each org, record its purpose, the data it holds, how much of that data overlaps with other orgs, the processes that run on it, its release alignment with the rest of the company, and its residency and compliance needs. This inventory is the input to every decision that follows.

2. Decide consolidate versus integrate, per entity. Do not make one global decision. Some entities, like Products, may consolidate cleanly into a single master, while others, like EU customer Accounts, must stay local. For each entity, ask whether the orgs can genuinely unify their process and whether compliance allows the data to move. If both are yes, consolidation is on the table. If either is no, integrate.

3. Choose the consolidation approach. If you are consolidating, pick merge-into-one, incremental, or hub and spoke based on the risk and cutover profile. Small orgs with little data can big-bang. Large enterprises should move a division at a time. If you need shared truth without full merge, use hub and spoke.

4. For integration, define the master per entity and a shared key. Every shared entity gets exactly one master org, and every other org is a consumer. Give the entity an external ID field that holds a value both orgs agree on, and use that key to match records across orgs. This is what prevents update loops and lost links.

5. Use Data Pipelines and Data Connect for the managed sync and zero-copy sharing. Use Data Pipelines to move and transform the shared entities between orgs or from external stores. Use Data Connect to expose data to external platforms and query external data in place, without copying it. This is how you share data without building point-to-point ETL.

6. For consolidation, build the migration plan around external IDs and cross-references. Because record IDs change on migration, you must capture the source external IDs and use them to relink related records and to re-key downstream systems. Plan for duplicate resolution across the merging orgs, since the same customer may exist in both source orgs under different records.

## Why record IDs change on migration

The exam trap about record IDs is worth its own section, because it is the most common way a consolidation breaks.

A Salesforce record ID is unique within its org. The Account `001A0000001` in org A is a different record from the Account `001A0000001` in org B, even if both represent the same company. When you consolidate, you move records from org B into org A, and the moved records get new IDs in org A. The old org B IDs no longer exist.

Anything that referenced the old ID breaks. Related records that pointed at the org B Account now point at nothing, because their lookup field still holds the old ID. Downstream systems that stored the org B ID to track the customer now have a stale reference. If you do not plan for this, the consolidation succeeds at the data level and silently breaks every link that depended on the old IDs.

The fix is external IDs and cross-references. Before the migration, you capture each record's source external ID. During the migration, you use those external IDs to relink related records to their new homes and to re-key downstream systems so they point at the new IDs. The external ID is the stable bridge between the old world and the new one, because it does not change when the record moves. This is what the exam's Q3 scenario is testing: downstream systems break because record IDs changed, and the prevention is using external IDs and cross-references to relink records and keep downstream links valid. Enabling more duplicate rules, turning off CDC, and increasing API limits do not preserve identity across orgs.

## The three exam traps

Trap one, assuming consolidation is always better. Data residency, process divergence, and release conflicts often favor integration. A company with EU data that must stay in the EU instance cannot consolidate into one org without breaking the law. The exam rewards the architect who recognizes when integration is the correct answer, not the one who reflexively merges everything.

Trap two, ignoring that record IDs change on migration. A consolidation is a data migration, and record IDs are org-specific, so they change when records move. Without external IDs and cross-references to relink related records and re-key downstream systems, the consolidation breaks every link that depended on the old IDs. Plan the migration around external IDs from the start.

Trap three, syncing two orgs bidirectionally without a per-entity master. Two orgs writing the same record will overwrite each other and create update loops and conflicting writes. Every shared entity needs exactly one master org, and every other org must treat its copy as read-only, updated from the master. Define the master per entity before you build any sync.

## Try it for yourself

In a sandbox, create two orgs, or simulate two orgs with two sandboxes. In each, create an Account for the same company, and give both Accounts the same external ID value, a company-wide identifier. Then build a simple sync between them, using Data Pipelines or a middleware tool, that treats one org as the master for Accounts. Confirm that a change to the master Account propagates to the consumer org, and that a change to the consumer Account does not propagate back, because the consumer is read-only for that entity. This reproduces the per-entity master design and shows why it prevents update loops.

Then reproduce the record-ID trap. In the consumer org, note the Account's record ID. Simulate a consolidation by exporting the Account and re-importing it into a fresh org, which assigns a new ID. Try to match the downstream system on the old ID and watch it fail. Then re-import using the external ID as the matching key and confirm the downstream system can relink to the new record. This is the external-ID bridge that keeps a consolidation from breaking downstream links.

Finally, reproduce the residency scenario from Q1. Set up two orgs and mark one as the EU instance. Confirm that consolidating the EU data into the other org would move it out of the EU instance, and that a Data Connect zero-copy share lets a central analytics layer query the EU data in place without copying it, so the global view exists while the data stays where the law requires.
