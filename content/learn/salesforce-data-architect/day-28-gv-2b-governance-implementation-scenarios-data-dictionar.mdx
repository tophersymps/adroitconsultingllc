Lesson 27 gave you the architecture of a governance program: the framework, the roles, the operating model, the policies and standards, and the metrics that make governance manageable. This lesson, requirement gv-2b, is where that architecture lands on concrete artifacts. It asks you to compare and contrast the various approaches and considerations for designing and implementing an enterprise data governance program, and to apply them through the data dictionary, data ownership, quality metrics, and supporting tooling.[^1]

The frame for this whole lesson is a single sentence. Governance is decision rights and accountabilities, and those decisions and accountabilities are made real by three operational artifacts. The data dictionary says what the data means, who owns it, and what quality bar it must meet. The ownership model says who answers for it. The metric and issue workflow says whether the data is actually getting better and who is fixing it when it is not. Lesson 27 was the why and the who. This lesson is the what, the where it lives, and the how you measure and fix it.

The exam tests this lesson mostly through scenarios. You are given a program that measured a problem, a duplicate rate or a completeness gap, and asked why it persists. You are given a catalog tool and asked what makes it worth owning. You are given an ownership structure and asked which role decides what. The common thread is that the exam rewards the architect who sees governance as a running, maintained system of artifacts and workflows, not a stack of documents and licenses. Everything in this lesson comes back to that.

## The data dictionary: the artifact that makes governance operational

Start with the artifact that ties the framework together, because the exam treats it as the key operational object. The data dictionary, sometimes called the business glossary, is the centralized definition of what the org's data elements mean and how they are governed.

A data dictionary defines each element's meaning, its owner, its classification, its quality threshold, and its source of truth. A business glossary carries the accessible, business-facing definitions, while a data dictionary documents the technical structure, but in Salesforce governance work the two terms are used closely enough that the exam tests the underlying artifact more than the label.[^2] What matters is that for each field or object, someone has written down what it means, who is accountable for it, how sensitive it is, what counts as good enough, and where the authoritative value comes from.

The data dictionary is the operational artifact because it is what makes the framework checkable. A policy that says "customer data must be accurate" means nothing against a specific field until the dictionary says that the Account.Phone field's source of truth is the billing system, that its completeness threshold is 98 percent, and that the customer data owner is accountable for it. Once those five answers exist for a field, that field is governed. It has a meaning, an owner, a sensitivity class, a quality bar, and a source of truth, and every one of them can be looked up and enforced.

Here is the trap the exam hits hardest on the dictionary. A data dictionary that is written once and never maintained diverges from the org and loses all credibility. The org adds fields, renames data sources, promotes a new system of record, and the dictionary sits stale at whatever it said on the day it was published. Teams stop trusting it, stop consulting it, and start making up their own definitions, and the artifact that was supposed to unify the definitions becomes one more document everyone ignores. A dictionary that is not current is worse than no dictionary, because it looks authoritative while being wrong.

So the first operational rule of governance implementation is that the dictionary is maintained, not written. It lives on a Schedule, it is updated when the org model changes, it is reviewed by the owners of the domains it describes, and it is audited for coverage: how many of the org's objects and fields actually have a definition, an owner, a classification, and a threshold. A fresh dictionary on a fraction of the estate is honest; a stale dictionary claiming to cover everything is a liability. The exam rewards the architect who treats dictionary maintenance as a recurring process with a cadence and a coverage metric, not as a one-time documentation project.

## Ownership models: who answers for the data

The second operational artifact is the ownership model, and it turns the three roles from lesson 27 into an accountability map. The exam is precise about what distinguishes each role, because the ownership and the accountability are where governance either holds or dissolves.

The business owner is accountable for the domain's meaning and use. The owner is the business leader who answers when the domain's data is wrong, who decides what the data should mean, who approves the standards that apply to it, and who resolves the escalations the stewards cannot settle. Accountability is the owner's defining trait. The owner does not have to operate a system or run a query; the owner has to own the outcome.

The technical custodian is the person who operates the systems that store and move the data. Where the owner is accountable, the custodian is operational and technical. The custodian runs the org or the database where the data lives, applies the technical controls, implements the sharing settings and the encryption, and keeps the integration jobs moving. Account and system administrators on Salesforce are custodians in this sense. The custodian is not accountable for the meaning of the data and does not decide the policy; the custodian operates the technology that stores and moves it.

The steward is the person who maintains data quality day to day. The steward sits between the two: the steward applies the owner's standards, resolves data quality issues, maintains the domain's definitions in the dictionary, manages exceptions, and works with the business to keep the data fit for purpose. If the owner is accountable and the custodian operates, the steward is the one who does the daily quality work that makes the other two roles' decisions real.[^3]

The rule the exam tests through Q3 is that the owner is accountable for the domain's meaning and use, while the custodian operates the systems. These are not the same role, and inverting them is a classic distractor. The owner does not run the systems, and the custodian does not define the meaning. The owner owns the outcome; the custodian runs the machinery.

The second rule is blunt and worth memorizing. A field without an owner is ungoverned. Every field that matters needs a named business owner who is accountable for its meaning and use. An org can buy a catalog, print standards, and stand up a dashboard, but if a field has no named owner, nobody answers when it is wrong, and nothing gets fixed. Ownership is the anchor that makes every other governance artifact hold, because a definition without an owner is a suggestion, a threshold without an owner nobody enforces, and a quality issue without an owner nobody resolves.

## Quality metrics: the six dimensions and how to report them

The third operational artifact is the measurement, and this is where governance becomes a managed program rather than an aspiration. The exam expects the six data quality dimensions to be second nature, and it expects you to know both what each one measures and how to report and trend it.

The six quality dimensions are accuracy, completeness, consistency, timeliness, uniqueness, and validity.[^4][^5]

Accuracy is how closely the value reflects the real-world fact it represents. A customer's recorded address is accurate if it matches the address they actually live at. Accuracy is measured against a reference, the authoritative source that knows the real value.

Completeness is whether fields are populated where they should be. It is measured as a non-null rate: of the records that should have a value, what percentage has one. Completeness is the dimension behind "this field is populated for X percent of records."

Consistency is whether the same fact agrees across records and systems. The customer's phone number should be the same in Salesforce and in the billing system. Consistency is measured as the rate of agreement across the systems that should agree.

Timeliness is how current the data is for its use. Freshness and latency are the timeliness measures: is the data refreshed within the window the business needs, or is it stale by the time anyone reads it?

Uniqueness is whether there are unintended duplicates. It is measured as a duplicate rate: the percentage of records that duplicate another record. The exam's Q1 rests on this one, so hold onto it.

Validity is whether the values conform to the expected domain and format. A date field should contain valid dates, an email field valid email addresses, a picklist value a value from the list. Validity is conformance to the definition in the dictionary.

Each dimension is measured against a threshold that the standards set, and the results are reported and trended. A single number is a snapshot; a trend is a signal. The exam wants you to see completeness dropping from 97 to 91 percent over a quarter as a governance event, something a steward investigates and owns, not a one-off reading that vanishes into a report. Metrics present the truth to leadership, and trending them over time is what turns that truth into a managed, improvable program.[^6]

## Metrics without a workflow fix nothing

Now the most important operational lesson, and the one the exam's Q1 tests directly. Measuring a quality problem does not fix it. A workflow does.

Consider the exam's scenario. A governance program measures a 22 percent duplicate rate on Accounts, and the duplicates persist. More metrics will not help: the program already knows the duplicate rate is 22 percent, and a bigger or better measurement changes nothing about the duplicates themselves. A larger data dictionary does not help: definitions describe the problem, they do not resolve records. Platform Encryption is unrelated to duplicate records. What is missing is the component that turns a measured problem into a fixed one: an issue-management workflow with owners and SLAs to resolve the duplicates.

This is the gap between diagnosis and treatment. The metrics are the diagnostics. They tell you the duplicate rate, the completeness gap, the consistency failure. But data does not fix itself when it is measured. It gets fixed when a human, a steward with a name, is assigned the specific duplicate records, given a target resolution time, and held accountable for the outcome. That is the issue-management workflow: log the issue, prioritize it, assign it to an owner, and resolve it within a service-level agreement.

The exam's logic here is that metrics identify the problem while the workflow resolves it. A governance program that measures quality but has no issue-resolution process is doing half the job. It produces a dashboard full of numbers that nobody is obligated to act on, and the failures persist indefinitely. More metrics or a larger dictionary do not fix data, and encryption is unrelated. The missing component is the workflow with accountability and SLAs.

## Quality issue management: the workflow that makes metrics sting

Because the workflow is so load-bearing, spell out what it contains. A quality issue-management process has five parts.

Logging. Every quality problem that exceeds a threshold is logged as an issue with a description, the affected object or field, the dimension it fails, and the evidence. Logging is what turns an observation into a tracked item, and it is the moment the metric becomes actionable.

Prioritization. Issues are prioritized by impact. A completeness gap in a low-traffic reference field is not the same priority as an accuracy problem in the finance close data. Severity and business impact drive the priority, and priority drives the order of resolution.

Assignment. Each issue is assigned to an owner, ordinarily the steward of the affected domain, who is accountable for resolving it. Assignment is where the ownership model from earlier in the lesson binds. An issue with no assigned owner has nobody to carry it.

Resolution. The owner works the issue to a resolution: correcting the records, fixing the process that creates them, or applying the standard. Resolution is the actual fix, and it is where the data changes.

SLA and aging. Each issue has a service-level agreement, a target time to resolution that matches its priority, and the program tracks aging, how long each issue has been open and how many aged past target. Aging is the operational health gauge. A growing backlog of aged issues tells you the stewards are over capacity or the process is broken, no matter how clean the policy document reads.

Stop and notice how this workflow uses the ownership model. The metric says the customer domain has a 22 percent duplicate rate. The steward of the customer domain is assigned the duplicate-resolution issue. The business owner is the one accountable for the domain and can be escalated to when the steward is blocked. The custodian operates the merge tooling. Metrics found the problem, ownership found the accountable person, and the workflow drove it to resolution.

## The tooling ecosystem: catalogs, quality tools, and on-platform tools

The fourth operational consideration is tooling, and the exam is careful about what tooling is and what it is not. Tooling supports the governance framework; it does not replace it.

The metadata catalog is the tool that scans the org, indexes its objects and fields, and gives the data dictionary a home. Enterprise catalogs such as Collibra and Informatica Data Catalog capture metadata, link business terms to technical assets, show lineage, and host the definitions that the dictionary holds.[^7] A good catalog makes the dictionary discoverable and automates part of its maintenance by scanning the live org for new fields.

The data quality and profiling tools are the tools that measure the dimensions. Informatica Data Quality and Ataccama are the ones the exam tends to name. These tools profile the data, compute completeness, uniqueness, validity, and the other dimensions across the estate, and feed the quality metrics into the scorecard. The buyer of a quality tool is a custodian or a data engineering team, but the measures it produces belong to the steward and the owner of the domain.

The on-platform tools are the Salesforce-native options that require no separate license. Object Manager field descriptions are where the field-by-field meaning can live, the dictionary boiled down to a description on each field. Field Usage, available in the field details in Object Manager, reports how many records actually have a value for a field, a direct completeness read for an on-platform review. The Setup Audit Trail records changes made in Setup, giving you a timeline of configuration changes, who made them and when, which supports the change-control and auditability side of governance. Reports and dashboards surface the quality numbers to the people who need them.[^8]

And here is the exam's tooling trap, tested through the broader governance trap in this lesson. Adopting a catalog or a quality tool before first defining roles, standards, and metrics leaves the tool with nothing to enforce. A catalog with no owners and no definitions is an empty index. A quality tool with no thresholds and no workflow produces numbers nobody acts on. Tooling is only as valuable as the governance structure behind it. Buy tools to support a defined framework; never buy tools hoping they create one.

## Using the tooling: Object Manager and Field Usage in practice

Because two of the on-platform tools matter for the exam, make them concrete. Object Manager is where the field-level dictionary and the completeness read live.

On any custom object, open Object Manager, open an object, and open its fields. Each field's detail page has a Description field, and that is where a governing org records the field's meaning, its source of truth, and its owner in plain language that a future admin or steward can read. A governed field has a description; an ungoverned field is blank.

The field detail page also shows Field Usage, the number of records that have a value for that field, or the percentage populated. That is completeness measured directly in the platform, no external tool required. An admin who opens the Account custom field Reporting_Channel__c and sees it populated on only 40 percent of records has just read a completeness gap that the dictionary's threshold would flag as failing.

The Setup Audit Trail is the change log. Every change made in Setup, who made it and when, is recorded there, up to the platform's retention window. For governance, the audit trail is what makes a standard auditable. When a naming standard says every custom field must follow a convention, the audit trail records when fields that violate it were created, who created them, and when they were corrected. Audit trail history is the evidence that enforcement is real.

## Governance scenario applications: single customer view and GDPR

The exam applies all of this through scenarios, and two scenarios recur often enough to warrant a pattern. The first is the single customer view, and the second is GDPR. Both exercise the same four artifacts, dictionary, ownership, metrics, and workflow, toward different ends.

A single customer view program exists to unify the customer's data across multiple systems into one trustworthy picture. Its governance shape is specific and worth naming. It needs ownership of the customer domain: a named customer data owner who is accountable for the unified meaning of the customer, stewards who maintain the quality day to day, and custodians who run the systems that feed the view. It needs a customer data dictionary that defines what a customer is, which field is the system of record for each attribute, and how the sources rank. It needs duplicate-rate metrics, because the single view fails if the same customer appears as separate records. And it needs a merge or quality workflow with owners and SLAs that actually resolves the duplicates the metric reveals. The single customer view is the scenario where the duplicate metric and the workflow from Q1 are the whole point: measure the overlap, then run a workflow that merges and deduplicates under owner accountability.[^9]

A GDPR program, by contrast, governs data through a compliance lens. It needs classification ownership, because GDPR obligations attach to personal data, and someone must own the classification that marks a field as containing personal data. It needs retention metrics, because GDPR limits how long personal data is kept, and the program must measure and report what is held against its retention schedule. And it needs erasure SLAs, because GDPR grants individuals the right to erasure, and the program must resolve erasure requests within a mandated window. Where the single view revolves around uniqueness, GDPR revolves around classification and retention, but the skeleton is the same: a dictionary that records the classification and retention disposition, ownership that answers for it, metrics that measure it, and a workflow with SLAs that resolves it.[^10]

## Reporting governance to leadership: the scorecard

The last piece turns all the artifacts into what executives can actually manage. A governance program that cannot report to leadership cannot sustain funding, and the exam wants you to know how that reporting is built.

The leadership report is a scorecard: a snapshot of the quality dimensions per domain, the coverage of the dictionary, and the aging of issues, presented as a manageable view. The scorecard gives executives a manage-by-metrics view of the program, so governance is no longer a policy document defended by anecdotes but a dashboard reviewed like any other business function. The customer domain's completeness is 91 percent against a 98 percent threshold. The dictionary covers 60 percent of custom fields. Twelve issues aged past their SLA this quarter. Those are decisions an executive or the governance council can review, prioritize, and fund.

Underneath the scorecard sit the considerations that keep the whole program scaling without heroics. Start with the high-value domains. Attempting to dictionary, own, and metric every object on day one stalls; focusing the first pass on the customer, product, and finance domains proves value on the data that matters. Keep the dictionary light enough to maintain. A dictionary nobody can keep current because it demands a paragraph on every field will go stale; a lightweight definition, owner, classification, threshold, and source of truth per element earns its maintenance cost. Automate metric collection. Hand-built quality reports die under their own effort; using a profiling tool or a scheduled report to compute the dimensions keeps the trend fresh without a manual treadmill. And tie the standards into deployments. Make the naming and classification standards a change-control gate, so nothing ships that violates them, and governance scales as a delivery gate rather than as a heroic manual review after the fact.

## Worked example: the duplicate-rate scenario end to end

Here is the applied scenario the exam is shaped like, and the reasoning it wants.

A governance program measures a 22 percent duplicate rate on Accounts and reports it to leadership. The duplicates persist for six months, and leadership asks why the program is not working. The naive answers, more metrics, a bigger dictionary, platform encryption, all miss that the program built the diagnosis and never built the treatment. What is missing is the issue-management workflow with owners and SLAs.

Now rebuild the scenario correctly. Instrument uniqueness, and the duplicate-rate metric reveals the 22 percent overlap. That metric feeds a tracked issue, logged with the affected object and the duplicate evidence. A steward of the customer domain is assigned the issue and the duplicate records behind it, with a resolution SLA proportional to the priority. The business owner of the customer domain is accountable for the outcome and is escalated to when the steward is blocked on decisions about which record is the survivor. The custodian operates the merge tooling and the matching rules that identify and merge the duplicates. The metric found the problem; the workflow resolved it. At the next scorecard review, the duplicate rate is trending down because aging and SLA tracking kept the issue from sitting.

That is the difference the exam tests in Q1: metrics identify problems, and a workflow with ownership and SLAs resolves them.

## Config walkthrough: implementing governance that holds

Here is the numbered walkthrough the exam expects you to be able to reason through.

1. Publish the data dictionary per domain. For each high-value domain, publish a dictionary that records, per element, the meaning, the owner, the classification, the quality threshold, and the source of truth. Capture the meaning and owner in the field descriptions in Object Manager so the dictionary lives next to the fields it describes. Keep it light enough to maintain, and commit to a cadence that keeps it current, because a stale dictionary loses all credibility.

2. Assign the ownership and record the accountability map. For each domain, name the business owner accountable for the meaning and use, the stewards who maintain quality day to day, and the custodians who operate the systems. Record the map so every field has a named owner. A field without an owner is ungoverned, and a governance map no one has written down is a governance intention.

3. Instrument the quality metrics and build the scorecard. Pick the dimensions to measure, completeness, uniqueness, validity, timeliness, and the rest, set the thresholds in the standards, and automate the collection from the profiling tool or a scheduled report. Feed the results into a scorecard that trends the dimensions per domain, so leadership reviews the program like any other business function.

4. Stand up issue management with SLA and aging tracking. Build the workflow that logs issues, prioritizes them by impact, assigns them to stewards, tracks aging, and targets resolution times per priority. This is the step that turns measured problems into fixed ones. Without it, the scorecard numbers identify problems that nothing ever resolves.

5. Automate where you can. Scan the metadata for dictionary coverage so you know what fraction of the estate is governed. Automate the quality reports so the trend stays fresh. And add a change-control gate in deployments so the naming and classification standards block anything that violates them. Automation is what lets governance scale without heroics.

## The three exam traps

Trap one, writing the data dictionary once and never maintaining it. A dictionary that diverges from the org loses trust. The exam rewards the architect who treats the dictionary as a maintained, audited artifact with a cadence and a coverage metric, not a one-time documentation deliverable.

Trap two, measuring quality without an issue-resolution process. Metrics alone do not fix data. The exam's Q1 is exactly this: a measured 22 percent duplicate rate persists because there is no workflow with owners and SLAs to resolve it. Diagnosis without treatment is not governance; it is a dashboard.

Trap three, adopting catalog or quality tooling before first defining the roles, standards, and metrics they will enforce. A tool with nothing to enforce is an empty index or a number nobody acts on. Define the framework, then buy the tools that support it.

## Try it for yourself

In a sandbox, practice the completeness read and the on-platform dictionary in Object Manager. Create a custom field on an object, leave it empty on several records so its Field Usage shows a partial population, and type a real description into the field detail that records its meaning, owner, and source of truth. Then pick a quality threshold, say the field must be populated on 95 percent of records, and draft the issue you would log when it sits at 60 percent: the affected object and field, the dimension it fails, the steward you would assign, and the SLA you would set. The point is to feel each artifact, dictionary, ownership, metric, and workflow, doing its job, and to see that a number and a description do not fix data until an owned, aged issue drives the fix.
