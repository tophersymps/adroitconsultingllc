A data model is the most expensive thing you will ever change on the Salesforce platform. Fields can be deleted, page layouts can be rebuilt, automations can be rewritten. But the moment you pick master-detail instead of lookup, or put a record type where a validation rule belonged, you have committed every downstream layer to that decision: sharing, deletion, roll-up availability, reporting, integrations, and migration strategy all inherit it. [^1][^2]

Data Modeling and Database Design carries 25 percent of the Platform Data Architect exam, the largest single domain, and lesson one of 42 starts where the exam starts. Requirement dm-1a asks you to compare and contrast standard and custom objects, field types, lookup versus master-detail relationships, and object features such as record types, roll-up summary fields, and validation, and then recommend the right technique for a given requirement. That last verb matters. The exam rarely asks what a feature is. It describes a business situation and asks which modeling choice holds up.

This lesson covers each building block, then shows you the decision sequence an architect runs in practice: choose the object category, then the relationship type, then the field types, then the UI and integrity features. Get the order wrong and you will find yourself rebuilding a relationship on a live object, which is one of the few things the platform makes genuinely painful.

## Deep Dive

### Objects are tables, and the object category fixes your feature set before anything else

Salesforce presents its database as objects, its columns as fields, and its rows as records. [^7] That mental model is worth keeping literal, because most of the constraints in this domain are database constraints wearing a friendly label.

Standard objects ship with Salesforce and you extend them. Custom objects are yours, carried in the API with a `__c` suffix. Trailhead frames the split as a trade: the platform hands you contacts and leads, and you build what it does not track for your business. [^7] In practice, standard objects come with platform behavior you did not have to design (activity tracking on record pages, feed and sharing defaults, standard report types, tabs) while their field sets stay locked to Salesforce's decisions. Custom objects give you full control over fields, layouts, relationships, and naming, and you own every consequence of that control, including the ones the platform would have handled for you. [^7]

Before objects and fields, though, there is a category decision that exam questions test and implementations get wrong. An object is not simply standard or custom. It can also be a Big Object (`__b`) or an External Object (`__x`), and those categories do not support the same features. The Big Objects Implementation Guide is blunt about it: big objects support only object and field permissions, not regular or standard sharing rules, and features such as triggers, flows, and processes are not supported on them. [^9] External Objects are equally constrained in a different direction. Only lookup, external lookup, and indirect lookup relationships are available for external objects; no other relationship types are supported. [^2] That single sentence kills a lot of tempting designs, because no master-detail means no native roll-up summary on an external object, and the field type list confirms roll-up summary is not available for external objects at all. [^6]

So the architect's first question is not "which fields do we need" but "which object category can carry this requirement at all". Archive history that runs to hundreds of millions of rows belongs in a big object, and you accept that it will not have validation rules. Live data from an ERP that should not be copied belongs in an external object, and you accept that it will not have master-detail relationships. Everything else lands in standard or custom objects where the full feature set is available.

![Object category comparison: standard and custom objects carry the full feature set, big objects grant only object and field permissions, and external objects allow lookup, external lookup and indirect lookup relationships with no master-detail and no roll-up summary](</diagrams/day-01-dm-1a-data-modeling-fundamentals-objects-fields-relation/1-object-category-feature-set.png>)


Two numeric limits from the platform documentation are worth memorizing because they come up in design reviews. Each custom object can have up to two master-detail relationships and up to 40 total relationships, and the platform's stated best practice is not to exceed 10,000 child records for a master-detail relationship. [^1] The second number surprises people who assume master-detail scales like anything else. It does not, and the reason is structural: the platform maintains the ownership and sharing the relationship implies, and that maintenance costs work on every parent record.

### Relationship type is the decision with the longest shadow

There are two main relationship types on the platform, lookup and master-detail, and the difference is not about which objects you can connect. It is about ownership, security, deletion, and aggregation. [^8]

Master-detail links objects tightly, with the master record controlling specified behaviors of the detail record. [^1] The behaviors are the exam content:

The Owner field on detail records is not available and is automatically set to the owner of the master record. Detail records inherit security settings and permissions from the master, and you cannot set permissions on the detail record independently. Custom objects on the detail side cannot have sharing rules, manual sharing, or queues, because all three require the Owner field. The relationship field itself is required on the page layout of the detail record. Deleting a master record also deletes its detail and subdetail records, while deleting a detail record only sends it to the Recycle Bin and leaves the master intact. Reparenting is off by default, though an admin can allow child records on custom objects to be reparented. [^1]

Read that list again as a list of business requirements rather than a list of features. "The owner of this contract line belongs to whoever owns the contract" is an ownership requirement. "An expense line cannot exist without its expense report" is a deletion requirement. "Users who can see the parent must be able to see the child" is a security requirement. Master-detail is the platform's answer to all three at once, and the exam tests whether you recognize the requirement underneath the wording. In the fixture scenario from the exam guide, opportunity line items must inherit the opportunity's owner and sharing and roll their revenue up to the opportunity. Owner inheritance and sharing inheritance are master-detail behaviors, and native roll-ups require master-detail, so the answer is master-detail plus a roll-up summary field, not a lookup with a Flow copying the owner. [^1][^3]

![Lookup versus master-detail compared across owner, sharing, parent deletion, native roll-ups, the required relationship field, conversion rules and platform limits](</diagrams/day-01-dm-1a-data-modeling-fundamentals-objects-fields-relation/2-lookup-vs-master-detail.png>)


Lookup is the looser association. A lookup links two objects so you can look one up from the other, and the documentation states the trade plainly: lookup relationships are similar to master-detail except they do not support sharing or roll-up summary fields. [^1] The child can live without a parent, which is exactly what you want when "sometimes a contact is associated with an account, and sometimes it is just a contact". [^8] When the lookup field is optional, you also choose what happens to the child if the parent is deleted: clear the value (the default), block deletion of the referenced record, or, for a custom lookup field on a custom object only, delete the child too. That last option produces a cascade-delete, it must be enabled for your org by Salesforce, and it bypasses security and sharing settings, so users can lose records they had no access to. [^1][^2]

Conversion rules are a favorite exam surface because they encode the difference between the two types. You can convert a master-detail relationship to a lookup only if no roll-up summary fields exist on the master, and you can convert a lookup to master-detail only if the lookup field contains a value on every record. Converting to master-detail changes the child's organization-wide default to Controlled by Parent and updates the sharing model to public read and write. [^2] The practical instruction for architects is to model with a lookup first when you are unsure, then convert once the data is clean, because the platform will not let you go the other direction after a roll-up exists.

The platform also supports multilevel master-detail relationships, up to three custom detail levels, which are how you model a chain such as Account, Expense Report, Expense Line Item. [^2] Standard objects cannot sit on the detail side of a custom object in a master-detail relationship, which is worth knowing before someone asks you to hang a custom parent above Contact. [^2]

### Roll-up summary fields: the master side of master-detail, and nothing else

A roll-up summary field calculates values from related records and displays the result on the master record. The detail record must be related to the master through a master-detail relationship. [^3] That sentence is the whole rule, and it is the single most tested constraint in this lesson.

The available calculations are count, sum, minimum, and maximum. There is no average in the native roll-up summary field. [^3] Which fields you can aggregate depends on the calculation: number, currency, and percent fields for SUM, and dates and date-times join them for MIN and MAX. Auto-number fields cannot be aggregated, and certain fields cannot be used in the filter column at all, including long text areas, multi-select picklists, description fields, system fields such as Last Activity, cross-object formula fields, and lookup fields. [^3] The roll-up summary field type is not available for external objects, which follows from external objects having no master-detail relationships. [^2][^6]

Two behaviors matter for real designs and for the exam. First, when you define a roll-up summary on the master, you can no longer convert that master-detail relationship into a lookup relationship. [^3] Second, roll-ups can recalculate in bulk: changing the roll-up definition, changing a referenced formula, replacing picklist values used in the filter, or changing currency conversion rates all trigger a mass recalculation, which can take up to 30 minutes depending on record volume, and a mass recalculation does not fire workflow rules or field validations. [^3] Architects use that detail to explain why a validation rule built on a roll-up behaves differently during a data load than during a single record edit. Salesforce also prevents a user from saving a record that would invalidate a related record through a roll-up, which is why a validation rule on a parent can surface an error while the user is editing a child. [^3]

One nuance that separates candidates who understand the model from candidates who memorized a slogan: the platform does not roll up directly from a grandchild. In a multilevel master-detail relationship, a master can roll up fields on its detail records but cannot directly roll up fields on subdetail records. The detail record must hold a roll-up summary of the subdetail's field, and the master then rolls up from that intermediate field. [^2] The correct exam answer is "no direct grandparent roll-up; chain the roll-ups", not "grandparent roll-ups are impossible".

![Chained roll-up: an expense line item rolls up to the expense report, which holds an intermediate total that the account then rolls up, because a master cannot roll up a subdetail field directly](</diagrams/day-01-dm-1a-data-modeling-fundamentals-objects-fields-relation/3-chained-rollup.png>)


The deeper architectural point is what happens when the relationship is a lookup. Roll-ups do not exist there, so aggregation becomes a build decision: a record-triggered Flow that maintains a total, Apex that recalculates on child DML with bulkification in mind, or one of the declarative roll-up tools on the AppExchange that maintain totals over lookup relationships for you. The exam's phrasing is usually the tell. When a requirement says "the child must inherit the parent's ownership and sharing" and "totals must appear on the parent", you are being asked for master-detail, not for a clever lookup workaround. [^1][^3]

### Field types are schema contracts, not display choices

Choosing a field type is not a cosmetic decision. It sets storage, precision, editability, and how the data behaves in queries, reports, and integrations. [^6]

For text, the differences are concrete. Text fields hold up to 255 characters. Text Area fields also hold up to 255 characters but display them on separate lines. Text Area (Long) allows up to 131,072 characters. [^6] That matters in reports: for standard rich text and long text areas, the first 999 characters are available in a report, while for custom fields only the first 255 characters appear. [^6] Formula fields calculate values from other fields and merge fields and do not store the result as editable data. [^6] Percent fields lose precision beyond 15 decimal places, and number fields round the way the field is configured. [^6]

The picklist family splits on storage. A picklist lets a user select a single value from a list you define; a multi-select picklist lets a user select several and stores them separated by semicolons. [^6] That semicolon-delimited storage is the reason a multi-select behaves like a text blob when you start filtering, grouping, and reporting on it, even though it looks like a structured field in the UI. When a requirement needs reliable filtering and clean downstream analytics, model a single-select picklist plus a related object for the additional values rather than reaching for a multi-select.

Roll-Up Summary, Formula, Auto Number, Currency, and most encrypted types are not available for external objects, and Roll-Up Summary is the type that surprises people because the requirement ("show the total on the parent") sounds innocent. [^6] The general lesson is that the object category decides the legal field types before you open the new field wizard. [^6][^9]

Then there is the integration-facing pair: External ID and Unique. External ID fields hold a key from another system, which is what makes record matching possible rather than name-based guessing. The platform builds on this directly: an external lookup relationship matches the child's field against the standard External ID field on the parent external object, and an indirect lookup relationship links a child external object to a parent standard or custom object by matching a custom unique external ID field on the parent against the child's field values. [^2] When a migration requirement arrives, "does the target object have a unique external ID for the source key" is a real integrity question, and the answer decides whether your upserts are deterministic or whether you are matching on company names.

### Object features shape behavior: record types, validation, and the integrity layer

Record types are the platform's mechanism for showing different users different versions of the same object. Before creating them, the master picklist must contain every value any record type will use, because record types draw from it. You then define the record type, associate a business process for opportunity, case, lead, and solution record types, make it available and optionally default per profile, and choose whether the record type uses one page layout for all profiles or a different layout per profile. [^4]

The critical thing to understand, and the thing exam questions probe, is that record types control what is displayed and selectable in the UI. They decide which picklist values a user sees, which page layout renders, and which business process applies. [^4] They do not validate data. A record type will not stop a value reaching the database through the API, a data load, or an integration that ignores the UI. Data integrity belongs to a different layer: validation rules, required fields at the field level, and the automation that enforces business logic on save. Validation rules verify that the data a user enters meets the standards you specify before the record is saved, and they apply to new and updated records even when the fields they reference are not on the page layout or in the API call. [^5] That is the sentence to remember when someone proposes a record type as a data-quality control.

One interaction between these features is a genuine exam trap in reverse, because it looks like a limitation but is a capability. Roll-up summary fields are not displayed on edit pages, which means you can reference them in validation rules but cannot use them as the error location for a validation message. [^3] So a parent-level rule such as "the count of line items may not exceed 25" belongs on the child object, where the user can be shown an error at a field, rather than on the master where the message has nowhere sensible to land. [^3]

![Record types and validation rules sit in different layers: record types control picklist values, page layouts and business processes in the UI, while validation rules apply to new and updated records even when the fields they reference are absent from the page layout or the API call](</diagrams/day-01-dm-1a-data-modeling-fundamentals-objects-fields-relation/4-ui-layer-vs-data-layer.png>)


### Junction objects, trees, and the optional-relationship compromise

Many-to-many is not a relationship type on this platform. You model it: a junction object is a custom object with two master-detail relationships, one to each side. [^1] The documented consequences are what make it workable. Junction records are deleted when either associated master is deleted, and permanently if both are deleted. Sharing access to a junction record is determined by a user's access to both master records. The first master-detail relationship you create becomes the primary relationship, which controls the junction object's detail page color and icon, and the record ownership the junction inherits. Roll-up summary fields that summarize the junction can be created on both masters. Formula fields and validation rules on the junction can reference fields on both masters. And a junction object cannot become the master side of another master-detail relationship. [^2]

That last constraint is why the last question in this lesson's quiz lands where it does. When a requirement says a single custom object relates to both Accounts and Opportunities while remaining optional on each, two master-detail relationships are wrong, because two MDs make the child depend on both parents and hand the sharing model to the parents. The workable answers are two lookups, or one lookup plus one master-detail where the dependent side genuinely cannot exist without the other. [^1][^2] The polymorphic option looks attractive and is a trap in most contexts: the standard polymorphic relationship fields are the activity fields WhoId and WhatId on Task and Event, which can point at different parent types, and they are not a general-purpose custom capability. [^10] A custom object that must point at either of two parents gets two relationship fields plus a validation rule that requires at least one, not a polymorphic lookup. [^10]

For hierarchies, two constructs cover most needs. Self-relationships are lookup relationships from an object to itself, which is the pattern behind Account.ParentId; the documentation notes that a single record cannot be linked to itself, and the platform does not allow a many-to-many self-relationship, meaning the two master-detail relationships on a junction object cannot share the same master object. [^1][^2] The hierarchical relationship is a special lookup available only on the User object, which is how you model a manager chain. [^1][^2] That restriction is absolute, so "hierarchy field to Account" is never the answer to a custom-object question.

![Decision path for a child that relates to two parents: two lookups when both sides are optional, one master-detail plus one lookup when one side is required, a junction object for true many-to-many, and two relationship fields plus a validation rule for a polymorphic need](</diagrams/day-01-dm-1a-data-modeling-fundamentals-objects-fields-relation/5-two-parent-decision.png>)


## Configuration Walkthrough

Set this up in a sandbox as you read. The point is to feel which decisions the platform blocks and where.

1. Create the objects. From Setup, open Object Manager, choose Create, then Custom Object. Set the label, the plural label, the record name (Text or Auto Number) and its data type before you save, since the record name type is not casually changed later. [^7]
2. Add the fields. On the new object, open Fields & Relationships and choose New. Pick the exact field type rather than the convenient one, and use help text to document the meaning of the field for whoever inherits the org. Set the field as required at the field level only where the rule is unconditional; conditional requirements belong in a validation rule. [^6]
3. Create the relationship. Back in Fields & Relationships, choose New, then Lookup Relationship or Master-Detail Relationship. Choose the Related To object, name the field, and decide the two behaviors the platform asks you about: for master-detail, whether child records on a custom object may be reparented; for lookup, whether the field is required and what happens when the referenced record is deleted. Remember that the platform recommends creating a master-detail relationship before the custom object contains data, because you cannot add one to an object that already holds records. [^1][^2]
4. Add the roll-up. On the master object, create a new field and choose Roll-Up Summary. Select the summarized object (the detail side), the aggregate function (COUNT, SUM, MIN, or MAX), the field to aggregate where the function needs one, and any filter criteria. Then check whether any existing validation rule or automation assumed the old value. [^3]
5. Configure the record types. From the object's management settings, open Record Types, select Master as the starting point so you copy the full picklist, give the record type a label that is unique on the object, associate a business process where relevant, make it available and optionally default per profile, and choose whether one page layout applies to all profiles or a different layout applies per profile. [^4]

Then do the integrity layer in the right order: validation rules and required fields for what must be true, record types and page layouts for what people should see. [^4][^5]

## Exam Traps

**Trap one: assuming a lookup can drive a roll-up summary.** It cannot. Native roll-up summary fields require the detail record to relate to the master through a master-detail relationship, and lookup relationships explicitly do not support them. [^1][^3] If a question offers "lookup relationship plus a Roll-Up Summary field", that option is internally contradictory and can be eliminated on sight. Aggregating across a lookup means a Flow, Apex, or a declarative roll-up tool, and that is a design cost you should name out loud when you recommend it.

**Trap two: treating record types as data-integrity enforcement.** Record types control picklist values, page layouts, and business processes in the UI. [^4] A data load or integration that supplies a value outside the record type's picklist is not stopped by the record type, and validation rules apply to new and updated records whether or not the fields appear on a page layout. [^5] When the requirement is "this must never be true in the database", the answer is a validation rule, a field-level requirement, or automation, never a record type.

**Trap three: assuming a child can hold two master-detail relationships and stay independent.** Two MDs make the child dependent on both parents, and sharing is governed by the parents rather than by the child's own owner, because the detail side has no visible Owner field. [^1][^2] That is sometimes exactly right, as in a junction object where every record genuinely belongs to both sides. It is wrong the moment one side is optional, because the relationship field is required on the detail page layout. [^1] The compromise is one master-detail plus one lookup, or two lookups when both sides are optional and you can live without native roll-ups.

## Try It

Build both versions and observe the platform enforce its own rules.

Create a custom object called Offer with a master-detail relationship to a parent object. Add an Amount currency field and a roll-up summary field on the parent that sums Amount. Create a child record and confirm two things: the child's Owner matches the parent's owner without you setting it, and you cannot find a place to give the child its own sharing rule. [^1] Then open the relationship field definition and try to convert master-detail to lookup. The platform will refuse, because the roll-up summary exists on the master. [^3]

Now build a second custom object with a lookup relationship instead, and look for Roll-Up Summary in the new field type list on the parent. It is not there. [^3] Add a Flow or a small Apex trigger if you want to see what replacing that native behavior costs in maintenance, then delete the field and try converting the lookup to master-detail while a single record has an empty relationship field. The conversion fails until every record has a parent. [^2]

Finally, create a record type on the first object with a restricted picklist, then load a record with a value that is not in that record type's picklist using the Data Import Wizard or the API. The record saves. That is the single best demonstration that record types govern the UI and validation rules govern the data. [^4][^5]
