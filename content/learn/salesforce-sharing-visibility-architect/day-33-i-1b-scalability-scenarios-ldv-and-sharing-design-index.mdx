Lesson 32 taught you the arithmetic underneath every sharing choice: a share row is a record plus a grantee, and the table grows with the product of the two. Lesson 33 stops teaching the model and hands you an actual scenario to design. This is requirement i-1b, the second half of the Implications of Security Model Choice domain, and the exam wording is specific: given a scenario, design the sharing solution to scale on large data volumes, including indexing considerations and recalc avoidance.[^1]

The distinction from yesterday matters. Yesterday you recognized the problem, that a design multiplies rows or recalculates constantly. Today you produce the design that does not have that problem in the first place. The exam presents an org with millions of records and an access requirement, then asks which sharing solution scales. Every correct answer is built from the same small set of moves: tighten the OWD so the baseline creates no rows, satisfy access with a handful of broad criteria rules pointed at groups, keep those rules on stable fields, batch the ownership changes and schedule the recalcs, and keep the share table small enough that its indexes stay useful. Learn to apply those moves in order and the scenario questions stop being a guess and become a checklist.[^2]

## Define the terms

**Large data volume (LDV).** An org, object, or table that holds so many records that standard operations stop being instant and start being measured in how many rows the platform has to touch. The exact threshold is elastic, but for sharing design the practical meaning is the same everywhere: when an object holds tens of millions of records, every per-record decision multiplies into real cost, and a sharing design has to account for row volume before it accounts for individual features. LDV is the condition that turns every sharing mechanism from a checkbox into a row-counting exercise.[^2]

**Organization-wide default (OWD).** The baseline sharing setting for an object, the access everyone has to records they do not own just by default. Private and Public Read Only are restrictive, meaning the platform grants nothing (or only read) by default and every exception has to be created explicitly; Public Read/Write is permissive, meaning the default alone satisfies broad access without a single share row. For LDV design the restrictive OWD is the starting point, because it keeps the table from growing for free, and the permissive OWD is only sound for data where truly everyone may read or edit it all.[^2]

**Criteria-based sharing rule.** A sharing rule that evaluates field conditions on records and grants access to a target public group, role, or territory for the records that match. A single rule is coarse and broad: it says one boundary, one target group, and it applies across every matching record. That coarseness is the entire scalability advantage, because the platform stores the grant at the group level and the share rows stay near the count of matching records rather than scaling with the number of people who need access or the decisions someone makes record by record.[^2]

**Share row.** One row in an object's underlying share table, representing one explicit grant of access to one record for one grantee. The size and health of the share table, which is its row count and how well its indexes hold up, is the single deciding factor in whether a sharing design scales on LDV. Every design decision in this lesson is ultimately a decision about share rows.[^2]

**Recalculation.** The process by which the platform re-evaluates sharing rules and re-derives the affected share rows when OWD, rules, groups, roles, or ownership change. On LDV a recalculation touches an enormous number of rows, it runs asynchronously and in parallel where the platform supports it, and it can be slow enough to need scheduling. Recalc avoidance is the discipline of designing so the platform has to recompute the table as rarely as possible.[^3]

**Defer Sharing Calculations.** The Salesforce feature that lets an admin temporarily suspend sharing rule and group membership calculations during a large maintenance operation, so a flood of configuration changes does not force the engine to recalculate after every single one. You make all the changes while calculation is suspended, then resume and run one full recalculation in the maintenance window. This is the tool for turning a cascade of recalcs into a single scheduled one.[^3]

**Index.** The database structure that lets a query find records without scanning every row. On the share side, share rows are indexed by fields such as the parent record and the user or group, and queries that must resolve the sharing model use those indexes to find the relevant grants fast. An index is only useful when the table it points at stays reasonably sized, which is why table bloat defeats the index: an enormous share table weakens the efficiency of the sharing-model lookups until the cost of the join dominates.[^4]

**Managed share reconciliation.** The process of keeping Apex-managed share rows in sync with the records and users they describe, adding rows for new grants and deleting them when they become obsolete. Left unreconciled, managed shares accumulate like any other per-record grant, so a reconciliation job is how an architect bounds the table growth the code introduces.

**Data partitioning.** Splitting one large population into separate objects, separate fields, or separate orgs, each with its own OWD and sharing model, so no single share table has to hold the whole strained population. It is the escalation answer when no amount of declarative tuning makes a genuinely huge, genuinely fine-grained requirement scale.[^2]

## Why it matters for the exam and for real builds

Requirement i-1b is a design objective, and the exam tests it with scenarios. The standard shape is an org at large data volume, an access requirement that sounds reasonable one record at a time, and four sharing designs, of which exactly one scales. The exam wants you to pick the design, and it rewards the judgment underneath: the coarser and broader you can make each grant, the smaller the table; the more stable the fields your rules depend on, the rarer the recalculation.

That judgment is not only for the exam. It is the difference between an architect who sets up a model and one who sets up a model the org can still use in eighteen months. I have watched a design that passed correctness on every check still collapse in production, not because anyone did the sharing wrong, but because the design chose a per-record mechanism on a high-volume object and the share table crept past tens of millions of rows by year two. Correctness and scalability are different tests, and on LDV the scalability test is the one that fails first in the real world. The platform can always honor a correct row; it cannot honor fifty million of them quickly no matter how well each one was made.[^2]

So the question this lesson trains you to answer whenever a scenario mentions a large data volume: does this design keep the share table lean, and does it keep the platform from rebuilding it constantly? If both answers are yes, the design scales. If either is no, keep working until both are yes, or escalate to reconsider the requirement.

## Design the sharing solution for large data volumes

The core LDV design is deceptively short, and the exam returns to it relentlessly. Prefer a restrictive OWD with a small number of broad, criteria-based rules to groups. That is the entire pattern in one sentence, and every correct large-data-volume answer on the exam is a variation of it. Let me unpack why each clause earns its place.

Start with the restrictive OWD. A Private OWD grants nothing by default, so the platform creates no share rows just from the baseline. Any access that most users should not have never appears in the table at all. A permissive Public OWD is only appropriate where genuinely everyone may read or edit the data, because it satisfies that broad access through the default setting rather than through rows, which is efficient, but it is useless as a base for a data set most people should not see. The restrictive OWD is the correct baseline on LDV because it guarantees the table starts empty and grows only when you grant an exception.[^2]

Then add the few broad criteria rules to groups. A criteria rule states one boundary, for example records in the Americas region, and targets one group, for example the Americas Analysts public group. Because the target is a group, the platform stores the group membership once and does not need a per-person grant for every matching record. Because the rule is broad, one rule covers every record that matches the criteria instead of someone sharing each record individually. The result is a small number of group-level rows that scale with the matching record count, not with the number of people or the number of manual decisions.[^2]

This is the exact judgment quiz question one tests. An object holds 50 million records and needs three analyst groups to each see a subset by region. The most scalable design is a restrictive OWD plus three criteria-based sharing rules, one per region, each targeting the group for that region. Each rule grants its group access to the matching region's records, producing a small number of coarse, group-level share rows across 50 million records. The wrong answers are the per-record reflexes: per-record teams on every record, manual sharing per record, and a deep role hierarchy. All three create rows per record, and across 50 million records that is the multiplication this lesson exists to prevent. Teams add a row per member per record, manual shares add a row per record per grantee created by hand, and a deep hierarchy adds a row per ancestor per record. Each satisfies the access in a way that collapses the moment the object is large.[^2]

The design-intent rule is the same one from lesson 32, now applied: satisfy the requirement with the fewest share rows. Restrictive OWD for the baseline. Broad criteria rules to groups for the exceptions. Coarse boundaries instead of fine-grained per-record access. Every one of these is a decision to keep the product of records times grantees small, and on LDV that decision is the whole game.

## Avoid mechanisms that create per-record share rows on high-volume objects

The flip side of the design rule is the list of mechanisms to avoid, but the list is really one idea repeated: anything that writes a per-record grant on a high-volume object multiplies rows with every record. Teams, manual shares, Apex shares written per record, and deep hierarchies are the four offenders, and the exam wants you to recognize each by its row multiplier.

Teams, Account Teams, Opportunity Teams, and case teams attach a named set of users to a single record. Each team member with access to the record is a grant, so the row count is records times members. A rollout that puts six collaborators on every one of a hundred thousand accounts is six hundred thousand grant rows before anything else has happened. On an object holding millions of records, teams multiply out of control almost immediately.[^2]

Manual sharing is the classic offline offender, because it is per record, per grantee, created by hand, and almost never cleaned up. One user trying to do their job shares a handful of records today, and a month of that across an org produces a share table nobody consciously decided to build. On LDV manual sharing is not a sharing tool, it is a row factory with no off switch.

Apex managed sharing lets you grant access programmatically, which is necessary for boundaries the declarative tools cannot express, but each insert is still a share row. The scalability is entirely in how the code writes them. Apex that inserts one managed share per record per member multiplies exactly like teams do. The discipline is to write shares in bulk, in batches, for coarse boundaries, and to run a reconciliation job that deletes obsolete managed shares so the table does not accumulate forever.

Deep role hierarchies add the subtle one, because nobody consciously grants anything. When an object has Grant Access Using Hierarchies enabled, Salesforce materializes hierarchy-derived access into share rows per ancestor. A record owned by a rep in a six-level hierarchy yields up to five ancestor rows, so a million records approach five million rows for access that, in most cases, only one immediate manager needed. The hierarchy is enabled by default on many objects, so this bloat can be happening in an org where nobody ever meant to build it. On LDV a deep hierarchy on a high-volume object is a standing multiplier you must explicitly account for.[^2]

The exam trap here is the contrast. Faced with a large-data-volume object and a requirement that several groups each see a subset, the per-record mechanisms all look like they solve the problem, and they all scale terribly. The correct answer is always to step back to the coarse boundary: restrictive OWD plus a few criteria rules to groups. Remember that the mechanisms exist and are useful at normal volume; the exam is testing whether you know they stop being useful the moment the record count climbs.

## Recalc avoidance: keep criteria on stable fields, batch ownership, schedule the rebuild

Table size is one half of the scalability story; recalculation frequency is the other, and this lesson's design work includes the specific moves that keep the platform from rebuilding the table constantly. Recalc avoidance is not an optional optimization, on LDV it is the difference between a model you can change and a model that punishes every change with a slow, lock-heavy recompute. Recalculations happen when OWD, sharing rules, groups, roles, or ownership change, because the platform has to re-derive the affected share rows. The way to avoid the cost is to control when those triggers fire.[^3]

The first and most important move is to keep criteria rules on stable fields. This is quiz question two in its cleanest form. A criteria-based sharing rule is re-evaluated whenever its criteria field changes on a record, so a rule built on a field that churns during routine work re-runs constantly. Consider a rule that shares cases where Status equals Escalated. Every time a rep flips a case to or from Escalated, the platform re-evaluates that rule for that record and adds or removes the grant. On a small object that is a daily triage convenience. On an object where millions of records flip that field as part of ordinary editing, it is a standing recalc tax that the org pays with every save. The architect's move is to base criteria rules on fields that change rarely, fields that represent a stable classification of the record rather than a transient work state, or to split the design so frequently-changing access is handled by a mechanism that does not re-derive everything.[^3]

The second move is to batch ownership changes. Ownership transfer is a recalculation trigger, because when a record changes hands the ownership-based grants and the hierarchy grants above the new owner must be re-derived. A design that changes ownership one record at a time, or that lets a reorganization blast through thousands of re-assignments without preparation, recalculates on every individual transfer. Batching the transfers, doing them in bulk during a maintenance window instead of scatter-shot through the day, concentrates the recalculation into a known event you can schedule and monitor rather than a constant background tax.

The third move is to schedule large recalcs. When a large-scale maintenance operation or org realignment forces a big recompute, you do not let it fire as a cascade. That is what the Defer Sharing Calculations feature is for: you suspend sharing rule and group membership calculations, make all your changes while calculation is off, then resume and run one full sharing rule recalculation in the planned maintenance window. After you complete the work, you must resume the calculations and manually initiate a full recalculation, or you risk leaving the org with incomplete access. The feature exists precisely so a flood of configuration changes does not force the engine to recalculate after every single one, and it is the architect's tool for turning a recalc cascade into a single scheduled event. In parallel, the platform adds the Async Deletion of Obsolete Shares feature, which removes obsolete shares asynchronously after groups are deleted or users are deactivated, so an admin does not have to wait on share deletion to keep working. Both are recalc-avoidance machinery the exam expects you to know exist.[^3]

The cost you are avoiding is real. Recalculation on large data volumes is CPU and I/O heavy, potentially slow enough to time out, and potentially lock-heavy enough to contend with the transactions trying to run at the same time. The point of the three moves is not to prevent recalculation forever, that is impossible, it is to make it rare, deliberate, and scheduled instead of constant, accidental, and concurrent with production traffic.[^3]

## Indexing and the share table: keep it lean so the indexes keep working

The third pillar of this lesson is indexing, and the exam frames it as a warning you must internalize: a big, badly indexed sharing table degrades queries even when each individual grant looks reasonable. This is the subtle part of LDV design, because the failure is not visible in the data model as a wrong grant. Every grant is correct. The problem is that the table has grown so large that the mechanism the platform uses to enforce access stops being fast.

Here is how it works. Share rows are indexed by fields such as the parent record and the user or group. When a user runs a query, the platform must resolve the sharing model: it has to determine which records of an object the user is entitled to see, and it does that through the sharing table. For a selective query on an ordinary org, the share-table indexes let the platform find the relevant grants quickly, and the whole access check is cheap. The indexes are real and they are doing useful work.[^4]

The problem is that an index is only as good as the table it points at. As the share table grows toward tens of millions of rows, the sharing-model join gets longer and the indexes have to cover far more rows, until the cost of resolving sharing through the bloated table dominates the query. The efficiency of the sharing-model lookups degrades precisely because the table is enormous, and no amount of proper indexing saves you once the table has outgrown practical index usefulness. Keeping the table lean is the way the indexes stay helpful: a small table means the join is short, the indexes are selective, and the access check stays fast even at high query volume.[^4]

This is quiz question three. Why does sharing-table bloat degrade query performance even on indexed fields? Not because indexes are disabled by sharing, and not because queries ignore sharing, which would be a security hole, and not because reports cache something. The reason is that queries must resolve sharing through the share table, and when the table has grown enormously, even indexed lookups slow down and the cost of the sharing join dominates. A bloated sharing table weakens the efficiency of the sharing-model joins and queries and can exceed the practical benefit of the indexes, so the discipline is to keep the table lean before the indexes stop working, not after.[^4]

The practical read for the architect: index design is not an alternative to table-leanness, it depends on it. You cannot fix a fifty-million-row share table by adding an index and calling it done, because the index alone does not make the join short. You fix it by refusing to create the rows in the first place, with restrictive OWD and coarse criteria rules to groups, and by keeping per-record mechanisms and constant recalculation out of the design. The index is the reward for an already-lean table, not the rescue for a bloated one.

## Monitor: watch the share table grow after every change

Design is not a one-time activity; it is a loop, and the loop needs a monitor. After any model change, of the OWD, a sharing rule, a group, or an ownership transfer, the architect watches whether the share table grew the way the design predicted. This is how a model that was correct at build time stays correct as the org grows, and it is how you catch a design that is multiplying more than intended before it becomes an incident.

Salesforce surfaces this through sharing-related reports and the Sharing tab. The Sharing tab on an object shows individual users or groups that have explicit access to a record, and for a low-volume check it shows the grants a rule created. For the volume question, you look at the reports that count access or that summarize the sharing model, and at the System Overview that shows how the org is using its data and storage. The goal is not to watch one record, it is to watch the trajectory: is the share table staying near the matching-record count the design predicted, or is it climbing toward record-times-grantee scale because a rule is broader than intended, a team rollout is wider than planned, or a managed-share job is leaking rows it never reconciles?

When you spot growth that does not match the design, find the mechanism that is multiplying before you tune anything else. A rule matching far more records than expected, a team structure that grew wider than the design assumed, a hierarchy deeper than anyone meant to build, a managed-share job that never deletes obsolete rows, each has a specific fix. The monitor tells you which one you have, and the design discipline tells you how to correct it, by narrowing the rule, disbanding the team, flattening the hierarchy, or scheduling the reconciliation. Monitor growth, explain the growth, and correct the mechanism that caused it.[^2]

## The escalation path when a requirement cannot scale declaratively

Every LDV design has a limit, and honoring that limit is part of the architect's job. When a requirement demands per-record granularity on a high-volume object, no amount of declarative tuning makes per-record access over a huge population fast. At that point the honest move is not to keep tuning the same mechanism, it is to reconsider what the requirement is really asking for, and the exam wants you to name the three options in order of preference.

The first is to revisit the requirement, to reduce granularity. Ask whether the business truly needs per-record distinction, or whether a coarser boundary can do the job while the sharp distinctions are handled where they do not scale the whole table. Often a requirement that sounds per-record resolves, once interrogated, into a handful of groups seeing different populations, which is a sharing-rule problem and scales beautifully. Do not keep a requirement that literally cannot scale and hope the platform finds a way; change the requirement to something the platform can honor.

The second is to partition the data. Where different populations must genuinely never see each other's records at volume, split the data by region, business unit, segment, or customer into separate objects or separate orgs, each with its own OWD and sharing model. Partitioning bounds the table growth to a size the platform can serve, because no single share table has to hold the whole strained population. It is more work up front and more surface to maintain, but it is the correct answer when the requirement is genuinely enormous and genuinely fine-grained.

The third is to move the access decision to Apex, where you control and reconcile the row volume. Apex managed sharing lets you express boundaries the declarative tools cannot, and because your code decides when and how many rows to insert, you can batch, coarse-grain, and reconcile the volume yourself. This is not a license to write per-record shares in a loop; that would multiply rows like any other per-record mechanism. It is a move to make when your code can express a boundary more cheaply than rows of declarative grants, and when you bind the row count through bulk inserts and reconciliation. Use it deliberately, with the volume under your control, and verify it in a sandbox before it touches production data.

The escalation path is arranged by preference: reduce granularity first, partition second, and reach for Apex managed sharing only when the requirement genuinely needs it and you can keep the row volume reconciled. Name the option before you promise a timeline, because only that choice moves the model from collapsing to servable.[^2]

## Configuring a scalable LDV sharing design, step by step

Walk the design in order so each step hands the next its input.

**Step 1: Quantify the record volume and the per-record grant growth.** Before you build anything, count the object's records and estimate what each candidate mechanism would do to the share table. Write down the record count. Then, for each mechanism under consideration, compute its multiplier: records times average team members, records times average ancestor depth, records times manual or managed grants per record, or matching-records for each criteria rule. Add the per-mechanism totals and you have your projected share-table growth. If the total is millions, the design needs to shrink before it reaches production, not after.[^2]

**Step 2: Choose the least-row mechanism that satisfies access.** Start from the restrictive OWD so the baseline creates no rows. Then satisfy each access requirement with the mechanism that adds the fewest rows: a few broad criteria rules to groups beats teams, manual sharing, Apex-per-record shares, and deep hierarchies on a high-volume object. Keep the rules coarse, one boundary, one group, and keep them few. This step is the design-intent rule applied: satisfy the requirement with the fewest share rows.[^2]

**Step 3: Keep criteria rules on stable fields and schedule recalcs off-peak or batched.** Review every criteria rule's field and write down when it changes. If a rule's field churns during routine work, the platform re-runs that rule constantly, so move the rule to a stable classification field or restructure the frequently-changing access. For any required recalculation, batch the triggering changes and run them in a maintenance window, using Defer Sharing Calculations for large realignments so the engine recomputes once at the end rather than after every change.[^3]

**Step 4: Monitor sharing-table growth with Sharing Overview and reports after each model change.** After you deploy the OWD, rules, and any group or ownership change, watch the share table's trajectory. Use the Sharing tab for individual grant checks and the sharing-related reports and System Overview for volume. If the table grows beyond what the design predicted, find the multiplying mechanism and correct it, whether that is a broader-than-expected rule, a wider team rollout, or a leaking managed-share job, before it balloons.[^2]

**Step 5: Revisit the requirement if per-record granularity is demanded on an LDV object.** When the requirement genuinely asks for per-record access over a huge population, do not force it into the sharing model. Reduce granularity by finding the coarser boundary the business actually needs, partition the data into segments that bound table growth, or move the access decision to Apex where you control and reconcile row volume. Name which one before you promise a timeline.[^2]

## Worked example: design a sharing solution for a 50-million-record object

Put the steps together in the scenario the exam most likes to build. A financial-services org has a custom object holding 50 million account-related records. Three analyst groups, Americas, EMEA, and APAC, each need to see only the records for their region, and every member of each group must be able to read and update the records in its region. The org asks you to design the sharing solution so it scales.

Run step one, the arithmetic. The object holds 50 million records. There are no per-record mechanisms in the design yet, so the projected table growth depends entirely on what you choose. The requirement is coarse by nature: three groups, three regions, no request for per-record distinction within a region. That is the shape a criteria-rule design handles best.

Now step two, the least-row mechanism. Set the object's OWD to Private so the baseline grants nothing and creates no share rows. Then create three criteria-based sharing rules, one per region. The Americas rule shares records where Region equals Americas with the Americas Analysts group, and likewise for EMEA and APAC. If each region holds roughly a third of the records, each rule produces on the order of 15 to 17 million sharing rows, the matching-record count for its region, and all three together stay near the full 50-million record count. Compare that to any per-record alternative: teams on 50 million records, manual sharing per record, or a deep hierarchy adding an ancestor row per record, each of which multiplies well past 50 million and collapses the table. The criteria-rule design wins because it shares to groups, and group membership is stored once, not per record.

Step three, recalc avoidance. Ensure the Region field is stable, which it is by design here, a record's region does not change as part of routine editing, so the rules re-evaluate rarely. Ownership changes are batched into a maintenance window rather than fired through the day, and any large realignment uses Defer Sharing Calculations so the engine recomputes once instead of after every change. The design keeps recalculation rare and scheduled instead of constant and concurrent with traffic.[^3]

Step four, monitor. After the rules deploy, watch the share table stay near the matching-record count per region, and use the Sharing Overview and reports to confirm it is not climbing toward record-times-grantee scale. Any team rollout or managed-share job that appears later gets reviewed the same way, because on LDV the trajectory, not the single grant, is what tells you the design is staying lean.

Step five, escalation is not needed here because the requirement is coarse. But if the business later asks for per-record distinctions within a 15-million-record region, the answer is the escalation path: revisit the requirement to find the coarser boundary, partition the region into segments, or move the sharp sub-boundaries to Apex managed sharing with a reconciliation job. The coarse rule design is the scalable base, and every sharper demand is handled on top of it only where the volume allows.

## Exam traps to avoid

**Trap: reaching for teams, manual shares, or a deep hierarchy on an LDV object.** All three create per-record share rows, a row per member, per grantee, or per ancestor for every record, and on a 50-million-record object they multiply to a table that collapses performance. The lean answer is always restrictive OWD plus a few broad criteria rules to groups, which the exam tests directly. When a scenario contrasts these on a large data volume, the criteria-rule design wins, and it is not close.[^2]

**Trap: ignoring the hidden cost of constant recalculation from changing criteria fields.** A criteria rule on a field that churns during routine work re-runs on every change, and on LDV that is a standing tax on every save. Avoid expensive recalculation by basing rules on stable fields, batching ownership changes, and scheduling any necessary recalcs off-peak or batched, using Defer Sharing Calculations for large realignments. Do not promise a design that lets a frequently-changing field drive constant recomputes.[^3]

**Trap: assuming a big share table is fine because the fields are indexed.** An index is only as useful as the table it points at. Queries must resolve sharing through the share table, so when it grows enormously, even indexed lookups slow down and the cost of the sharing join dominates. Table bloat weakens index efficiency and degrades performance even though every grant looks reasonable. Keep the table lean before the indexes stop working, and never treat an index as the rescue for bloat.[^4]

## Try it yourself

In a scratch or sandbox org, prove the LDV design lesson in miniature. Create a custom object with a Region picklist and about a thousand records spread across three regions, set its OWD to Private, and create three criteria-based sharing rules, one per region, each targeting a different public group. Then run a query against the object's underlying share records and count the rows: you will see the count stay near your matching-record population for each region, not multiply by the number of group members.

Next, test the recalc avoidance. Create a criteria rule on a field, change that field on a handful of records, and watch the rule re-evaluate; then change a stable field and note that nothing re-evaluates. Feel the difference between a rule on a churning field and a rule on a stable one, then imagine the same single change across millions of records to internalize why stable fields matter.

Then model the index lesson. Build the same object with a small share table and run a selective query, note how fast the sharing check resolves. Now create many more records with per-region shares to bloat the table and re-run the query, and you will feel the sharing-model join get slower as the table grows, even though the fields are indexed. Finally, test the escalation instinct in your own words: explain why a per-record, per-user requirement over a huge population cannot be fixed by an index, and which of the three escalation moves you would pick first. Each test turns this lesson's design rules into something you have watched the platform do.

## The design frame, in one screen

Keep requirement i-1b in one frame. On large data volumes, design the sharing solution as a restrictive OWD with a small number of broad, criteria-based rules to groups, because coarse group-level grants produce few share rows and keep the table near the matching-record count. Avoid anything that writes per-record rows on a high-volume object, teams, manual shares, Apex shares per record, and deep hierarchies, because each multiplies with every record. Avoid frequent recalculation by keeping criteria rules on stable fields, batching ownership changes, and scheduling large recalcs off-peak or batched with Defer Sharing Calculations. Keep the share table lean so its indexes stay useful, because a bloated table degrades even indexed queries when the sharing join dominates. Monitor the table's growth with the Sharing Overview and sharing-related reports after every change. And when a requirement cannot scale declaratively, reduce granularity, partition the data, or move the access decision to Apex managed sharing where you control and reconcile row volume. Do those and the sharing model scales with the data instead of against it.[^2][^3][^4]
