You have spent thirty-one lessons learning what each sharing mechanism does and when the spec says to use it. Lesson 32 steps back from the mechanism and asks the question that separates an architect from an admin who knows the Setup pages: what does each choice *cost*? Every sharing rule, every manual share, every hierarchy-enabled record quietly writes rows into the Sharing table, and at large data volume (LDV) those rows stop being free.[^2]

This is the opening lesson of the Implications of Security Model Choice domain (18% of the exam), and its objective, requirement i-1a, is blunt: given a scenario, determine the scalability implications of the sharing solution. The exam hands you a design, sometimes a sensible one, and asks what it will do to the platform when the org grows big. The answer is almost always about two things: how many share rows the design creates, and how often the platform has to rebuild them. Nail those two and you can reason about any sharing design's scaling profile without memorizing a feature list.

## Define the terms

**Sharing table.** The internal table (technically a set of tables, one per shareable object, with the AccountShare, OpportunityShare, and custom-object share tables being the ones you see in the data model) that stores individual record-access grants that go beyond what OWD and ownership already provide. Each row is a single grant: a parent record, a user or group granted access, and the access level (Read, Read/Write, ...). Manual shares, Apex managed shares, sharing-rule results, team memberships, and hierarchy-derived grants all land here as rows. The size of this table is the single thing that decides whether your sharing design scales.[^2]

**Share row.** One row in an object's underlying share table, representing one explicit grant of access to one record for one grantee. When you hear "sharing-table bloat" or "sharing-table growth," it means the count of these rows climbing into the tens or hundreds of millions.

**Recalculation.** The process by which the platform recomputes the sharing table for a set of records. It happens when sharing rules change, when record ownership transfers, when role hierarchy membership changes, or when a per-record sharing operation requires the platform to re-derive downstream grants. On LDV, recalculation touches an enormous number of rows, and it is neither instant nor cheap.[^3]

**Criteria-based sharing rule.** A sharing rule that evaluates one or more field conditions on records and grants access to a target group for only the records that match. The rule result rows land in the Sharing table, but the key for scalability is that a single rule targets a *group*, so the row count is tied to the matching records, not to the number of people who need access.

**Ownership-based sharing rule.** A sharing rule that grants access to members of a group that is higher in the hierarchy than, or otherwise related to, the role of the record owner. It is simpler to set up than a criteria rule and exists before you even write criteria; it also writes rows into the Sharing table.

**Manual share.** A record access grant that a user (or an admin) creates explicitly for an individual user, group, or role from the record's Sharing button. It is a per-record, per-grantee row, and it is the classic source of unbounded growth when people use it as a daily workaround instead of designing the sharing model.

**Apex managed share.** A share row created programmatically through Apex, usually for a custom scenario the declarative tools cannot express. Each insert is one row (or a batch of rows) into the share table, and rows added through Apex are the developer's responsibility to insert, update, and delete.

**Team.** A named, per-record collection of users attached to a single record (an Account Team, an Opportunity Team). Each team member with access to the record represents a grant; across many records and many members, team structures multiply share-table and related index rows quickly.

**Role hierarchy.** The org's reporting-line structure. When an object has "Grant Access Using Hierarchies" enabled (on by default), a user can access records owned by users lower in their hierarchy. The hierarchy itself is not a row table, but on many objects Salesforce materializes hierarchy-derived access into share rows per ancestor, which is exactly why deep hierarchies bloat the table.[^2][^4]

## Why it matters for the exam and for real builds

Requirement i-1a is a judgment objective: the exam hands you a sharing design inside a scenario, and the "implication" you must name is almost always one of three outcomes. The first is table growth, a design that multiplies share rows until queries and flush operations slow down. The second is recalc cost, a design that forces the platform to rebuild a huge number of rows repeatedly. The third is the breaking point, a requirement that is structurally impossible to satisfy at scale with per-record sharing, forcing you to revisit the requirement or partition the data.

In production this is where architectures quietly die. I have seen an org where every regional sales rep "shared" every hard-won account to their own small team, a reasonable instinct one record at a time, and the AccountShare table grew past forty million rows within a year. Another where the role hierarchy was seven levels deep and every case at the bottom spawned one hierarchy share row per ancestor, so a million cases produced millions of rows nobody had consciously created. In both cases the sharing *works*, in the sense that the right people can see the right records. The problem is that the platform is spending more and more of its life writing, flushing, and re-deriving rows, and the whole org slows down.

The exam wants you to predict that outcome from the design before it happens. That requires thinking in terms of the multiplication underneath each mechanism: sharing table size is roughly records times grantees. A design that grants access to groups stays close to the record count. A design that grants access to individuals, or that materializes per-record, per-ancestor rows, multiplies far faster. Learn to estimate the multiplying factor of each mechanism, and the "scalability implication" of any scenario becomes a small arithmetic problem you can run in your head.

## The Sharing table grows with records times grantees

The single most important sentence in this lesson: a share row is a record plus a grantee. So the Sharing table's size does not depend on how many records you have alone, and it does not depend on how many people you have alone. It depends on the product of the two, the number of grants you chose to materialize. Every mechanism you pick is really a decision about what that multiplication looks like.[^2]

![A comparison table showing how each sharing mechanism multiplies share rows, ending with the rule that sharing table size roughly equals records times grantees](</diagrams/day-32-i-1a-scalability-fundamentals-sharing-table-growth-reca/1-mechanism-multipliers.png>)

Let me make that concrete. Suppose you own a service org with 1 million case records, and you need the managers of each case's owner to see those cases. Two ways to satisfy it, wildly different table sizes.

Approach one, the role hierarchy. Enable "Grant Access Using Hierarchies" on the case object, put managers and directors above the reps, and let the platform grant upward access. On a three-level hierarchy (rep, manager, director), each case owned by a rep yields up to two hierarchy share rows, one for the manager ancestor, one for the director ancestor. One million cases, three levels, roughly two million share rows. Now imagine the same hierarchy built eight levels deep; a record near the bottom yields up to seven ancestor rows, so one million cases approach seven million rows, for a requirement that only ever needed one manager's eyes. That is the classic hierarchy bloat driver, a share row per ancestor per record.[^2]

Approach two, a criteria- or ownership-based sharing rule to a group. One rule says "share all cases with the Sales Management group." The target is a single group of an arbitrary number of managers, but the platform stores group membership separately and does not need a per-manager row for every case. The row count stays close to the number of matching records rather than crawling toward records times group members. One million cases, one rule, on the order of the matching-record count. The rule did more with fewer rows, because it shares to a group instead of to individuals.

![Two approaches to the same requirement, a side-by-side comparison showing a role hierarchy creating roughly two million share rows versus one sharing rule to a group staying near the matching-record count](</diagrams/day-32-i-1a-scalability-fundamentals-sharing-table-growth-reca/2-hierarchy-vs-group.png>)

This is the heart of quiz question one. The design MOST likely to cause sharing-table bloat on large data volumes is a deep role hierarchy where every record gains an ancestor row, combined with large per-record teams, because each adds a share row per granted ancestor or member and the two multiply across every record. The lean designs are the inverse: a restrictive OWD with a few sharing rules to groups, or a single criteria rule to one group. A read-only Public OWD, by the way, does not bloat the table either, because it grants the same access to everyone through the OWD setting instead of through rows.[^2]

The arithmetic runs the same way for every mechanism. Manual shares are the worst multiplier, because they are per record, per individual, created by hand, row by row, and they almost never get cleaned up. Apex managed shares let you insert batches in bulk, which is better than manual, but each insert is still a row, and if your code inserts one managed share per record per member it multiplies exactly like anything else. Teams, Account Teams, Opportunity Teams, multiply per record per member, and a rollout that puts six collaborators on every one of a hundred thousand accounts is six hundred thousand grant rows before you have done anything else.[^2]

The architected pattern, and the design-intent rule this whole objective rests on, is to satisfy the requirement with the FEWEST share rows. Keep OWD restrictive so the table does not grow from the default. Share to groups rather than individuals, because a group membership is stored once and shared across records. Prefer a few criteria rules that match only the necessary records over many per-record grants. That is the entire scalability discipline in one sentence: population-level rules to groups are always leaner than per-record grants.

## Recalculation cost is the hidden tax on every change

Table *size* is the obvious scaling problem. Recalculation cost is the one that surprises people, because it does not show up in the data model as a growing table. It shows up as the org slowing down, or worse, as a change that times out or locks, the moment someone edits a rule or transfers a batch of records. Recalculation is the process of recomputing the sharing table, and at scale it is expensive because it is not a single row update; it is the platform re-deriving and validating many rows at once, over a large volume, under locks.[^3]

Here is what triggers a recalculation. When you edit a sharing rule's criteria or its target group, the records that matched the old criteria and the records that now match the new criteria must be re-evaluated, and the share rows added and removed to reflect the change. When a record changes ownership, whether through the UI, the API, or a transfer, the ownership-based grants and the hierarchy grants above the new owner must be re-derived for that record. When a user's role changes, or a user is moved in the hierarchy, the access they gained and lost ripples across the records they and their reports own. Each of these is a decision to re-run the sharing engine over a set of records.

![A flow diagram walking from the three recalculation triggers through what the engine re-derives to the cost at scale, ending with the design consequence to minimize recalculation frequency](</diagrams/day-32-i-1a-scalability-fundamentals-sharing-table-growth-reca/3-recalculation-cost.png>)

Why is that expensive on LDV? Rebuilding a share row is not free in CPU or I/O, and it is not free in locks. A single ownership transfer on a small org is instant, one record, a handful of rows, done. The same transfer on an org with five million records owned under one transfer, or on a deeply hierarchical structure where one record's re-derivation cascades to related records, forces the engine to touch the share table at scale. On large data volumes the recalculation can be slow enough to time the job out, and lock-heavy enough to contend with the queries and transactions that are trying to run at the same time. The cost is not the number of shares you *intend*; it is the number of rows the engine must *rebuild* to honor the change.

That is precisely quiz question two. Recalculation is expensive at scale because it rebuilds and validates many share rows across large volumes, which is CPU and I/O heavy, slow, and potentially lock-heavy, so designs should minimize how often recalculation is needed. The wrong answers are the ones that treat it as trivial, changing page layouts, affecting only reports, or requiring a license. None of those is what recalculation does. It recomputes the sharing table, and that is the expensive part.

The design consequence is to minimize recalculation *frequency*. The most common driver of unwanted recalcs is a criteria-based sharing rule whose criteria field changes often in the normal course of work. If a rule says "share cases where Status equals Escalated," then every time a rep flips a case to or from Escalated, the platform must re-evaluate that rule for that record and add or remove the grant. That is fine for a daily triage flag with a modest volume. It is a standing tax when the criteria field churns on millions of records as part of routine editing. Prefer criteria fields that are stable, or split the design so the frequently-changing access is handled differently.

A second recalculation driver is ownership and role churn. A design that leans on ownership-based rules and a deep hierarchy recalculates a lot whenever ownership transfers or people move roles, because each change ripples up the hierarchy and across related records. A design that keeps ownership-based grants to a minimum, or routes the requirement through team or group grants that do not re-derive on ownership change, recalcs far less often.

## When the sharing model stops scaling, and what to do

There is a point at which no amount of tuning saves you, because the requirement itself is rooted in per-record access, and per-record access does not scale to huge populations. The failure is not a mistake in Setup; it is a structural mismatch between what the business asks for and what a row-per-grant model can deliver. When you see it, your job is not to squeeze the design, it is to revisit the requirement or the mechanism.[^2]

The scenario that triggers the redesign is always the same shape: fine-grained, record-by-record access is required for a very large record and user population, and satisfying it multiplies share rows beyond any acceptable performance. Think of an org where every one of ten thousand users must see a different, individually curated slice of one million records, and no group, rule, or hierarchy can express those boundaries, so the only way to honor it is tens of millions of per-record, per-user grants. No OWD tweak, no batching, no index makes that fast; the model itself collapses under the row count, and every query that checks access has to wade through a table the size of a small mountain.[^2]

That is quiz question three. A sharing model "stops scaling" when fine-grained per-record access is required for a very large record and user population, multiplying share rows beyond acceptable performance. At that point you must revisit the requirement, change the mechanism, or partition the data. The wrong answers, setting OWD to Public, creating a single rule, or enabling FLS, are either the opposite of the problem or irrelevant. The bloat comes from per-record grants over a huge population, not from these.

What does a redesign actually look like? There are three honest moves, and you should name all three on the exam.

The first is to revisit the requirement. Ask whether the business really needs per-record distinction, or whether a coarser boundary can do the job while the sharp distinctions are handled elsewhere. Often the requirement, once you interrogate it, resolves into "a handful of groups see different populations," which is a sharing-rule problem, not a per-record problem. Do not keep a requirement that literally cannot scale and hope the platform finds a way; change the requirement to something the platform can honor.

The second is to change the mechanism. If the true boundary is group-based, express it as sharing rules to groups rather than manual or Apex shares per individual. If the boundary is ownership-based, lean on a role hierarchy or ownership-based rules and stop sharing per record. If the access is genuinely the same for everyone, a Public OWD or a broader OWD setting removes rows altogether by granting through the default instead of through the table. Every mechanism has a scaling profile; the redesign is matching the mechanism to the scale you are actually at.

The third is to partition the data. Where the requirement genuinely demands that different populations never see each other's records at volume, the cleanest answer is often to split the data by segment, business unit, region, or customer, into separate objects or separate orgs, each with its own OWD and sharing model, so no single Sharing table has to hold the whole strained population. Partitioning bounds the table growth to a size the platform can serve, and it is the answer the exam wants when it presents a genuinely enormous, genuinely fine-grained requirement that no single model can honor.

![A diagram showing the shape that triggers a sharing redesign, followed by the three honest moves of revisiting the requirement, changing the mechanism, and partitioning the data](</diagrams/day-32-i-1a-scalability-fundamentals-sharing-table-growth-reca/4-breaking-point-redesign.png>)

The mitigation list runs through every healthy design, and you should keep it whole in your head, because the quiz composes these into the "scalability implication" you have to name. Keep OWD restrictive so the table does not grow for free. Share to groups instead of individuals. Prefer criteria rules to many per-record grants. Batch and reconcile Apex and manual shares so rows get cleaned up instead of accumulating. And partition the data where a genuinely huge, genuinely fine-grained requirement demands it. Every one of those is a decision to keep the product of records times grantees small.[^2]

## Configuring a scalable sharing design, step by step

Walk a design review in order so each step hands the next its input.

**Step 1: Estimate the share-row volume before you build.** For each mechanism in the proposed design, compute records times the average grant count it adds. Hierarchy: records times average ancestor depth. Teams: records times average members. Criteria rules: matching records times one (group) row. Manual or Apex shares: count them directly. Add the numbers for every mechanism and you have your projected sharing-table growth. If the total is millions, the design needs to shrink before it reaches production, not after.[^2]

**Step 2: Prefer the mechanisms that add rows only where needed.** Start from restrictive OWD so nothing leaks and no rows are created by default. Then satisfy the access requirements with the fewest rows: restrictive OWD plus a few group-targeted criteria rules beats broad teams, deep hierarchies, or stacks of manual shares, because the rule's rows are tied to matching records rather than to member counts. This step is the design-intent rule applied: choose the mechanism that satisfies the requirement with the fewest share rows.[^2]

**Step 3: Minimize recalculation frequency.** Review every criteria-based sharing rule and write down what field changes when. If a rule's criteria field churns during routine work, the platform re-runs that rule constantly, which at scale is the recalc tax. Prefer criteria fields that change rarely, or restructure so frequently-changing access is handled by a mechanism that does not re-derive everything. Audit ownership churn too: a design that triggers a re-derivation on every ownership or role change will spend its budget on recalculation.[^3]

**Step 4: Batch and reconcile programmatic and manual shares, and monitor after go-live.** Create Apex managed shares in bulk batches rather than row by row, add a cleanup job that deletes stale shares, and review manual shares periodically, because they accumulate. After go-live, watch the Sharing table's growth the way you watch any other table: if it is climbing toward millions when the requirement looked small, the design is multiplying more than intended and needs review.[^2][^3]

**Step 5: Reassess the requirement for the genuinely huge cases.** If the requirement demands per-record access for a very large population, do not force it into the sharing model. Revisit the requirement to find the coarser boundary that the business actually needs, change the mechanism away from per-record grants, or partition the data into segments that bound the table growth. Name which one before you promise a timeline, because only that choice moves the model from "collapsing" to "servable."[^2]

## Worked example: decide the scalability implication of a real scenario

Take this scenario, which is the exact shape the exam likes. A global services firm has 4 million case records and a case team structure. Each case is owned by one rep, and the org has enabled "Grant Access Using Hierarchies" on cases, with a hierarchy six levels deep. Every case also carries an average of four named Case Team members who need access, added manually by reps as the work proceeds. The org ask is simply that the right people see each case, which it does, and nobody has complained about correctness. You are asked to determine the scalability implications.

Now run the arithmetic. The hierarchy alone: four million records, up to five ancestor rows each once you are within the six-level tree, so up to twenty million hierarchy share rows, for access that in most cases only one immediate manager needed. The teams alone: four million records times an average of four members, up to sixteen million team-grant rows. Together, before any sharing rule, manual share, or Apex grant, the design is on a path toward tens of millions of share rows. Every access check against the case object has to contend with a share table the size of a small country.

![A two-row arithmetic table showing how four million cases at up to five ancestor rows each reach twenty million hierarchy share rows, and four million cases at an average of four team members reach sixteen million team rows](</diagrams/day-32-i-1a-scalability-fundamentals-sharing-table-growth-reca/5-worked-example-arithmetic.png>)

The scalability implication you name: table bloat, driven by the deep hierarchy (a share row per ancestor per record) plus broad per-record team sharing (a row per member per record), each multiplying across all four million records. The recalc read follows immediately: every ownership transfer and every Case Team change re-derives rows across a large volume, so the org will feel the recalc cost every time reps hand work off between them, which is constant. The model will flush slowly, access-check queries will degrade, and the platform will spend its time maintaining rows that a leaner design would not have created.

The architected fix, in order. First, reconsider the hierarchy depth. If upward visibility only ever needs the immediate manager, a two-level structure produces far fewer ancestor rows than a six-level cascade, or move the "director sees everything below" requirement to a sharing rule targeting the directors' group instead of letting the hierarchy materialize an ancestor row at every level. Second, replace the manual per-record team members with defined Case Teams or a group-based grant, so the access is expressed per structure rather than per record per person. Third, review the criteria the teams really need, and where the boundary is a group boundary, express it as a criteria rule to a group so the row count follows the matching records, not the member population. That sequence keeps every requirement satisfied while taking the design from tens of millions of rows down to something the platform serves comfortably.

## Exam traps to avoid

**Trap: broad sharing that multiplies rows is the number one scalability mistake.** Deep hierarchies, wide teams, and stacks of manual or Apex shares each add a share row per granted ancestor or member, and they multiply across every record. When a scenario describes broad, individual, per-record sharing at volume, the correct answer is table bloat and degraded performance, not a field-level issue and not a licensing concern. Reach for the records-times-grantees arithmetic before you answer.[^2][^4]

**Trap: recalculation is not free.** Heavy criteria- or ownership-based recalcs on LDV cause slowness and locks, and editing a rule, transferring ownership, or moving a role at volume re-runs the engine over many rows. A design that triggers recalculation constantly is paying a standing tax, so prefer stable criteria fields and minimize ownership and role churn where you can.[^3]

**Trap: sharing rules to individuals or to very large teams scale poorly compared to groups plus rules.** A rule that grants access to a group stores the membership once and keeps the row count tied to matching records; a rule that grants to many individual users, or a team with hundreds of members spread across records, multiplies rows the way per-record grants do. When the exam contrasts these, the group-targeted rule wins on scalability.[^2]

## Try it yourself

In a scratch or sandbox org, prove the multiplication. Create three roles (rep, manager, director), enable "Grant Access Using Hierarchies" on a custom object, create a few dozen records owned by reps, and run a query against that object's underlying share records (AccountShare or the custom object's equivalent) to count the hierarchy-derived rows. Add a manager and a director above a rep and watch the row count tick up by one per ancestor per record. Then create a sharing rule that grants a single group access and watch how few rows it adds by comparison.

Next, feel the recalc cost. Create a criteria-based sharing rule on a field, change the field on a handful of records, and note that the platform re-evaluates the rule; then imagine the same single change across millions of records and you have internalized why recalc is the hidden tax. Finally, model the breaking point in miniature: create a small population, grant every record to every user through manual shares, and watch the grant count grow as records times users, then explain in your own words why that design would collapse if you scaled either number up by a thousand. Each test turns this lesson's arithmetic into something you have watched the platform do.

## The design frame, in one screen

Keep this whole objective in one frame. A share row is a record plus a grantee, so sharing-table size is records times the grants you materialize: deep hierarchies add a row per ancestor per record, broad teams add a row per member per record, and both multiply across your whole data volume, which is why restrictive OWD plus a few group-targeted rules keep the table lean while broad per-record sharing bloats it. Recalculation is the recompute the engine runs when you edit a rule, transfer ownership, or move a role, and at LDV it is slow and lock-heavy, so designs that minimize recalc frequency, by preferring stable criteria fields and bounded churn, scale where others stall. And when fine-grained per-record access is required across a very large population, the model itself stops scaling and you revisit the requirement, change the mechanism, or partition the data. Estimate the row volume before you build, and you predict the implication before it becomes an incident.[^2][^3]
