You wrote the policy. You scored the vendors. You built the inventory of what people actually use and you routed it into approve, restrict, or replace. That was real work, and it closes the loop for a day.

Then the day ends. A team quietly starts a new tool. A vendor ships a feature that changes what a sanctioned product touches. An employee who was reassigned keeps a license you are still paying for. Three people who should have stopped pasting client names into the writing assistant have not stopped. None of this makes a sound, and none of it shows up on the day you announced the policy. It shows up later, as a surprise. The tool that was fine in March is upstream of a problem in September, and you had no idea because nobody checked.

The scheduled check is what turns governance from a moment into a program. This lesson is about that check: how to audit your AI use on a regular cadence, what the audit actually looks at, and how to run one without turning it into a box-ticking exercise that catches nothing.

## The words you need

Three terms carry the lesson, and they are easy to blur.

**An audit** is a scheduled, evidence-based review that checks whether a defined set of controls and records reflect reality. It is not a meeting. It is not a dashboard glance. It is a structured pass where you pick a scope, pull specific evidence, compare the evidence to what you expected, and record what you found. The word audit comes from the financial world, where an independent reviewer matches the books to the records, but you do not need to be an accountant to use the discipline. The same shape works on AI: compare your inventory to what is actually installed, your policy to what people actually do, your licenses to who actually uses them.

**Continuous monitoring** is the always-on version, watching signals the whole time instead of checking them periodically. Real-time detection of which tools are in use, what data they touch, and whether a model drifted off its expected behavior. Monitoring and auditing are complements, not rivals. Monitoring tells you, continuously, whether anything crossed a line you set. Auditing pulls back and asks, on a schedule, whether the lines themselves are still right. An audit catches a problem a monitor was never watching.

**Cadence** is the schedule on which you repeat the audit: quarterly, twice a year, annually. The cadence is not decoration. It is what makes the audit trustworthy, because a check you never schedule is a check that drifts off your calendar and never happens.

## Why auditing has to be scheduled, not remembered

The reason an audit needs a cadence and cannot just happen "when we get around to it" is that AI does not sit still, and neither does your organization. NIST, the U.S. standards body, makes this a structural expectation of its AI Risk Management Framework: "risk management should be continuous, timely, and performed throughout the AI system lifecycle," and the framework's GOVERN function calls for "ongoing monitoring and periodic review of the risk management process and its outcomes," including deciding the frequency of that review.[^1] The framework treats periodic review as a named governance task with an assigned frequency, not a hope.

NIST's own frame is older than AI and apolitical, which is what makes it useful. It is a voluntary, risk-management-oriented set of guidance for anyone designing an AI system, and it has been clear from the start that the model and its risk profile change over the system's life, so a single point-in-time assessment is not enough.[^1]

The consulting and board guidance goes further and proposes actual numbers. Diligent, a governance-software firm, describes a board-level cadence that includes reviewing the AI inventory and material AI risks at least annually, running a full AI policy review annually, and refreshing the AI inventory on a quarterly basis for high-risk systems.[^2] A governance operating model guide from Atlan, a data-company, lays out the layered cadence behind that: strategic review by the board and executive team quarterly, operational review by a center of excellence monthly, and continuous monitoring at the technical layer.[^3] The pattern across all three is the same. High-risk things get checked more often. Governance sits on a schedule. Nobody's planner is a substitute for a calendar.



## The trap: the audit as theater

There is a version of this that fails, and you should recognize it so you do not run it. It is the audit as a performance: email the policy to everyone, hold a 30-minute review where people nod, declare the program is working, and do nothing else. The always-on governance crowd calls this the "check the box" audit, satisfying the stakeholder and waiting for the next scheduled review, and it is a real and documented failure mode.[^4] The reason it fails is that it measures intentions, not the world. You are not checking whether the policy held; you are checking whether anyone objected to the policy in the meeting.

What fixes the theater is evidence. An effective audit does not begin with opinions. It begins with a concrete scope and a list of the specific records you will pull to test it. If you cannot name the evidence you will look at, you do not yet have an audit, you have a discussion.

## What the audit checks

For a non-technical adoption owner, a practical AI audit on a regular cadence tests a short list of things that map directly to the work you did earlier in this course. Write these four as your default scope.

**1. Inventory matches reality.** Pull the list of AI tools your organization is actually using, the way you did in the shadow-IT lesson, and compare it to the inventory you govern. Every tool people use should be on the list, and every tool on the list should be something a human can locate. A tool that appears in neither your inventory nor your discovery signals is fine. A tool people use that is missing from your inventory is a gap.

**2. Policy is being followed in the data, not just on paper.** Pick the two or three data-safety and access rules that would hurt you most if broken, and check them against real usage. Are client names still going into the unrestricted writing tool? Is the coding assistant still attached to the source repository your policy said it could touch? An effective check here is a small sample, examined for evidence of the specific rule, not a survey of whether people feel compliant.

**3. Licenses and spend match users.** Cross check who holds a paid AI license or seat against who is actually using it, and who is using a tool without a license. The mismatch runs both ways: paying for seats nobody uses, and people using a tool they never got a license for. The first is waste you can close. The second is unmanaged exposure on a tool someone found a workaround to.

**4. The policy itself is still current.** Re-read the policy against the tools and vendors now in operation. A new tool that was not in the market when you wrote the policy has no rule. A vendor that changed what their product does, features shipped, or who it shares data with may have outgrown the section that approved it. An annual full policy review is the standard minimum, with a lighter refresh on the same cycle you audit the inventory.[^2]

## The cadence, chosen to match risk

Pick a cadence that matches how fast your organization changes and how much risk each system carries, then put it on the calendar. A reasonable default for a typical organization is: refresh the AI inventory and run the evidence checks quarterly, revisit the full policy annually, and review high-risk systems (anything handling client data, money, or regulated information) on the tighter quarterly cycle regardless of the rest.[^2][^3] If your organization is moving fast on AI, or you are mid-incident, tighten it. If you have three tools and all of them are boring, you can comfortably widen it, as long as the widest interval still has a date.

The operative sentence is "a date." Schedule the audits before they are needed, the way you schedule a fire drill, because a check that only happens when someone remembers is not a cadence, it is a mood.

## Worked example: the quarterly audit that caught three things

Let me run a concrete one. A 200-person services firm set up its AI governance six months ago: a policy, an approved-tool list, a data-safety rule keeping client data out of unapproved tools, and a quarterly audit the adoption owner committed to.

Quarter one was mostly theater, honestly. The owner forwarded the policy, invited fifteen people to a review call, and declared success when nobody objected. Nothing was actually pulled or compared. Quarter two, she ran the audit for real, and it took her staff assistant an afternoon.

She defined five evidence pulls. The identity-provider log of third-party apps authorized by staff. The procurement list of paid AI subscriptions. A list of who was granted access to each sanctioned tool. A random sample of twenty documents produced across four teams, scanned for whether client names appeared in a tool that policy said must not receive them. And the approve/restrict/replace inventory itself.

Three findings came back, none of which monitoring had flagged, because none of them were the kind of thing a real-time signal catches.

First, licenses: the firm was paying for forty seats on a writing assistant and twenty-four were actively using it. Sixteen seats, roughly $26 a month each, were dead weight, about $5,000 a year of spend with a real number on it. The firm cut the license to thirty seats and saved the difference, a clean, auditable win to report up.

Second, a drift in tool use: the finance team, three people, had quietly shifted their forecasting work from the sanctioned spreadsheet tool to a newer one the vendor had released, because the new version had better charting and it had been sitting in the same subscription all along. Nothing illegal, but the new tool was not on the inventory, had not been through the vendor scorecard, and touched real budget numbers. It was an approved product, but with a feature the policy had not reviewed. The audit caught it before anyone treated the output as governed, took it through the quick rubric, and added it to the inventory with the charting feature explicitly named.

Third, the policy was wrong about the present: it still listed the coding assistant as "approve for engineering only," but product, two people, had started using it for their own work based on a colleague's recommendation. The authorization never made it to the policy. A one-line policy update fixed the mismatch so the approved language described the actual usage, the exact state a stale document never captures.

The whole episode cost an afternoon and changed three things, each small, each the kind of thing that compounds if uncaught. The owner also changed the audit itself: she added a standing agenda, a working file of evidence pulls, and a short "what changed since last quarter" section to the top, so the next audit was faster and started from the last audit instead of from memory.

![The quarterly audit caught three things: 16 dead seats on a writing assistant cut from 40 to 30, a finance team drifting to an unreviewed forecasting tool pulled back onto the inventory, and a stale engineering-only policy corrected in one line](</diagrams/auditing-ai-use-on-a-regular-cadence/3-quarterly-audit-findings.png>)

The example is illustrative, but its shape is universal: none of the three findings were dramatic, and that is the point. A working audit mostly catches the unglamorous drift that, left alone, becomes the surprise. The value is not the drama of a big catch. It is the quiet removal of small gaps before they compound, on a schedule you can defend.

## Try it: run your first audit on a real scope

Run a first audit this week, small and real, using the four-item scope above. Do not review whether the program is good; that is the theater mode. Pull evidence.

Write one line answering each of these. Then, honestly, check the ones you assumed instead of verified.

- Inventory: can you name every AI tool in active use by your teams this month, and does each one appear in the record you govern?
- Policy: pick one data-safety rule that would hurt most if broken. What specific evidence would show it held, and have you looked at that evidence, or only assumed it?
- Spend: name the seats or licenses your organization pays for. Can you say, without checking, how many are in active weekly use? If not, check.
- Currency: re-read one paragraph of your policy. Does it describe a tool, a vendor, a feature, or a data flow that no longer exists, or omit one that now does?

Then answer the meta-question: how would the finding travel? If an auditor (or your board, your CEO, your client) asked "how do you know this is true," would you hand them evidence, or would you hand them your confidence? The difference between those two answers is the whole lesson. Confidence is what you feel. Evidence is what you can show, and an audit on a regular cadence is how you keep the two from drifting apart.

Finally, put a date on the next one before you close the file. A cadence is not a number in your head, it is the next check already on a calendar, with a scope someone will actually pull.
