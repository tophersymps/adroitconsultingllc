Lesson 9 made the case that honest reporting is what builds trust. That assumes people are listening. Many of them are not, and not because they are tuning you out. They are afraid the new tool will take their work, or skeptical that the promised change will ever arrive, and no amount of accurate reporting lands cleanly until you address that reaction directly.

This lesson is about that reaction. It separates the two responses you actually face, fear and skepticism, explains why they need different handling, and gives you a practical way to address the concern underneath before it hardens into resistance that stalls the whole program.

## Fear and skepticism are not the same thing

The word "resistance" gets used as if it were one uniform wall. It is not. Two distinct reactions hide behind it, and they respond to completely different leadership moves.

**Fear is emotional.** It is the worry that AI will take your job, reduce your value, or make you look replaceable. It lives in the gut, and it does not answer to logic. You cannot argue someone out of a fear of losing their livelihood with a chart of productivity gains, because the fear is not about the chart. It is about the threat the tool represents to their identity and security.

**Skepticism is rational.** It is the doubt that the tool actually works, that it is worth the disruption, or that the leadership pushing it has thought it through. A skeptic is asking a fair question: why should I believe this will help me? Skepticism does answer to evidence, but only to evidence that is specific and honest, which is exactly what Lesson 9 taught you to produce.

The research literature draws this same line. An integrative review in the Human Resource Management Review frames employee AI resistance as three dimensions: fear, inefficacy, and antipathy. Fear is distress about the perceived threats of the technology. Inefficacy is the feeling of being unable to keep up or master it. Antipathy is skepticism, distrust, or resentment toward AI, often rooted in concerns about privacy, ethics, and job security.[^1] Treating all three as one problem is how leaders fail: they answer a fear with data, or answer a skepticism with reassurance, and neither lands because each is aimed at the wrong target.

## This is the norm, not an edge case

Before you decide who in your org is "the resistant one," look at the baseline. The people who are worried are not a small vocal minority. They are close to the majority.

Pew Research Center surveyed 5,273 employed US adults in late 2024 and found that 52 percent are worried about the future impact of AI use in the workplace, 32 percent think it will lead to fewer job opportunities for them in the long run, and 33 percent feel overwhelmed by the prospect. Only 6 percent think it will create more opportunities for them.[^2] The Microsoft and LinkedIn Work Trend Index found a related pattern: among people who actually use AI at work, 53 percent worry that using it on important tasks makes them look replaceable.[^3]

The stakes are not abstract. SHRM's research estimates 19.2 million US jobs are at high or very high risk of displacement from automation, and a Kyndryl survey found 45 percent of CEOs say their employees are reluctant or hostile toward AI.[^3] The Cloud Security Alliance, drawing on a 2024 EY survey, reports that up to 70 percent of change initiatives, including AI adoption, fail due to employee pushback or inadequate management support, and that 75 percent of employees worry AI could eliminate jobs.[^4]

Here is the pattern worth sitting with. A large share of your workforce is worried, a smaller but real share is hostile, and the failure rate of AI change initiatives is driven substantially by that human reaction, not by the technology. Yet most leaders spend their energy on the tooling and the rollout plan, and almost none on the fear and skepticism that decide whether the plan survives contact with the team.

## Why arguing does not work

The natural instinct, when someone says "this is going to take my job," is to argue: no it will not, it will make you more productive, look at all the new roles it creates. That instinct is wrong, and it is wrong in a specific way.

Arguing against a fear confirms to the person that you do not understand the fear. They are not asking you to prove the tool is safe. They are asking you to acknowledge that their concern is legitimate enough to be taken seriously. When you skip straight to reassurance, you signal that their worry is naive, and that signal lands harder than the reassurance. The Cloud Security Alliance report makes the point directly: traditional top-down rollouts fail with AI because the human stakes, career anxiety, skill erosion, and trust, demand a different leadership approach than command-and-control deployment.[^4]

There is also a quieter failure mode. When you argue against a fear and the person stays quiet, you assume you won. You did not. SHRM describes this as the resistance you do not see: employees who worry that using AI will be seen as a sign of laziness will disengage, withhold insights, or offer surface-level input rather than fully contributing to a system they view as a threat.[^3] Silence is not agreement. It is often the fear going underground, where you cannot address it and it keeps eroding the program from inside.

The leader's job is not to win an argument. It is to address the concern underneath, so the person can move from defending against the tool to deciding whether it helps them.

## A practical way to address the concern

Here is a framework that works across both reactions. It does not require you to be a therapist or a change-management consultant. It requires you to do six things in order.

**Name the concern out loud.** State what you believe people are worried about, specifically, before they have to say it. "Some of you are worried this will take over the work you have built your career on" is a sentence that costs you nothing and tells the team you are not going to pretend the worry does not exist. Naming the concern is the difference between talking at people and talking with them.

**Show what changes and what does not.** Fear thrives on ambiguity. The scariest version of AI is the one nobody has explained. So be concrete about the boundary: which tasks the tool will draft or assist with, which tasks still require a human decision, and which parts of each role are untouched. The review in the Human Resource Management Review stresses that resistance is alleviated when employees see AI as augmenting rather than replacing human roles, and that experiential learning, actually working with the tool in a supported way, is what debunks myths and reduces fear.[^1] You cannot promise nothing changes. You can promise that the change has a defined edge, and that is what people need to hear.

**Pair the tool with the person, not against them.** Frame the tool as doing the work with the employee, not instead of them. The research language for this is human-AI augmentation: AI handles the draft, the extraction, the first pass, and the human owns the judgment, the review, and the decision.[^1] When a leader models this, by using the tool openly and narrating what it does and where it falls short, the team sees a usable relationship with the technology instead of a threat.

**Give people control over the pace.** Fear spikes when change is imposed. It drops when people have a say. Let teams opt into pilots, choose their own use cases, and report what works and what does not without blame. Lesson 9 built the channel for honest setbacks; this is where it pays off. A person who can shape how the tool enters their work is a person who stops treating it as an enemy.

**Let peers model it, not just you.** The ZS analysis of AI change management makes a point that maps directly here: an AI workflow that stalls despite repeated leadership communication often takes hold the moment a respected peer starts using it openly and narrating what works.[^5] Your authority legitimizes the program. A peer's example makes it feel safe to try. Identify the people the team already trusts, get them using the tool on real work, and let their example do the persuading your memos cannot.

**Build AI literacy, not just access.** Much of the fear and most of the skepticism comes from not understanding what the tool can and cannot do. Give people a way to learn that is low-stakes and hands-on, so their judgment about the tool is based on experience rather than rumor. The Human Resource Management Review is explicit that supporting AI literacy and training is how organizations address the anxiety, inefficacy, and antipathy that drive resistance.[^1] This is also the bridge to the next lesson, because training that scales is how literacy stops being a one-time event and becomes the norm.

## Worked example: Mara faces the finance team

Mara owns AI adoption at a mid-sized manufacturer with about 400 employees. The rollout has reached the finance team, and it has stalled. Two senior accountants, Priya and Tom, have been openly dismissive in meetings, and the rest of the team has gone quiet around them.

Mara's first instinct is to present the productivity numbers again. She catches herself, because she has seen that fail twice already. Instead she schedules a working session with the team and starts by naming the concern out loud: "I want to be straight with you. I think some of you are worried this tool is going to take over the close, and I think some of you do not believe it will actually help. Both of those are fair things to feel, and I want to talk about them directly."

Priya, the more vocal skeptic, asks the question she has been holding: "Fine. Show me one thing this does that I cannot do faster and better myself." Mara does not argue. She pulls up a real reconciliation from last month, runs the AI draft on it live, and shows the team the output, including a clear error the tool made on a split account. "Here is what it is good at, and here is exactly where it gets it wrong, which is why it does not run without you. Your job is the part it cannot do: the judgment on the splits and the sign-off."

That one session does more than the three memos before it. By showing the tool's actual limits, Mara gives the skepticism a specific, honest answer instead of a slogan. By naming the fear, she lets the quieter members of the team hear that their worry is legitimate. And by pairing the tool with the human role, she reframes the tool from a replacement into a draft that still needs the accountant's judgment.

Then she turns the pace over to the team. She asks Priya and Tom to pick one low-risk reconciliation each, run it with the tool for a month, and bring back what they find, good and bad. She commits to reporting their results honestly in the monthly review either way. Priya still does not love the tool. But she stops blocking it, because she was given control over how it enters her work and an honest answer to her one real question. The quiet resistance on the rest of the team lifts, because the person they trust, Priya, is now engaged in testing the tool rather than dismissing it.

## Try it: prepare for the two conversations

Do this before the next rollout meeting, not during it.

First, write the fear statement you will open with. What is the specific worry you believe your team holds about AI, named out loud in one or two sentences? If you cannot write it, you have not identified it, and you will default to arguing against a fear you never named.

Second, write the boundary statement. Which specific tasks will the tool handle, which tasks still require a human decision, and which parts of each role do not change? Be concrete enough that someone could act on it. A vague "AI will make us more efficient" is not a boundary.

Third, identify your peer model. Which one or two people does the team actually trust, and what real work can they use the tool on in the next two weeks? Pick people whose example would carry weight, not just your most enthusiastic early adopter.

Fourth, decide what control you will hand over. What choice will the team get about how the tool enters their work, and what channel will they use to report what is working and what is not, without blame? If the answer is "none," assume the fear stays underground and plan accordingly.

## What's next

This lesson gave you the way to address the fear and skepticism that meet every rollout at the door: name the concern, draw the boundary, pair the tool with the person, hand over control, let peers model it, and build literacy. But addressing the reaction in the moment is only the start. A team that stops resisting still needs to actually learn how to use the tool well, and the one-time onboarding burst that got you here will not carry the whole organization. Lesson 11 is about training that scales: beyond the initial roll-out, which is how you turn a handful of willing users into a workforce that is genuinely skilled, past the first wave and into the ongoing program.
