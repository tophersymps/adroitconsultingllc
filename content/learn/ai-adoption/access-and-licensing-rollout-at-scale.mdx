Last lesson you published a standard stack and set up the front door that keeps it short. This lesson is the hallway behind that door: how the right people get access to a standard tool, in a defined order, with a permission level you chose, and how the money attached to those permissions stays tied to people who use them.

Most programs get this wrong in one of two directions. In the first, someone signs an annual agreement, assigns every seat on the first Monday, and declares the rollout complete. Coverage looks excellent. Learning stops immediately, because there is no next cohort to learn from and no unassigned seat to withhold. In the second, access is granted one request at a time by whoever is closest to the tool, which produces accounts nobody can enumerate and leavers who keep their logins for months.

The measured outcome of the first failure mode is well documented. Gartner's 2025 Microsoft 365 and Copilot survey of 187 IT and customer service leaders found 40 percent of organizations piloting Microsoft 365 Copilot and a further 19 percent planning to pilot, while only 5 percent of the organizations that had finished a pilot were moving to a larger deployment that year. Of the 127 respondents who had piloted or rolled out the product, nearly half rated it "some value, shows promise."[^1] Interest converted into pilots. Pilots rarely converted into deployment.

This lesson is about the machinery that makes conversion possible, and about the seat economics that decide whether conversion is affordable.

## Eight terms to settle first

**Entitlement.** The right to use a product, independent of the account that exercises it. An entitlement exists because the company bought a seat, a capacity pool, or a subscription. Access is the mechanism that turns an entitlement into a login.

**Provisioning.** Creating the account, assigning the role, and attaching the license when a person becomes eligible. Deprovisioning is the reverse, and it is the half that fails more often.

**Single sign-on (SSO).** Authentication routed through the company identity provider, so the platform never holds its own password and the company can enforce its own sign-in policy.

**SCIM.** System for Cross-domain Identity Management, the standard that lets the identity provider create, update, and remove accounts in the platform automatically as the directory changes. SSO answers who is signing in. SCIM keeps the account list correct when nobody is signing in.

**Seat assignment.** Attaching a paid license to a named user. It can be direct (one user, one license) or group-based (the user inherits the license from a group membership).

**Dormancy window.** The period of inactivity after which a seat becomes a candidate for reclamation. Thirty days is a fair review trigger for a daily-use tool. Ninety days is the point at which "they will get to it" stops being a credible explanation.

**Reclamation.** Removing a license from a person who is not using it, returning the seat to the pool, telling the person and their manager, and giving them a route back in. Reclamation without a route back in is a punishment, and people route around punishments.

**True-up.** The contractual moment when your license count is reconciled against reality, either at renewal or at a mid-term checkpoint written into the agreement. Adding seats mid-term is usually easy and cheap. Removing them mid-term is usually neither, which is the single most important fact in this lesson.

## Two license shapes, and why the shape decides your options

![A comparison of three AI license shapes across what the license meters, what the vendor optimizes for, whether the count can shrink mid-term, and the failure each one produces: per-seat subscription, consumption or credit metering, and a hybrid platform fee with consumption on top](</diagrams/access-and-licensing-rollout-at-scale/2-license-shapes.png>)

**Per-seat subscription.** A fixed price per named user per month, often with an annual commitment and a minimum quantity. Microsoft's own documentation is a clean example of how much structure sits behind a simple seat price: Microsoft Copilot is available with Microsoft 365 E7 or as an add-on to eligible plans, and the small-business variant, Copilot Business, is an add-on for organizations up to 300 users with an annual commitment and a maximum of 300 licensed users per tenant. The same page warns that licensing availability and product terms change, so eligibility gets confirmed before purchase rather than assumed from last year's contract.[^2]

The per-seat shape is predictable, which is why finance likes it, and it makes coverage a decision rather than an accident, which is why operators should like it too. Its weakness is that you pay for the seat whether or not the person opens the tool, and the count resists shrinking until the contract lets it.

**Consumption or credit metering.** You buy a pool of units (credits, messages, tokens, agent runs) and pay as the organization draws them down. This shape tracks cost to activity, so a dormant user costs nothing and a power user's cost is visible. It also moves the risk onto you: an automated workflow or a single enthusiastic team can drain a pool, and forecasting becomes a modeling exercise rather than an arithmetic one.

**Hybrid.** A platform fee per seat for access, plus consumption on top for the expensive actions (long-context reasoning, agents, image generation). Most enterprise AI contracts are drifting toward this shape, and it deserves a warning: it combines the commitment of a seat with the volatility of a meter, so you need both a seat reclamation loop and a consumption monitor, and they are different instruments.

The practical rule: match the shape to how the work behaves. A tool people use daily with predictable intensity is a seat purchase. A tool used intensely by a few specialists, or one that agents call programmatically, is a consumption purchase with a hard cap. Buying a seat for every employee "so nobody is blocked" is a decision to fund 100 percent coverage for 40 percent usage, and it is the most common expensive mistake in this part of the program.

## Buy below the eligible population

Here is the counterintuitive part of a large rollout: you should almost never buy seats for everyone who is eligible at the start.

Eligibility is a permission. Adoption is a behavior. Buying to eligibility converts a behavior question into a sunk cost, and it removes the one lever that keeps a rollout honest: the waitlist. A waitlist is not bureaucracy, it is a measurement. People who ask for a seat have declared intent, which makes them far more likely to use it than people who received one in a bulk assignment, and it tells you which teams are actually pulling rather than which teams were named in a spreadsheet.

The economics reinforce it. Roughly half of provisioned SaaS licenses sit unused in a given 30-day period by Zylo's measurement, which the company puts at about 21 million dollars of annual waste for the average organization.[^3] Flexera's 2025 State of the Cloud survey found 84 percent of organizations naming cloud spend management as their top cloud challenge, with budgets already being exceeded by 17 percent, and 64 percent of respondents saying they now manage the cost of cloud licenses.[^4] AI seats are the newest entry in a category with a well-established waste rate, and the contracts that carry them are annual.

So the purchase decision is a simple asymmetry. Adding seats later is a phone call. Removing them is a negotiation. Buy to your first two waves, hold a float, and let the waitlist price the rest.

## The provisioning chain, and its four edges

![The access chain from HR record to platform seat, showing the identity provider as the source of truth, entitlement groups mapped to job categories, SCIM syncing membership and removal, and the platform resolving group membership into role plus license, with four edge cases labeled beneath: nested group members, direct assignments, break-glass accounts, and deprovisioning that does not revoke live sessions](</diagrams/access-and-licensing-rollout-at-scale/3-provisioning-chain.png>)

The clean version of the chain is short. The HR system marks someone as hired or moved. The identity provider puts them in a group that represents a job category or an entitlement. SCIM syncs that group to the platform. The platform resolves membership into a role and a license. When the person leaves the group, the license goes with them.

Group-based licensing is what makes this work at scale, and the mechanics are worth knowing precisely because they explain the failures. When licenses are assigned to a group, all members inherit them, new members receive them on joining, and members who leave the group lose them. You cannot remove an inherited license from an individual's properties, because the group owns that decision. Changes typically take effect within minutes of a membership change. And licenses attach to unique members, so the group needs at least as many licenses as it contains people.[^5]

Now the four edges, each of which produces a real incident in a real tenant.

**Nested groups.** Microsoft's documentation is explicit that when a license is applied to a nested group, only the immediate first-level user members of that group receive it.[^5] Someone builds a tidy hierarchy of teams inside a department group, the hierarchy looks right in the admin console, and a slice of the department silently never gets access. The fix is boring: keep entitlement groups flat, and let the directory do the nesting somewhere else.

**Direct assignments.** A direct grant to one person sits on top of group membership, and it survives the group change you thought would remove it. Every direct assignment is a future orphan, so it needs a named approver and an expiry date in the same record. If an exception has no end date, it is not an exception, it is a permanent second system.

**Break-glass and service accounts.** Every tenant needs at least one account that can still get in when SSO is broken. That account should be named, monitored, excluded from the provisioning group, and reviewed on a schedule. The same discipline applies to service accounts used by integrations: they are accounts, they count toward your license inventory, and nobody's offboarding process touches them.

**Deprovisioning that does not revoke.** This is the security edge, and it is the one people underestimate. Marking an account inactive in the platform is not the same as ending the person's sessions. Microsoft's own access-review tooling exists for exactly this gap: access reviews let an organization regularly review group memberships, application access, and role assignments so that only the right people keep access, and the documentation names the situations where automation cannot decide, such as a group being reused for a new purpose or a person who needs access after leaving a group.[^6] Test deprovisioning against every surface you actually ship: web, desktop, mobile, API keys, and any connected app. A leaver whose phone still has a valid refresh token is an open door with a closed file.

## The seat funnel

![The seat funnel in the worked example, moving from 1000 purchased seats to 1000 assigned, 940 technically enabled, 720 active in 30 days, 410 active weekly, and 330 habitual, with the gap between each layer labeled by cause and by the action it triggers](</diagrams/access-and-licensing-rollout-at-scale/1-seat-funnel.png>)

One number cannot describe a rollout, and the attempt to force it into one is why so many AI programs report adoption to leadership and then cannot explain why the renewal feels expensive. Count the layers separately, because each gap has a different cause and a different owner.

**Purchased.** Contracted seats from the agreement. This is the only number finance already trusts.

**Assigned.** Seats attached to a named person. A gap here means an entitlement nobody claimed, which is a finance and provisioning problem.

**Technically enabled.** Assigned and actually able to use the product: the prerequisite license is present, the client or browser meets requirements, and any admin toggle that matters is on. A gap here is an IT problem, and it is invisible in any report that starts at "assigned."

**Active.** A qualifying action inside a stated window. The window is a decision, not a fact, and whoever sets it decides your adoption number.

**Habitual.** Use across most weeks in the period, at any volume. This is the layer that predicts whether next quarter's renewal is defensible.

Dormancy is the inverse of the middle of that funnel, and reclamation is the loop that acts on it: flag anyone with no qualifying activity for 30 days, notify them and their manager with a two-question prompt (still need it, or release it), reclaim at 90 days if there is no answer, and put the person on the waitlist for reinstatement. Two questions and an automatic path back in is the difference between a policy people comply with and a policy people hide from.

## Rollout in waves with gates

![Four rollout waves with entry criteria and exit criteria for each, and the three decisions available at every gate: expand to the next wave, hold the current wave for another cycle, or reclaim dormant seats before expanding](</diagrams/access-and-licensing-rollout-at-scale/4-wave-gates.png>)

A wave is not a batch of names. It is a cohort with an entry criterion, a day-one task, and an exit criterion.

**Entry criterion** answers who qualifies for this wave: a named process the tool improves, a manager who will run the first session, and the technical prerequisites confirmed for every person in the cohort. Microsoft's deployment guidance follows the same three-phase shape, pilot then deploy then operate, and recommends a test environment plus pilot testing before licenses go wide.[^7] Its admin guidance also keeps the mechanics modest: licenses can be assigned to individuals or to groups, reassigned between users, and verified per user with a diagnostic that checks whether an account meets the requirements for the product's features.[^7]

**Day-one task** is the thing I would make non-negotiable if I could only enforce one rule. Every person in a wave gets a license and a named task, in the same message: rewrite this proposal's summary, prepare for this customer call, turn this meeting into decisions and owners. A license without a task is a trial, and trials decay.

**Exit criterion** is what must be true before the next wave, stated in the funnel's terms: at least two thirds of the cohort active in the last 30 days, at least one named workflow per person in use, and support ticket volume back to baseline. If the wave misses, you hold the wave. Holding is not failure. Expanding past a wave that has not landed is how a program acquires a thousand idle seats and a reputation for not working.

Microsoft's measurement tooling maps onto this directly: the Copilot Dashboard in Viva Insights and the Microsoft 365 usage reports in the admin center are the two places the vendor expects you to look, and both report activity over a defined window.[^7] Whatever platform you run, insist on a report that separates assigned from active, and export it monthly rather than reading it live.

## Worked example: Nadia's thousand seats

Nadia runs the AI program at a 1,650-person insurance company. Of those, 1,400 are knowledge workers who could plausibly use a general assistant. Her finance director has approved 1,000 seats at 31 dollars per seat per month on an annual commitment, which is 31,000 dollars a month and 372,000 dollars a year. The remaining 400 people are eligible and unlicensed. This is the good version of that decision, and the reason is what she does next.

She builds four waves with gates. Wave one is 60 people: the champions network, one cohort per business unit, each with a named process. Wave two is 240 people, drawn from the waitlist by business unit pull rather than by headcount. Wave three is 400. Wave four is the remaining 300 seats, held back as a float for new hires, team-level expansion, and the waitlist that keeps growing.

Provisioning runs through the identity provider from day one. Entitlement groups are flat, one per job category, mapped to a license. Direct assignments exist only as documented exceptions with an end date, and there are four of them in the first quarter rather than four hundred. Break-glass access sits outside the groups, in a vault, with a monthly review on the calendar.

At day 120 the funnel looks like this, and these are the numbers she takes to the steering meeting:

- Purchased: 1,000
- Assigned: 1,000
- Technically enabled: 940, because 60 people are on a base plan that is not eligible and two are on an update channel that has not moved yet
- Active in the last 30 days: 720
- Active weekly: 410
- Habitual, meaning three or more of the last four weeks: 330
- Dormant for 90 days or more: 180

Three of those gaps have three different owners, which is the argument for counting them separately. The 60 enabled gap is an IT queue with a two-week fix. The 220 people assigned but inactive in 30 days are a manager conversation, and most of them never received a day-one task because their wave lead skipped the kickoff. The 180 dormant seats are a reclamation decision, and Nadia makes it in writing: notify, wait 30 days, reclaim, reinstate on request.

The 180 reclaimed seats do not reduce this year's bill, because the commitment is annual and the count does not shrink mid-term. They reduce next year's. She reassigns 120 of the reclaimed seats to the waitlist immediately, which is a real gain, since those are people who asked for access and now have it without a new purchase. Sixty stay in the float. At renewal she signs for 820 seats instead of 1,000, which at the same rate is 66,960 dollars a year she does not spend, and the 820 is defensible because she can show the activity behind every one of them.

The part that surprises her steering group is the support math. Tickets per 100 users peak in the first three weeks of a wave and fall by roughly two thirds once the cohort has run its day-one task. Four waves of that shape cost less in total service desk hours than one 1,000-person launch, because a 1,000-person launch arrives as a single spike with no learning between cohorts. The wave structure is not a courtesy to users. It is the cheaper operating model.

## Failure modes to design against

Bulk assignment at signing, which buys coverage and loses the waitlist, the cohorts, and the learning. Never do this to hit a launch date.

Buying to 100 percent eligibility, which converts a behavior question into a sunk cost and makes the renewal conversation about last year's optimism.

Direct assignments as the default, which creates a second, undocumented licensing system that survives offboarding and group changes.

A dormancy policy with no route back in, which teaches people to keep a seat they do not use rather than admit they do not use it.

Reclamation owned by nobody. If the loop has no owner and no date, it runs once, produces a satisfying report, and never runs again.

Counting a single adoption number. A program reporting "72 percent adoption" should be asked which layer that is, over what window, and what the habitual count is.

## Try it: build the access and licensing control sheet in a week

Block an hour a day for five days.

Day one, write the four numbers down for today: purchased, assigned, technically enabled, active in the last 30 days. If the platform will not give you the enabled count separately, find out why, because that gap is where the quiet failures live.

Day two, map the provisioning chain on one page: HR system, identity provider, groups, platform roles, license. Mark every place a human has to touch it. Each of those marks is either a controlled exception with an owner and a date, or an incident waiting to happen.

Day three, define the dormancy window and the reclamation loop in four sentences: trigger, notification, deadline, reinstatement. Name the owner of each step. If a step has no owner, the loop will not run.

Day four, write the wave plan for the next two quarters. For each wave: cohort, entry criterion, the day-one task, and the exit criterion. If you cannot name a task for a cohort, the cohort is not ready.

Day five, take one contract and read the termination and true-up clauses. Write down the date you can shrink, the date you can grow, and the notice period in each direction. That date belongs in the renewal calendar you built last lesson, because it is the only day the reclamation work turns into money.

## What's next

You now have the machinery: an entitlement model that follows the directory, a provisioning chain with its edges named, a funnel that separates assigned from active from habitual, waves with gates that can hold, and a reclamation loop with a date that turns into savings at renewal. What you do not yet have is a way to tell whether any of it is working over a period long enough to matter. Weekly numbers move for reasons that have nothing to do with your program, and quarterly numbers hide the sequence that produced them. Lesson 33 takes on measuring adoption over quarters rather than weeks, including how to read a plateau without panicking, how to separate seasonal noise from a real decline, and which two or three numbers deserve a place on the steering agenda.
