In Lesson 20 you discovered the unsanctioned AI your people are already using, and the discovery surfaced a consent problem: tools and data your organization did not approve were meeting in the open. This lesson covers the force that makes that meeting matter. Most AI use runs on personal data, and personal data is regulated. The moment an employee types a name, an email, a salary figure, or a health detail into a tool, or the moment a screening model scores a candidate, privacy law is involved, whether anyone planned for it.

This is a teaching lesson, not legal advice. It will not tell you whether your organization is compliant, because that answer depends on your specifics and on counsel who knows your situation. What it can do is give a non-technical owner the vocabulary, the map of the two privacy frameworks you are most likely to bump into, and a checklist for figuring out which rules apply to which AI use case. An owner who can ask the right questions beats one who is silently hoping IT or legal already handled it.

## What "personal data" means, because everything else hangs on it

Both the GDPR and the CCPA are built around a single idea: some data is personal, and personal data is handled under stricter rules. You do not need a law degree to work with this. You need to know what counts and why AI turns more data into personal data than you expect.

Under the GDPR, personal data is any information relating to an identified or identifiable person. That is deliberately broad. A name is obvious. But so is an email address, an IP address, a customer ID, a voice recording, a photograph, or a set of details that together single someone out, even if no single field names them. The GDPR is explicit that a person can be identifiable from an identifier like a name, an ID number, location data, or factors specific to their physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity.[^1] The test is not whether you already know who the person is. It is whether you could work it out.

Here is where AI changes your thinking. A fact that is not personal on its own can become personal once a model infers something about a person from it. Payroll data about your team is clearly personal. But AI also produces personal data it did not receive: a model that scores a customer's credit risk, predicts a candidate's likely tenure, or groups people into segments is generating inferences about identified people. Privacy regulators treat those inferences as personal data too, because they relate to an identifiable person and can be acted on in ways that affect them. The European Parliament's analysis of the GDPR and AI makes exactly this point: models applied to personal data can make inferences about particular individuals, and the GDPR allows such inferences only with appropriate safeguards in place.[^2]

The practical consequence for an owner: the question "does the tool touch personal data?" is almost never answered with a confident no. If the answer seems too easy, you may not have counted the data the tool itself generates about people.

## GDPR: what it applies to and the ideas you have to hold

The GDPR is the European Union's data protection regulation, in force since May 2018. It applies not only to organizations inside the EU but to any organization anywhere that processes the personal data of people in the EU when it is offering goods or services to them or monitoring their behavior. That reach is why a US-based firm with EU clients, employees, or website visitors can find itself inside its scope.

Three ideas carry most of what an owner needs to understand about the GDPR and AI.

The first is a lawful basis. Under the GDPR you cannot process personal data just because you think it is useful. Every instance of processing needs a lawful basis, a reason the law recognizes, such as consent, the performance of a contract, a legal obligation, or a legitimate interest. There is no automatic right to use data for any new purpose you discover later, which matters deeply for AI because one of AI's main habits is taking data collected for one reason and reusing it to train models or to infer things for another. Regulators frame this as purpose limitation: you may reuse data when the new use is compatible with the purpose you originally collected it for, but you have to be able to show that compatibility, and more demanding purposes edge toward needing their own basis.[^2][^3]

The second idea is the special treatment of decisions made on your behalf. Article 22 of the GDPR gives people the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, including profiling, where that decision produces legal effects on them or similarly significantly affects them.[^4] Read that plainly: a tool that decides something important about a person, with no meaningful human in the loop, is restricted. The regulator's own guidance describes what counts. It can be a legal effect, like an automatic refusal of an online credit application. Or it can be a similarly significant effect, like an e-recruiting process that screens out candidates with no human review.[^4] A credit decision, a hiring shortlist that a human simply stamps, a benefits eligibility call, these are the territory of Article 22. The law does not ban such decisions outright. It allows them in specific circumstances, and it requires safeguards: the person affected must have a route to human intervention, a way to express their view, and a way to contest the decision.[^4] For your AI program, the working rule is: when a model produces a decision a person will feel, route it through a human who can actually overturn it, and be ready to explain how the decision was made.

The third idea is assessment before the risky processing starts. Article 35 of the GDPR requires a data protection impact assessment, a DPIA, before processing that is likely to result in a high risk to people's rights and freedoms. The regulation names the kind of processing that triggers it, and it names AI directly: a systematic and extensive evaluation of personal aspects based on automated processing, including profiling, on which decisions that produce legal or similarly significant effects are based.[^3] In practice, a DPIA is a written walk-through of what the processing does, why, what the risks to people are, and what you will do to reduce them. A non-technical owner does not author the whole thing alone, but you are the one who should ensure it is scheduled before a high-risk AI rollout, not after a complaint.

## CCPA and the new California rulebook for automated decisions

Where the GDPR is a national law with a broad reach, the CCPA is a California state law. It applies to businesses that collect or sell the personal information of California residents and that meet certain thresholds, commonly summarized as doing business in California and meeting either a revenue test or a data-volume and revenue test.[^5] The practical reading for many organizations: if you have customers or employees in California and your operation is large enough, you should assume you are in scope, and confirm with counsel.

The CCPA's core is a set of consumer rights over personal information: the right to know what a business collects, the right to delete it, the right to correct it, and the right to opt out of certain uses like selling or sharing information for cross-context behavioral advertising. Your AI use inherits these. If a model is trained on, or operates on, the personal information of California residents, those residents can ask you what you have, ask you to correct it, and ask you to delete it, and you need a functioning process to respond.

Then California went further, and this is the part an AI owner needs on the calendar. In late 2025 the state finalized regulation under the CCPA aimed squarely at automated decision-making technology, ADMT. ADMT is defined as technology that processes personal information and uses computation to replace or substantially replace human decision-making. It does not touch every AI tool: the obligations are triggered when the technology is used to make a "significant decision" about a consumer, which the regulation ties to decisions affecting finances, housing, education, employment, or health care.[^5] For a business using ADMT that way, the new rules introduce a risk assessment, a pre-use notice to consumers explaining how the technology works and what it relies on, an opt-out, a right to access information about the logic of the ADMT and how its outputs are used, and a right to appeal the result.[^5] Those obligations phase in starting in 2026 and 2027, so they are not a tomorrow problem; they are a now problem if you run any automated hiring, lending, fraud, or customer-scoring use case.

Compare the two and the pattern is the same even where the words differ. Both laws put the burden on the organization that decides to run AI on people's information, both care about the moment a machine influences an important decision about a person, and both expect you to be able to explain and correct or restrict the machine's output. The details differ, and you should not conflate them. But the mental model an owner needs is identical: personal data, significant decisions, defined rights, and a documented, challengeable process. If you build your AI program to that standard, you will be moving in a defensible direction under both.

## What AI owners actually get wrong about privacy

The headline rules are now on the table. What separates a defensible program from a reactive one is not knowing the law by heart; it is refusing three comfortable myths.

The first myth is that "the tool anonymizes everything, so we are fine." Anonymization is brittle. A model trained on data can memorize and re-emit individuals, and a dataset that has been stripped of obvious names can still single people out when combined with other information. The GDPR's own analysis warns that re-identification is a real risk and must be prevented unless the conditions for lawful collection are met.[^2] Treat "it's anonymized" as a claim you verify on a case-by-case basis, not as a reason to stop thinking.

The second myth is that the vendor bears the compliance load. It does not. When your organization decides to feed personal data into a tool, or to run a model that decides something about a person, privacy law looks at you. The GDPR assigns responsibilities between controller and processor, but the controller, the party that decides why and how the data is processed, remains accountable even when a processor handles the mechanics.[^3] California's analysis of who must respond to a consumer's rights request points the same way: the business deciding to use the technology holds the obligation. Your vendor contract manages blame between you and the vendor; it does not remove you from the frame.

The third myth is that privacy compliance only matters once you are big. The GDPR does not have a small-business exemption, and its reach follows the people whose data you hold, not your headcount. An organization of forty people handling EU client data can still face the full menu. The size of your risk scales with the sensitivity and volume of the personal data you process, not with your revenue.

![Three myths that separate a reactive program from a defensible one: the tool anonymizes everything, the vendor bears the compliance load, and privacy only matters once you are big, each with the reality that the organization turning personal data into value owns the consequences](</diagrams/privacy-compliance-gdpr-ccpa-and-your-ai-use/4-three-myths.png>)

The through-line across all three myths is accountability. Privacy law is designed so that the organization that turns personal data into value also owns the consequences. Your job as owner is to make sure someone in your organization can answer each of these questions before there is an incident to answer it.

## Worked example: hiring tool or scorecard

Run a realistic case through the map so you can see the obligations land. This is an illustration, not a real company, and it is not legal advice; use it to see which questions matter, then get your own answers.

Suppose a mid-size company, about 300 staff, starts using an AI tool to screen job applicants. The tool scores how well each applicant fits a role and surfaces a shortlist for recruiters to review. It handles names, contact details, work history, and the content of applications, all clearly personal data about identifiable people.

The GDPR lens: the company processes personal data of applicants, probably including applicants in the EU. Each processing activity needs a lawful basis. The screening generates inferences about each candidate, which counts as personal data even though the tool produced it. Because the tool evaluates people and feeds a decision that affects their employment, this is squarely the territory of Article 22's automated decision-making rules, and the company needs a genuine human review that can overturn the tool, plus the ability to explain and contest decisions. Because it is high-risk processing involving evaluation of people, a DPIA should be done before the tool is rolled out, not after a rejection complaint.

The CCPA lens, if the company is in scope and handles California applicants: those applicants have rights to know what the company has on them, correct it, and delete it. And because the tool makes a significant decision about employment, it looks like automated decision-making technology under the new California rules, which will eventually carry a pre-use notice, an opt-out, a right to access information about the tool's logic, and a right to appeal.[^5]

The same use case produces an obligation list under both laws, and the practical action list a non-technical owner can start today is not enormous. Inventory the tool and what it decides. Confirm a human can and does overturn its output. Make sure a privacy impact assessment was or is being done for high-risk use. Stand up a way to respond to a person asking what you have on them, how you decided, and asking you to correct or delete it. None of these need code. They need documentation, a named owner, and a process that actually runs.

## A checklist for figuring out which rules apply to your AI use

You now have the map. Here is the plain procedure for applying it to any AI use case in your organization, and this is the part you can run without waiting for legal.

Ask these six questions about each use case, one tool and one purpose at a time:

- Does this tool touch personal data, including data it infers about people? If you cannot rule it out, assume yes.
- Which people does it touch? People in the EU point at the GDPR. People or residents in California point at the CCPA. A tool used across a normal organization can touch both, so run both lenses before you decide.
- Does the tool make or substantially influence a decision a person will feel, such as hiring, credit, benefits, housing, or access to services? If yes, the automated-decision rules in both frameworks are the place to focus.
- Is a human in the loop, and can that human actually change the result? Write down who and how, and make the path demonstrable rather than assumed.
- Has anyone assessed the privacy risk before this use case started, and should a formal impact assessment or risk assessment be done? This is the discipline that protects you when the use case is high risk.
- Can you answer a person asking what you have on them, how a decision was made, and asking you to correct or delete it? Stand up one real process that works under both laws rather than two perfect ones that never run.

Work through these with the tool owner in Lesson 20's inventory, and you will have a short, concrete list of the compliance work that use case actually needs, instead of a vague sense that privacy is someone else's problem.

## Try it: run the six questions on one AI use case

Take one AI use case that runs or is planned in your organization, ideally the same one you have been carrying through this course. Write out the six questions above and answer them in one page of honest notes:

1. Name the personal data the tool touches, including anything it infers about people.
2. Say which people are affected (EU, California, or both) and why.
3. Decide whether the tool makes or substantially influences a meaningful decision about a person.
4. Name the specific human who can change the tool's output, or write down that there is none and flag it.
5. Record whether any privacy or risk assessment was done, and if it was high-risk processing, whether a formal one is due.
6. Describe the actual process for answering a person who asks what you have, how you decided, and asks for a correction or deletion.

Bring that page to whoever owns privacy and legal at your organization and use it as the discussion document. If step four has no named human, or step six has no process, you have found the gap this lesson is about, and you found it while you could still act. Hold the core idea: privacy compliance for AI is not a certificate you buy once. It is the set of real answers to these questions, kept current as your AI use changes.

## What's next

You have now learned to see the privacy rules that anchor your AI use and to identify which obligations apply to each use case. That work is preparation for the moment every program eventually hits, and it is better to have done the prep first.

The next lesson is Incident response: when an AI tool goes wrong, Lesson 22 of the course. It picks up where this one stops: you have documented what is at stake and who owns it, and now the lesson teaches you what to do when a tool actually fails, whether that is a data breach, a model saying something harmful, an automated decision that harms someone, or a tool your team was using without approval. It walks you through how to tell the difference between an incident and a near miss, who has to be in the room, how far you have to go to be transparent with the people affected, and how to turn the aftermath into a change in your policy instead of a repeat.

For now, hold the transferable idea, not the statute text: privacy law cares about personal data, and AI runs on personal data, so the two were never going to stay separate. Your job is not to become a lawyer. It is to make sure your AI use has a lawful basis, a human who can overturn significant decisions, an assessment for risky processing, and a real way for a person to ask about, correct, or challenge what was done with their data. Answer those, and you will be defensible under GDPR, under CCPA, and under whichever privacy law comes next.
