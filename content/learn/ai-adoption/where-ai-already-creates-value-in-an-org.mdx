# Lesson 2: Where AI Already Creates Value in an Org (and Where It Does Not)

Your organization is past the "will anyone use this?" stage. The last global survey on the subject is blunt about how far adoption has come. McKinsey's 2025 survey of nearly two thousand participants found 88 percent of organizations reporting regular AI use in at least one business function, up from 78 percent the year before. [^1] The tool is in the building, and somewhere in almost every company, people are using it.

That makes the next question harder and far more important: does that usage add up to value, and if not, why not? The same survey gives a sobering answer. Nearly two-thirds of organizations had not yet begun scaling AI across the enterprise. Only 39 percent attributed any bottom-line impact to AI at all, and most of that small group said the impact was under five percent of earnings. [^1] You have a near-universal adoption rate and a rare value rate. That gap is not an accident. It is the direct result of where organizations point AI, and too many point it everywhere instead of where it actually produces value.

This is the lesson where you learn to aim. Lesson 1 gave you the identity shift, that adoption is a program with a named owner, not a rollout that ends. This lesson gives you the judgment. You cannot run the program well until you can look at one piece of work in your organization and say honestly whether AI improves it. Applied function by function, that judgment is what separates an organization that collects AI activity from one that captures AI value. [^1]

## First, the words we are using

Three terms anchor everything in this lesson, and they deserve definitions before the argument.

A **use case** is a single, named instance where AI is applied to a specific workflow. "Customer support drafts replies" is a use case. "We use AI" is not a use case, it is a wish. Keeping things at the level of the use case matters because value and failure both live at that level, not at the level of the technology or the department.

A **workflow** is the sequence of steps, by whoever and whatever performs them, that turns an input into an output. The workflow is the stage where AI either earns its keep or quietly becomes a cost. You will hear this word a lot, because the research repeatedly points at the workflow, not the model, as the place where value is won or lost.

**Value** from AI means one of two things: the work costs less to produce, or the work brings in more revenue. A third category, improved quality or speed that lets the same people do more of the work, counts as value too, but it usually shows up in the cost or revenue columns eventually. Anything AI does that does not land in one of those columns is activity, not value. Diagnosing whether a use case will produce value is mostly a matter of reading the workflow against a short set of tests, which is what the next section gives you.

## The shape of work that AI actually improves

The value in AI is not spread evenly across everything a company does. It concentrates in work that has a recognizable shape. After you have looked at enough use cases, the pattern is consistent, and you can test any workflow against five questions.

**The inputs are structured and repeatable.** The task looks broadly the same each time it happens. An invoice, a support ticket, a purchase order, a standard contract clause: these arrive in predictable forms with predictable fields. The more a workflow's inputs vary, the less reliably AI can handle them, because the model is doing more guessing about what it is even looking at.

**There is a clear output that someone consumes.** The work produces a defined thing, a drafted reply, a coded entry, a summary, a prioritized list. The output may still need human review, but its shape is clear before AI touches it. Work whose output nobody can name is not ready.

**The output can be checked cheaply.** A human can look at what AI produced and tell, quickly, whether it is right. This is the test that quietly filters out most glamorous use cases. If a wrong answer is hard to spot, you have built a machine for manufacturing confident error.

**Errors are cheap to fix.** When AI gets something wrong, the downside is small and recoverable. A mistyped field is re-edited. A draft reply is corrected before it ships. The expensive failures are the ones where an error is hard to notice and hard to undo.

**The task happens at scale.** The workflow runs often enough that savings or output gains accumulate. A process that happens twice a month cannot fund a redesign that took a month to build.

Line those five tests up and the picture of value-ready work appears: high volume, predictable inputs, a clear deliverable, cheap and easy verification, and forgiving errors. Whenever you hear a claim that AI will transform some part of the business, run it through these five. If the work does not have the shape, the claim is salesmanship.

## Where the data says value actually lives

The survey data lands exactly on that shape. The same McKinsey study asked where organizations were seeing benefits, and the answers are consistent with the five tests. [^1]

**Cost benefits concentrate in three places: software engineering, manufacturing, and IT.** [^1] Think about why. Generating boilerplate code, inspecting defects on a production line, and running a service desk are all high volume, predictable-input, cheap-to-verify work. They are the shape of value-ready work written down. Software engineering is perhaps the clearest example: the craft has a huge volume of routine, well-defined steps that still require a human at the end to review the whole, which is exactly the profile this lesson describes.

**Revenue increases are most often reported in marketing and sales, strategy and corporate finance, and product and service development.** [^1] These are the functions where AI helps produce drafts, build analysis, size options, and move more potential deals down the funnel. Here the underlying volume is lower but the leverage is higher, one better draft or one sharper analysis can shift a larger outcome.

**The broadest reported benefit is qualitative: 64 percent of respondents said AI is enabling innovation, and nearly half reported improvement in customer satisfaction and competitive differentiation.** [^1] These matter, but read them for what they are. They are leading indicators, signs that a use case might later turn into hard cost or revenue. They are not proof that it already has. An adoption owner who stops at "everyone feels more innovative" has collected activity and called it value.

The other number that should shape your whole program is the share of organizations at scale. Only about one-third of organizations had begun scaling AI across the enterprise, and the minority seeing real financial impact, which McKinsey calls AI high performers, sits at roughly 6 percent of respondents. [^1] Almost everyone is using AI and almost nobody has scaled it. That is the opportunity this course exists to help you seize, because the field is not crowded with competitors who have already won. It is crowded with competitors who bought the tools and stopped.

## The lever that turns use into value

Here is the most important finding in the whole survey, and it is the one adoption owners should internalize. The difference between high performers and everyone else is not the tool. It is the workflow.

Organizations seeing the most value are nearly three times as likely as others to have fundamentally redesigned individual workflows, and the survey calls that intentional redesign one of the strongest contributors to meaningful business impact of all the factors it tested. [^1] The natural reading is stark: value from AI is not a property of the model, it is a property of the workflow you build around the model. Two companies can use the identical product and get radically different results because one bolts the AI onto an unchanged process and the other rewires the process to take advantage of what the AI does well.

This reframes where AI fails. When a use case does not deliver, the failure is usually not "the AI was bad." It is "we did not change the work around the AI." A support team that pastes a ticket into a tool, copies the reply, and sends it without review has not captured value, it has added a step. The same team that redesigned how replies are drafted and reviewed, with the AI producing a first pass and the human owning the final send, has genuinely changed the work. The tool is identical. The workflow is not.

That is why this lesson keeps coming back to the shape of the work. The five tests tell you where the opportunity is, and the redesign tells you whether you actually capture it. Both have to be true.

## Where AI does not create value, yet

For balance, name the places where AI reliably fails to add value, because understanding those is as useful as understanding the successes.

**Work with no cheap way to verify the answer.** Final judgments, a single legal opinion with either a fine or a lawsuit on the other end, a one-time pricing call in a market where being wrong loses a deal, these fail the verification test. A confident wrong answer that nobody can catch is worse than no answer.

**Judgment with no objective ground truth.** When "good" is a matter of experience and taste, a human opinion, a creative concept that is the firm's differentiator, a sensitive personnel call, AI produces a plausible-sounding average that captures the wrong thing. The output looks reasonable and is meaningfully off.

**High-stakes, low-frequency decisions.** Even where the work is otherwise structured, if it happens once a quarter and an error is expensive and hard to undo, the scale and error-cost tests both fail. You do not want the model drafting the terms of the one acquisition your company does this year without deep human ownership.

**Any workflow you are not willing to redesign.** This is the silent killer. If the organization is not ready to change how the work happens, adding AI to an unchanged process is pure cost, a new subscription on top of the same workflow. The value does not fail in the model. It fails in the unchanged process around it.

A useful way to think about it: AI is strong where the work has a repeatable structure and a way to check the result, and weak where value rides on unrepeatable judgment with no easy verification. Most disappointments happen because someone pointed AI at the second kind of work and expected the first kind of result.

## A worked example: scoring Lucia's two candidates

Lucia runs operations at a mid-size freight-brokering firm, and after Lesson 1 she made her map of where AI is actually being used. Two candidate areas emerge, and she scores both against the five tests before she spends any money.

**Accounts payable invoice processing.** Invoices stream in by the thousands each month from carriers. The inputs are structured: vendor, invoice number, amount, service dates, all in predictable fields. The output is clear, a coded entry that feeds the payment system and a work queue for review. Verification is cheap, an accounts clerk scans a batch and flags anything odd. Errors are cheap, a mis-coded line is corrected before payment. And the volume is high. Every one of the five tests passes. Score: value-ready. Lucia's pilot is clear, have AI pre-fill the coding and the clerk review the exceptions rather than keying every line.

**A strategic sourcing bid for a single new carrier contract.** This happens a few times a year, the scope shifts each time, the relevant terms live in different places, what a good outcome looks like is a judgment call made by an experienced commercial lead, and an error, a missed liability clause, has expensive, long-lived consequences. It fails the repeatable-inputs test, the cheap-verification test, and the error-cost test at once. This is not a place for AI to decide anything. It is a place for a human to use AI as a research aid, with the human owning the entire judgment and the final send.

The point of the example is not the two answers. It is the method. Scoring is fast, it is honest, and it removes the emotion from the decision. Lucia does not need to be an AI expert. She needs to read a workflow and answer five questions. The difficulty of the course is not technical. It is the discipline of applying the same five questions to every department that wants budget. [^1] That discipline, remembering to treat the whole workflow as the thing being changed, is exactly what separates organizations that capture value from those that merely adopt. [^1]

## Try it

This exercise takes about thirty minutes and turns your Lesson 1 map into a shortlist of pilot candidates.

Take two pieces of work from your Lesson 1 map, places where AI is either already being used or where someone wants to use it. For each, write down its answers to the five tests: are the inputs structured and repeatable, is there a clear output someone consumes, can the output be checked cheaply, are errors cheap to fix, and does the work happen at scale. Give each a pass or fail, no partial credit, and add a one-line note for anything that is not clear.

When you are done, you will have a table with three columns: the function, its pass count out of five, and the one-line note. Two follow-up questions sharpen it. First, for anything that passed most of the tests, what would changing the workflow around it look like, not the tool, the workflow? Write one sentence about who reviews the AI output and what they do when it is wrong. Second, for anything that mostly failed, what single change to the work, more standardization, more steps, a clearer review step, would move it toward passable?

The candidate that passes the most tests and has a credible answer on the workflow question is your likely pilot. The one that fails most tests is your answer to the next person who asks why you are not "doing AI" there. You are, in the only sense that matters, you are doing it where it has a chance of producing value, and you can now show the reasoning instead of shrugging.

Bring that one-line pilot plan to the next lesson. It is exactly the use case you will need when you learn to measure whether AI is actually paying for itself.

## What's next

You now have the aim. You can look at any piece of work, run it through five tests, and judge whether AI has a real chance of improving it or whether it is a dressed-up way to spend budget. You have also seen the hardest lesson in the whole practice: the value lives in the redesigned workflow, not in the model, which is why the same tool changes the economics so differently from one company to the next.

That still leaves the question you need before you invest a dollar further: how do you know it is actually working? Pilot projects and polished demos are not evidence. Lesson 3 takes on measurement directly, teaching you the outcomes that matter and how to track them. You will learn what to measure, namely time saved, quality, usage, and churn, why those four tell you whether a use case is real, and how to read them without drowning in dashboards. It builds directly on the pilot you just picked, because you cannot measure what you have not chosen.

For now, hold the two ideas from this lesson. Value concentrates in work with a repeatable shape, structured inputs, a clear checkable output, cheap errors, and real volume. And the tool does not create that value, the redesign of the workflow around it does. Score the work before you spend, and aim the program where the shape says value actually lives.
