Every AI program produces two kinds of news: wins and setbacks. Most adoption owners handle them as if they follow different rules. Wins get announced, inflated slightly, and put on the internal newsletter. Setbacks get quietly handled, softened, or pushed down a level so nobody has to own them out loud. The habit feels natural, and it is exactly what erodes the trust the program runs on.

Here is the uncomfortable version that actually works: the wins and the setbacks deserve the same treatment. Same footnotes, same numbers, same audience, same plain language. Not because every result is equal, but because the thing people are actually watching is whether you can be trusted to report accurately. This lesson is about building that accuracy into how you communicate, before the first real failure makes it urgent.

## What honest AI communication means

Honest AI communication is reporting what AI actually does in your organization, the good and the bad, with the same specific evidence and the same level of directness for both. It is not a style choice. It is a discipline with measurable rules.

Three rules hold it together.

**Report measured results, not intentions.** When you claim a win, you need the number that backs it, the same way you would for any investment you are accountable for. Not a future hope, not a project title, an actual measured outcome: time saved on a workflow, error rate on a process, throughput on a team. When the number is thin, say the number is thin. A launch event is not a business result, and describing it as one is the start of the exaggeration problem.

**Report setbacks with the same directness as wins.** A failed pilot, a tool that did not deliver on its pilot claim, an adoption metric that fell after you told everyone it would rise. These get the same public treatment as a win: named, measured, and put in front of the same people. That is the part most leaders resist, and it is the part that carries the most trust.

**Leave room for uncertainty without hiding it.** You will not know the answer to every question. Say so plainly, state what you are doing to find out, and set a time when you will report back. Pretending to be certain when you are not is its own form of fabrication, and teams notice it faster than you think.

There is a name for the behavior this discipline guards against, and it is common enough to have a label: AI washing.

## The two failure modes: overstatement and concealment

AI washing is the practice of exaggerating, misrepresenting, or falsely claiming AI capabilities or results to gain reputation or advantage. It shows up as product claims that overstate what the technology does, and as internal communication that inflates what the program has achieved.[^3][^4] Leaders fall into it for a reason that is easy to understand: they are under pressure to show the program is working, so the message gets dressed up to sound like better news than the data supports.

The problem is that AI washing does not just sound dishonest. It breaks the program's credibility in a specific way. When the overstated claim is discovered, and it will be, the trust that the whole adoption effort depends on takes the hit, not just the claim. Staff who were leaning toward giving the tool a real chance read the inflated number, see through it, and give up on the messenger along with the message.[^4]

The second failure mode is the mirror image and it is quieter. Rather than overstating the wins, leadership downplays or conceals what is going wrong. The American Management Association makes the point directly: leadership teams that downplay or misrepresent AI's real impact will almost certainly undermine employee trust in leadership's competence and intentions, which lowers engagement, performance, and retention.[^1] The concern that drives concealment is understandable, nobody wants to spook the workforce with bad news. But employees already know AI is powerful, because they use it themselves. So when a leader hides a setback, the team does not conclude nothing went wrong. They conclude the leader is not telling them the whole story, which is a far worse conclusion for the program.

Both failure modes, overstatement and concealment, share a root cause. The leader is treating communication as a way to manage feelings rather than as a way to report accurately. It feels safer in the moment and it is corrosive over time.

## Why honesty is the durable strategy

Across these two directions, the same pattern emerges from the evidence: employees are already alert to the gap between what leaders claim and what AI actually does.

Employee trust in leadership is thin as a baseline. Per Gallup's research, only about a fifth of people trust their leadership, and the gap is widening even as organizations push AI adoption forward.[^2] Into that environment, an inflated claim or a hidden setback lands with force, because it plays directly into an existing suspicion. And the numbers on resistance are not small: one industry survey found roughly 30 percent of employees actively sabotage their employer's AI strategy, rising to 44 percent among Gen Z workers, a resistance the survey concludes stems less from the technology itself than from concerns about how the organization is implementing it.[^2]

This is the case for honesty stated plainly. You cannot buy your way out of that skepticism with a cheerier newsletter. You can only wear it down with a track record of accurate reporting, win by win, setback by setback, until the team has independent reason to believe the next thing you say.

The honest approach also protects you from your own blind spots. The Berkeley research on AI washing notes that when leaders lack the technical grounding to evaluate AI claims, they become reliant on others to tell them what the technology does, which is exactly when exaggerated or misleading claims pass through unchecked.[^3] A communication discipline that requires every claim to be tied to a measured result is the check against that. It forces you to ask, before you say anything, what the number actually is. If nobody can produce one, the claim does not go out.

## A reporting cadence that tells both truths

The mechanics of honest communication are not complicated, but they need to be built into a rhythm, or they quietly collapse back into spin. Here is a cadence that works across a standard program.

**Share wins and setbacks in the same forum, on the same schedule.** Pick one recurring moment, a monthly adoption review, a standing item in the ops meeting, and report both in it. A "wins and setbacks" slot that always appears establishes that neither is being hidden. The moment the setbacks stop showing up, people start assuming they are being managed off the books, and you lose the reputation you spent months building.

**Lead the number and name the source.** For every claim, state the metric, the value, and where it came from. "Time to close a support ticket is down, based on our ticket system's workflow report" beats "AI is making the team faster." The second invites skepticism. The first invites a follow-up question, and answering it is what builds trust.

**Retire stale claims on a schedule.** The biggest source of AI-washing-adjacent damage is not new lies, it is old claims left standing after the situation changes. A pilot that reported a great win in March and then could not scale in June needs its March claim updated, not quietly left on the board. Build a review where every published claim gets revalidated or corrected.

**Report a setback with what you learned and what is next.** A setback reported without forward motion reads as bad news, and bad news alone discourages people. A setback reported with the specific lesson and the specific next step reads as a team learning to use the tool better, which is exactly what a maturing program looks like. This maps to the change-cadence advice from Lesson 7, where resurfacing outcomes on a fixed rhythm does more than any one brilliant announcement.

**Make it easy for people to bring you bad news.** Honest communication is not only about what you say. It is about whether problems can travel to you at all. If the only feedback channels punish the messenger, setbacks will stop reaching you, and no amount of honest reporting on your end can fix information that never arrives. Build a channel where a failed experiment or a wrong result can be surfaced without blame, because that flow of honest problems is what keeps the program's claims truthful in the first place.

## Worked example: Aisha learns that honesty is the pitch

Aisha owns AI adoption at a regional financial-services firm with about 250 employees. Her north-star metric, set in Lesson 5, is the share of client-onboarding data-entry work that is drafted with an AI tool, targeting 65 percent by the end of the quarter.

In week two of the rollout, the number jumps to 40 percent and Aisha is tempted to announce it loudly. She almost writes the newsletter: "AI is transforming our onboarding, 40 percent adoption in two weeks." She stops, because her rule is that every claim needs a measured result and a named source. The 40 percent is real, but it is reach, how many entries involved the tool at all, not depth, how many were actually completed with it end to end. She reports the honest number instead: 40 percent of entries touched an AI draft, 11 percent finished entirely that way, and the bulk of the work still runs through the old process.

The restraint costs her a flashier headline and it buys her the thing that matters more. The operations lead, who has been skeptical since the program was announced, comes back with a pointed question: "Fine, but will this actually hold up under a real end-of-month volume spike?" Aisha says she does not know yet, that the tool has not seen a full month-end close, and that the team is going to run a controlled test through the next close to measure it, and she commits to reporting the results in the monthly review either way.

The month-end test does not go well at first. Throughput does not improve under volume, and the tool starts suggesting split accounts that the compliance team rejects. Aisha reports it plainly at the monthly review, number by number, in the "setbacks" slot she never skips. She names the specific failure, what the team learned about where the tool's training fell short for their account structure, and the next step, which is a stricter prompt template and a compliance review pass.

Nothing about that report reads as failure to the people watching. It reads as accuracy. And the operations lead, who has now seen Aisha report a miss completely straight, is more willing to trust the next claim she makes than if she had papered over everything with momentum talk. By the end of the quarter the north-star metric clears 65 percent, partly because the honest reporting kept a skeptical operator engaged in the fix instead of retreating to the old way. The win came through trust, and the trust came through the setbacks being reported the same way as the wins.

## Try it: build your communication policy on paper

Write out how you will handle the two news types, before you need to, so the pattern is already set when the first real win or miss arrives.

First, write your one-line rule for wins. What number must exist before you will announce a win out loud, and where does that number come from? If you cannot name the source metric, you have not written the rule, you have written a slogan.

Second, write your one-line rule for setbacks. What conditions will make you report a problem to the same audience, on the same schedule, as a win? The answer should not be "if it is big enough to matter," because by then half the trust is already gone. It should be based on the result being materially different from what you communicated before.

Third, name your recurring forum. Where and on what cadence will wins and setbacks both appear, every time? If you have no standing slot for the setbacks, assume they will default to being handled quietly, and decide now whether that is a risk you can afford.

Fourth, list one current claim that is aging. Which number currently on your internal board or update is the oldest and most likely to be stale? Write the correction it needs and when you will issue it. Retiring it now is cheaper than defending it later.

## What's next

This lesson made the case for reporting AI wins and setbacks with the same plain accuracy, because the trust a program runs on is built one honest report at a time, and it survives the first real failure only when the setbacks were never hidden. But honest reporting has a counterpart it depends on: the people hearing the message are not neutral. Plenty of your audience is afraid the new tool will take their work, or skeptical that the promised change will ever arrive, and no amount of accurate reporting lands cleanly until you address that fear directly. Lesson 10 is about addressing fear and skepticism as a leader, which is the skill that determines whether your honest reports get heard at all.
