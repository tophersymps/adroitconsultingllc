The previous lesson handed you a budget that can survive AI's cost shape. This one answers the harder, more political question that budget silently depends on: whether the money is worth it. A company can build a defensible budget, govern every workflow, and still lose the argument for AI, because a budget answers "what does it cost" and the board is asking "what is it worth." Proving the value is a separate job, and every later stage of the program rests on doing it well.

The backdrop makes the stakes concrete. Adoption is everywhere and the payoff is not. In McKinsey's 2026 survey, 89% of respondents said their organizations use AI regularly in at least one function, and 44% said it is scaling across the enterprise, up from 38% a year earlier. Yet only 37% attribute at least some earnings impact to AI, essentially unchanged from the year before, and just 6% qualify as the high performers who attribute 5% or more of earnings to AI and call the impact significant.[^1] At the same time, 80% of respondents say AI has improved their own individual productivity.[^1] Broad adoption and strong individual gains sitting on top of flat financial impact is the exact problem this lesson exists to solve. The value exists somewhere in the organization. Your job is to prove it, not to hope leadership notices it on its own.

## Define the terms before the argument

Five terms frame every ROI conversation, and the meeting ends in disagreement when people use them loosely.

**ROI.** The financial value an AI investment creates, measured against its total cost of ownership and adjusted for the risk the AI introduces.[^3] The risk adjustment matters. A tool that saves time but also drives errors, or one a vendor can change under you, earns a lower realized return than its gross numbers suggest.

**Baseline.** What the business did before AI entered the picture, captured at the same level of detail you intend to measure after.[^3] You capture it for a full quarter before a deployment and again for the same period after. Without a baseline you can report activity, but you cannot prove impact, because there is nothing to subtract the before from.

**Leading indicator versus financial outcome.** Adoption rate, prompts per user, and self-reported time saved are leading indicators. They are useful early in a deployment for showing momentum. They are not a financial outcome. The claims you can defend to a board are revenue uplift, cost reduction, and risk reduction, the three outcomes a use case should connect to if it is going to be presented as ROI.[^3]

**Total cost of ownership.** Not just licences and build. Integration, training, workflow changes, and governance all sit inside the real cost, and they are often where the realized return is decided.[^2] Lesson 27's three-bucket budget is the input here; you already did this work.

**Realisation assumptions.** The expectations that must hold in practice for the benefit to be real: adoption (do people actually use it), translation (does an individual's gain become team value), and performance (are the outputs reliable).[^2]

## Lay the claim out in four layers

The quickest way to lose a CFO is to lead with a dashboard of usage. Usage is a behavioural number; it does not answer a financial question. Structure the proof instead as four layers, each feeding the one above it: financial outcomes on top, operational metrics beneath them, behavioural metrics next, and AI-specific health at the base.[^3]

The top layer carries the dollars and it is the only one the board reads on its own: revenue impact, margin change, cost saved, risk reduction. Underneath sit the operating levers that produce those dollars, cycle time, throughput per employee, error rate, resolution time. Below those sit the behaviour changes that drive the levers, adoption, usage depth, and the share of work where AI is now in the path. At the base sit the AI-specific health numbers that keep the whole stack honest: hallucination rate, guardrail interventions, drift, and the cost of ownership.[^3]

Arrange them that way on purpose. When the CFO asks "where is the money," you point up the stack, not sideways at a usage chart. Each layer is the evidence for the layer above it, and the bottom three exist to defend the top one.

## Prove it use case by use case

Program ROI is the sum of a set of use case ROIs, and each one starts the same way. KPMG's framework is explicit that credible measurement begins with one targeted use case with a clear, measurable outcome, because a broad rollout dilutes ownership and makes the ROI impossible to prove.[^2] Match expectations to the phase as well. Early work validates feasibility and risk, scale drives the financial outcomes, and stage gates decide whether to keep funding.[^2] Treat the pilot as the phase where you earn the right to claim a number, not as the number itself.

Then model the full cost base, not just the licence line. KPMG names integration, training, workflow changes, and governance as part of the cost, and notes that indirect and structural costs often have the biggest impact on realized ROI.[^2] This is where lesson 27 pays for itself: the budget you built gives you the cost side of the equation already.

## Stress test the headline number

Here is where most ROI claims quietly die. A figure like "we save eight hours per person per week" is not a financial outcome until you can answer where the eight hours went. The translation between an individual gain and organizational value is one of the three realisation assumptions KPMG says to test, alongside adoption and performance.[^2] And the common ways a claim collapses all sit in the same gap between activity and realized value: confusing usage with value, ignoring total cost of ownership, counting time saved that was never reinvested, and reporting only the successful pilots while the failures disappear from the deck.[^3]

Run the redeemed-capacity test first. If AI saves a team time but the team does not pick up additional, higher-value work, the company has paid for capacity it did not consume, and the savings exist only on paper.[^3] Put this question to every claim: did the time become revenue, avoided cost, or reduced risk, or did it just go home? Only the first three count, and you can prove them by watching the redeployment, not by assuming it.

## Present it like any capital investment

The discipline pays off when you sit across from the person who owns the budget. Present AI the way you would present any major capital investment: a baseline, a financial outcome, a cost line, and a statement of risk.[^3] The fact that the investment is AI does not change the structure of the ask.

McKinsey's high performers give you the content of that ask. They are not distinguished by spending more. They are distinguished by how they deploy: they pursue growth and innovation alongside efficiency, and they fundamentally redesign the workflows AI enables rather than inserting AI into the ones that already exist.[^1] A pilot that automates the same steps with the same handoffs is a cost giveback. A redesign that changes which steps happen, and who owns them, is a return. When you prove ROI, prove it on the redesigned version, because that is the number that scales to the next lesson.

## Worked example: Priya proves the support automation

Priya owns AI adoption at a mid-size software company. The pilot everyone points at is the customer support automation, and leadership wants a number before she rolls it out anywhere else. She builds the case in the four layers, stating her numbers as assumptions out loud, because this is a model, not a forecast, and the analysis is only as strong as the assumptions she will then verify.

She starts with the baseline, captured for the quarter before the pilot. The support team resolves 8,000 tickets a month. Average handle time is 14 minutes a ticket, and the fully loaded cost of an agent hour is $40. That is about 1,867 agent hours a month on resolution, or roughly $74,700 in labor, a baseline of about $9 per ticket.[^3]

After the pilot, in the same-length period, the same 8,000 tickets resolve in an average of 8 minutes as agents draft replies with AI. That is about 1,067 hours, or $42,700. The gross monthly labor saving is about $32,000, which annualizes to roughly $384,000. That is the headline number, and it is not the ROI.

The realisation test comes next, because the headline assumes every minute shows up as value. Priya and the support lead trace where the saved time actually went. About half covered a growing ticket queue and avoided a new hire, which is real cost avoidance. About a quarter went into higher-quality responses, visible in better first-contact resolution and a stronger customer-satisfaction score. The last quarter was not actually redeployed in the period, so she leaves it out of the hard number and logs it as upside. On the realized half alone she books about $192,000, not $384,000. That split is her assumption to confirm with the team's time logs, not a fact she asserts.

Then the cost side, from the budget built in the previous lesson. The automation's share of platform usage, a slice of the seat and usage buckets, plus integration, training, and a share of the program owner's time and governance, comes to about $95,000 a year.

The calculation reads: realized annual benefit around $192,000, total cost of ownership $95,000, net about $97,000, simple ROI of roughly 100%. A couple of times the money back, once you accept only the realized portion. If she had taken the full $384,000, she would have quoted a 400% return that the finance team would dismantle the moment the time logs failed to back it.

Below the top layer she attaches the evidence that makes the number credible: resolution time by ticket class, the share of tickets where an agent drafted with AI, and the AI-health numbers showing the automation is not quietly introducing errors. On the risk line she names what could change the figure, a vendor price move on usage tokens and a shift in ticket mix, and states how each would be watched. That turns a number into a case.

![The worked example reconciles to a roughly 100 percent realized ROI: gross annual saving of 384 thousand dollars, about half realized as avoided hire and higher quality, a 95 thousand dollar total cost of ownership, and a net of about 97 thousand](</diagrams/proving-the-ai-programs-roi-to-the-business/3-priya-roi.png>)

Her four-page board note keeps it tight: page one the financial summary, page two the operational evidence behind it, page three the portfolio view of which efforts are working, paused, or stopped, and page four the risks and the controls on them.[^3] She reports the pilot as one use case with a verifiable baseline, because that is the format that survives a finance review, and it is the one that lets the next lesson take the same discipline to the rest of the organization.

## Try it: build one ROI case

Do this now, on your real program, before the next budget conversation.

First, pick the single use case the program would most like to defend, the one with the clearest measurable outcome. Second, write out its baseline captured before AI changed it, at the same detail you will measure after. If the deployment is already live, build the best counterfactual you can from historical data or from a team that does not have the tool.[^3] Third, model the full cost from your lesson 27 budget, including integration, training, and governance, not just licences. Fourth, run the redemption test: say where the saved time went, and book only the share that became revenue, avoided cost, or reduced risk, logging the rest as unliquidated upside. Fifth, lay the result out in the four layers and write the two sentences you would say to a CFO: the business outcome this use case changes, and its cost per unit of that outcome.

When you finish, it should survive one question: what baseline, what outcome, what full cost, and what proof that the benefit was realized? If you can answer all four with numbers the CFO can check, you have an ROI case. If you cannot, that is the gap to close before anyone asks.

## What's next

This lesson turned one use case into a defensible ROI story: a baseline, a financial outcome, a full cost line, and proof that the benefit was realized rather than assumed. But one credible use case is not a program, and a single number, however well evidenced, does not yet justify AI across an organization. Lesson 29 is where the proof earns its keep: scaling from pilot to org-wide, taking the discipline that won the first case and repeating it across the portfolio, standardizing the measurement so each new use case reuses the pattern instead of rebuilding it, and moving the conversation from "one automation paid for itself" to "the program, as a whole, is worth funding."
