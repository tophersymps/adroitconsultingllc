Lesson 17 sent you out to score an AI tool against a rubric before you paid for it. That was the right discipline, and it is worthless the moment your signature leaves a gap between what the sales team promised and what the contract actually grants. The demo was about capability. The license is about rights, costs, and risks, and the two rarely line up.

Most AI buying is decided on price and enthusiasm, then signed without reading the parts that define who owns what and who pays when something breaks. When you sign for AI you are not buying software in the way you bought it in 2015. You are buying a right to use a service the vendor can change, a cost that may scale with usage you cannot forecast, and a set of obligations that mostly land on you. This lesson is the map of that contract.

## What a license is, and what it stops being for AI

A software license is permission to use something you do not own, under terms the owner sets. For a classic SaaS product that was a simple idea: you rented access to predictable software with predictable features, you supplied your own data bounds, and the "if any" language rarely mattered because the output was deterministic.

AI breaks every one of those assumptions, which is why a standard SaaS template is a trap for AI buying.[^2] An AI tool is not a fixed product with a fixed behavior. Its output is probabilistic, it can change under you, and the vendor can alter or remove the model on short notice. That changes what the license has to cover: who owns the outputs, whether your data trains the model, who answers for a wrong or infringing output, and what happens to your work when the model you signed for gets replaced.

## The pricing models vendors will quote you

The first thing a vendor hands over is a price. Before it means anything, you need to know which of these you are looking at, because they behave completely differently under real usage.

![Six AI pricing models, showing what each charges per unit and who carries the risk when usage grows](</diagrams/licensing-and-procurement-what-you-are-actually-buying/1-pricing-models.png>)

**Per seat** charges a fixed monthly or annual fee per person with access, regardless of how much they use it. It is simple to budget and familiar. It fits tools with low, bounded, predictable usage. It becomes a margin problem for the vendor when a few heavy users consume far more compute than the seat fee covers, and that pressure is exactly why pure per-seat AI pricing is fading in favor of hybrids.[^1]

**Per token** charges per unit of input and output the model processes (typically per 1,000 tokens), with output usually priced higher than input. It aligns cost with usage, which is transparent to a technical buyer. It is terrible for budgeting when the buyer is finance or a non-technical owner, because a single complex request can consume 50 to 100 times the tokens of a simple one, so the bill is hard to forecast.[^1]

**Per activity** charges per discrete action the AI performs, like each API call, conversation, or workflow run. **Per output** charges per generated artifact, like each image or drafted document. **Per outcome** charges only when a defined business result happens, like a resolved ticket or a closed case. Outcome pricing transfers cost variance from the buyer to the seller, so it only gets offered when the vendor is confident about its own cost per outcome and the outcome is measurable.[^1]

**Hybrid** combines a subscription base with a usage or outcome component. The most common shape is a monthly base that includes an allowance, with usage beyond the allowance billed at a per-unit rate, or prepaid credits drawn down per action. This is why most mature AI products price this way: the base gives the buyer a predictable number to budget against while the variable part protects the vendor when adoption scales past the allowance.[^1]

The pricing model is not trivia. It decides which side of the table owns the risk when usage goes up, and it decides whether the number in next year's budget is a fact or a guess.

## Worked example: the same assistant, three ways

Let me run the arithmetic so you can see how the model changes what you actually pay. Assume a mid-size firm wants an AI assistant for 120 support staff drafting responses to client tickets. This is an illustration, so the rates are placeholders, not a quote from any vendor; replace them with the tool under consideration before you draw any real conclusion.

Take a working assumption that each active user sends about 15 drafting requests a day, across 20 working days a month, and each request burns roughly 1,000 input tokens and 300 output tokens. That gives this firm about 36,000 requests a month, or 432,000 a year. The math is straightforward and you can redo it for your own numbers.

![The same assistant priced three ways, landing at 57,600, 66,960, or about 78,566 a year depending on the pricing model](</diagrams/licensing-and-procurement-what-you-are-actually-buying/2-same-assistant-three-ways.png>)

**Per-seat pricing at $40 per user per month.** The bill is flat: 120 seats times $40 times 12 months equals $57,600 a year, no matter how heavily the team uses it. Easy to budget, and cheap if they use it a lot.

**Per-token pricing at $0.05 per 1,000 input tokens and $0.35 per 1,000 output tokens.** Each request is $0.05 of input plus $0.105 of output, about $0.155 in total. At 36,000 requests a month that is $5,580 a month and $66,960 a year, already above the flat seat price because this team uses the tool heavily. The kicker is variance. In a month where volume spikes to 60,000 requests, the bill jumps to $9,300. And if a single complex request hits the 100x token range, one such request alone costs around $15.50 against a typical $0.155, which is a hundredfold swing any month a user pastes a long context. The annual number is a guess until the year is over.[^1]

**Hybrid pricing with a $20 per-user base, an included allowance of 12 requests per user per month, and $0.12 overage per request.** The base is $28,800 a year. The allowance covers 1,440 requests a month, leaving about 34,560 in overage each normal month, which at $0.12 comes to roughly $4,147 a month and about $49,766 a year of overage, for a total around $78,566. More expensive, but the base is a locked number and only the overage floats with usage, which is far easier to forecast and to cap than a pure token meter.

The point of running all three is not that one is right. It is that the same tool, same people, same workload, can land at $57,600, $66,960, or $78,566 a year depending on how the vendor prices it, and that the per-token number carries a variance the others do not. When you compare quotes, compare apples to apples: the model, the assumed usage, and who carries the risk when usage moves. A quote is not comparable until you know the usage those numbers assume, because that assumption is doing all the work.

## The clauses that hide the real deal

Once the price makes sense, the contract itself matters more than the price. These are the clauses to read before you sign, not the way a sales walkthrough phrases them.

![The contract clauses that hide the real deal: output ownership, the training-data default, who pays for a wrong output, model changes, auto-renewal, and liability caps](</diagrams/licensing-and-procurement-what-you-are-actually-buying/3-clauses-that-hide-the-deal.png>)

**Who owns the outputs.** Vendor templates often assign you "all right, title, and interest, if any" in the outputs. The "if any" is doing the heavy lifting. Under current U.S. copyright law, a work generated entirely by AI without sufficient human authorship may not be copyrightable at all, so there may be no right to assign regardless of what the sentence claims.[^2] A marketing agency promising clients full IP transfer while its AI vendor grants only a license to use outputs has a conflict that becomes a breach the moment the tool is used for that client.[^3] Before you sign, confirm what you actually get: outright ownership, or a perpetual, irrevocable license to use, modify, and distribute the outputs whether or not they are protectable.

**Whether your data trains the model.** Many AI agreements default to using customer prompts, uploads, and outputs to improve the vendor's models. For a consumer product that may be fine. For an enterprise tool handling client data, trade secrets, or regulated information, it can mean your proprietary material ends up embedded in a model served to competitors, or a HIPAA-level violation if protected health information is involved. The fix is an explicit, survivable prohibition on using customer data for any training, fine-tuning, or model improvement, written into the contract, not claimed on a marketing page that can change.[^2]

**Who pays when the output is wrong or infringes.** AI tools produce plausible but false information, and vendor agreements routinely disclaim liability for it, refusing to warrant accuracy, completeness, or fitness for any purpose.[^2] On the IP side, most vendor indemnification covers only one direction: if a third party sues because the tool's output infringes their copyright, the vendor defends you. It does not cover the other direction, your inability to enforce the output as a usable asset.[^2] If the output is inaccurate, defamatory, or infringing, and the limit of liability caps the vendor's exposure at the fees paid, your organization absorbs the rest.[^3] For high-consequence uses, push for accuracy representations, disclosure of known failure modes, and remedies beyond the right to cancel, and keep your own internal review process documented.

**What happens when the vendor changes or removes the model.** Standard SaaS contracts protect you from the vendor changing the product on short notice. AI vendor agreements routinely reserve the right to modify, suspend, or deprecate the model on very short notice, sometimes none at all, because they treat model access as a discretionary service rather than a contracted commitment.[^2] Before you sign, ask for a minimum notice period for any model change, transition help, data portability so you can move to another provider, and prorated refunds for prepaid terms if the model you bought is discontinued.

**Auto-renewal and the exit window.** A clause that auto-renews the agreement for successive twelve-month terms unless you give written notice no less than ninety days before the end of the current term looks harmless. Combined, ninety-day notice and a twelve-month term leave a three-week window each year in which you can actually get out, which is the most common reason organizations end up trapped with a tool after it stops performing.[^4] Push the notice down toward thirty days, or ask for month-to-month after the initial term. And check for silent price escalation, where fees adjust annually at the vendor's discretion and can compound well above the quoted price over a few years.[^4]

**Liability caps.** Almost every AI agreement limits the vendor's total liability, often to the fees paid in the preceding months. That is normal in software, but it means for the failures that matter most, a data breach or an infringing output, the vendor's worst case is a refund of recent fees and the rest of the damage is yours.[^3] Read the cap, understand what it excludes, and factor that exposure into your risk assessment rather than discovering it after something fails.

## Try it: read the agreement you are actually considering

Take the AI tool your organization is most seriously considering, the same one you scored in Lesson 17 if you have it. Get the actual agreement, the master services agreement, the terms of service, or the order form, not the sales deck. Work through it in writing and answer each of these:

1. What pricing model is it, and what usage does the quoted price assume? Redo the worked example's arithmetic with your headcount and the vendor's real rates. Does the number stay predictable as usage grows, or does it carry hidden variance?
2. Who owns the outputs, in plain language? Is it outright ownership, a revocable license, or the "if any" version that may convey nothing? Do your client or customer promises contradict what the vendor grants, as in the marketing agency trap?
3. Does your data train the model? Is there an explicit, survivable prohibition, in the contract, or only a silent default that lets the vendor use your prompts and outputs to improve its models?
4. What is the notice period to exit, and does the agreement auto-renew? How many days each year can you actually leave, and can the price escalate without your consent?
5. Who carries the risk if an output is wrong, defamatory, or infringing? What is the liability cap, and what does it exclude?

Write the answers down. Flag every clause you cannot answer or that shifts risk onto you, and take that list to whoever actually negotiates. Most vendors will accept reasonable changes to the exit window, the training-data default, and the IP language, and the ones who will not are telling you how the relationship will go when you have a real problem.[^4]

The habit to build is patience. The contract is the tool after the honeymoon, and every uncomfortable clause you resolve before signing is a problem you will not have to resolve at renewal, when the dependency has become the vendor's leverage. The exit path you tested in Lesson 17 only stays real if the contract you are about to sign keeps it open.
