# Lesson 38: Case study: an org that scaled AI well

Lesson 37 gave you the instruments. This lesson is what they look like when the program attached to them is working.

A word about method before we start, because case studies are where courses cheat. The organization in this lesson is a composite, assembled from published accounts and from our own work with mid-market clients. The figures are illustrative and checkable, and the pattern is the real thing. Where a claim rests on a named, published source, you will see the citation and you can go read it yourself.

That distinction matters more than it sounds. Most "AI success stories" in circulation are vendor case studies, which is not a reason to ignore them, but it is a reason to read them as evidence of what is possible rather than proof of what is typical. We will use one of those deliberately, later in the lesson, because it happens to be unusually specific and because the organization behind it publishes its own constraints.

One more thing before the story. This is the first of two case studies, and the two are deliberately paired. This lesson shows a program that went well. Lesson 39 shows a program that failed, and the failure is not exotic. It is the same company shape, the same tools, and a different set of choices about inventory, access, and what you are allowed to claim in public.

## Define the terms

**Pilot.** A bounded test of one use case with a named owner, a sampling method, and a decision date. A pilot without a decision date is a small permanent deployment with no review.

**Scale gate.** The written conditions a use case must clear before it goes wide. The four that do the work are measured quality on your own tasks, a named business owner, a support path, and a unit cost you can defend.

**Workflow redesign.** Changing the process around the tool rather than inserting the tool into a process that stays the same. This is the difference between the same work going faster and different work happening.

**Change capacity.** The number of process changes a team can absorb in a quarter without the work it already does getting worse. It is a real constraint and it is almost never written down.

**Enablement.** Training plus the path a person uses at four in the afternoon when the output looks wrong and their deadline is tomorrow. Training is the event. Enablement is what happens after it.

**Unit cost per unit of work.** Total cost divided by completed tasks of the type you care about. Cost per seat tells you what you bought. Cost per completed task tells you what you got.

## The org, and the honest starting point

Nadia runs AI for a 3,400-person third-party logistics company. She got the job because she was the operations director who kept saying yes to tools, and eventually the volume of yes outran anyone's ability to keep track. When she inherited the program, the picture was not a disaster, and that is the point. It was the picture most mid-market organizations have.

Nine pilots, in various states of life. Three vendors plus two free tools in use that nobody had approved. Three hundred and forty licensed seats. Weekly active use across the whole estate at 12 percent, which sounds low until you look row by row and find that two teams were at 60 percent and four pilots had zero activity since the person who championed them moved roles.

No inventory. Asked to list which systems were handling customer data, Nadia could name five. The real number, once her team went and looked, was eleven.

One policy, six pages, written eighteen months earlier, describing a tool the company no longer used.

A budget line that had grown every quarter and a reporting line that could say what was spent but not what anything cost per shipment or per claim or per call.

Nadia's first move was not a tool decision. She wrote a one-page list of what she could not answer, and took it to her sponsor. Which systems touch customer data. Which of these nine things is still alive. What one automated document costs against what a person costs. Who can turn each one off. The list fit on a page and it made the case for the next two quarters better than any slide deck.

## The four moves that made the difference

Nadia's program took eighteen months to get from that page to something she could describe to the board without wincing. Four moves did most of the work.

**They wrote scale gates before they needed them.** The gate is four lines: quality measured on thirty of our own tasks, a named business owner, a support path that exists on a Friday, and a unit cost we can defend against the manual alternative. Three of the nine existing pilots failed the first line immediately, because nobody had ever measured quality on anything. Two of them were formally killed in the first quarter. Nadia's team prepared a one-page note for each killed pilot explaining what it was, what it would have needed, and the date it would be reconsidered. That note is the reason killing them did not cost her political capital. The teams involved were not told no. They were told not yet, with a date.

**They redesigned two workflows instead of buying three more tools.** The instinct when adoption is low is to add capability, so the obvious move was three new specialist tools. Nadia did the opposite. She took the two teams already at 60 percent weekly active use and spent a quarter making their work genuinely different rather than faster. The customs documentation team did not get a better summarizer. The sequence changed: the assistant drafted the classification and the exceptions list first, a specialist reviewed the exceptions, and the routine entries stopped passing through a human at all. Time to file a routine entry went from about 26 minutes to under 9, and the specialist's day shifted from entry to exception handling. This is the shape the evidence keeps pointing at. In McKinsey's 2026 global survey, the small group of organizations it calls high performers are distinguished by fundamentally redesigning workflows that AI enables rather than inserting AI into workflows that already exist.[^1]

**They converted change capacity into a number and spent it.** Nadia asked each of the six operations directors one question: how many process changes can your team absorb this quarter without service levels slipping? The answers ranged from one to three, with a total of eleven. Eleven became the budget. When a twelfth idea arrived, it went into a queue with a date rather than into a meeting with enthusiasm. This is the single most transferable move in the lesson, and it is also the one that requires the most nerve, because saying the number out loud means refusing work that a senior person just proposed.

**They made the sponsor's involvement operational rather than ceremonial.** The chief operating officer did not appear at kickoffs and disappear. She chaired the quarterly review, signed every exception, and was the person who said out loud in a meeting that a specific pilot was being killed. That matters because of what it does to the middle of the organization. The NIST AI RMF playbook puts the outcome plainly in its Govern function: executive leadership of the organization takes responsibility for decisions about risks associated with AI system development and deployment.[^2] Responsibility taken by a named executive is a different kind of pressure than responsibility taken by a working group, and the difference shows up in how quickly a risky deployment gets stopped.

## The real case behind the pattern

The composite above is shaped by a real, published account, and it is worth reading on its own terms because the constraints are unusual. BNY, founded in 1784 and one of the institutions whose failure regulators treat as a systemic risk, has described its AI program through Microsoft's WorkLab series.

Three details from that account are worth carrying into your own plan.[^3] First, governance was visible and physical: the account describes a sixteen-step review process of review boards, compliance gates, and model risk evaluations mapped on a wall, the path a use case walks from idea to production in a heavily regulated bank. Second, enablement was measured in hours and cohorts, not in a rollout email: a 40-hour AI bootcamp run during paid work hours that graduated roughly 2,300 employees after starting with 35 participants, with later sessions running 800 strong, and each participant required to build a working prototype from their own part of the bank. Third, the operating model adapted to what the tools actually became. The bank runs roughly 140 internal "digital employees," each with credentials, an employee number in the corporate directory, and a named human supervisor who assigns work and reviews output, and the bank's talent function is building a formal process for deciding when a problem calls for a digital employee rather than a person or a contractor.

Read it as a vendor-published account and it still teaches something specific: at scale, the discipline that matters is the same discipline you would apply to a new hire. Scope deliberately narrow, review the work, recalibrate when reviewers keep sending output back, and have a structured offboarding for when a better version replaces the current one. The account reports that opening investigations in payments dropped by nearly 80 percent after a narrow payment-validations agent went live, and that validations which once took five or six minutes cleared in under thirty seconds. Those numbers come from the vendor and the bank together, so treat them as a demonstration of what a narrow, supervised deployment can do rather than as a benchmark for your own program.

## What month eighteen looked like

| Measure | Month 0 | Month 18 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Live use cases | 9, of which 4 dormant | 5, all measured |
| Licensed seats | 340 | 780 |
| Weekly active use, whole estate | 12 percent | 54 percent |
| Systems touching customer data, on the inventory | 5 (actual: 11) | 14 (actual: 14) |
| Cost per automated customs entry | unknown | 0.41 dollars against 3.80 dollars manual |
| Open findings from the internal review | none recorded | 6, all dated, 2 closed |

The honest parts matter as much as the flattering ones. Two of the nine original pilots were killed, and one of them was killed a full quarter later than it should have been, because Nadia's team needed two quarters to build the sampling that proved it was not working. A workflow redesign on the returns team landed in the middle of a warehouse reorg, adoption never recovered, and that use case is now the one Nadia describes as "paused indefinitely" rather than live. Six open findings is not a clean program. It is a program where someone is looking.

One number is the one she quotes most often, and it is not on the table. Of the 780 licensed seats, 620 are genuinely used weekly, and 160 are not, and the team reviews that gap every quarter rather than celebrating the 780. Licence utilisation is the least glamorous metric in this course and the one that most reliably finds real money.

## What the evidence says about the gap

Nadia's program is unusual, and it is worth knowing how unusual, because the distance between a program like hers and the median is mostly not a technology difference.

McKinsey's 2026 survey found that 44 percent of respondents report AI is scaling across their enterprise, up from 38 percent a year earlier, and that 80 percent of respondents say AI has improved their own individual productivity. The enterprise-level picture is thinner. The share of respondents attributing at least some EBIT impact to AI has held flat at 37 percent, and the proportion it classifies as high performers, meaning organizations attributing at least 5 percent of EBIT to AI while describing its impact as significant, has stayed at about 6 percent of all respondents.[^1]

Read those three numbers together and you have the central problem this course exists to solve. Individual productivity is real and widely reported. Enterprise financial impact is flat and concentrated in a small group. The high performers are not doing something exotic; the survey describes them as pursuing growth and innovation alongside efficiency, redesigning workflows rather than inserting tools, and backing deployments with leadership commitment and operational rigor. That list is unglamorous, and every item on it is available to a mid-market logistics company with no data science team.

Why does any of this need a framework rather than a good spreadsheet? Because the four moves above are a risk management process whether or not anyone calls them that, and the published standards exist mainly to save you from rediscovering the shape from scratch. The NIST AI RMF organizes the work into four functions, govern, map, measure, and manage, and it is explicit that the framework is voluntary, non-sector-specific, and outcome-focused rather than a set of requirements, which means nobody hands you the answer, only the structure.[^4] The management system standard ISO/IEC 42001 does something complementary: it specifies what it takes to establish, implement, maintain, and continually improve an AI management system, and it is the document an auditor or an enterprise customer is most likely to ask you about.[^5] Neither one will design Nadia's scale gate for her. Both will tell her that a gate, a named owner, and a review cycle are supposed to exist.

## Common failure modes

**Reading this case as a tooling story.** Nothing in Nadia's four moves required a better model. Two of the four were meetings and one was a refusal.

**Copying the numbers instead of the sequence.** Her month eighteen numbers depend on her starting point, her industry, and her margin structure. The sequence, gates then redesign then capacity then sponsorship, is the transferable part.

**Treating "redesign" as permission to rip up a process.** The customs documentation redesign kept every control and removed one handoff. It did not remove a control to save a step.

**Killing nothing.** A program that has never stopped anything is not a disciplined program, it is an accumulating one. The health review in lesson 37 exists partly to make stopping routine.

**Confusing a vendor case study with a benchmark.** BNY's payment-agent results are a demonstration under specific conditions. Your version of that number will be different, and you should be the one who measures it.

## Try it: find your own month zero

Do these five things before you read lesson 39, because the failure case only lands if you have a starting point to compare it against.

Write down how many AI use cases are live in your organization. Then go and count them properly, using billing records and sign-in logs rather than memory. The gap between the two numbers is your first finding.

Pick your lowest-usage live use case and write the four scale gate lines for it: quality on thirty of your own tasks, named business owner, support path, unit cost against the manual alternative. Then check how many of the four you can honestly answer today.

Ask each team lead you work with how many process changes their team can absorb next quarter. Write the numbers down and add them. That total is your change capacity, and it is very likely smaller than your list of ideas.

Pull the licence report and separate seats that were used in the last seven days from seats that were not. Multiply the unused count by the annual unit price. That number is the easiest business case you will make this year.

Write the one-page list of what you cannot answer, the way Nadia did, and take it to your sponsor. Do not bring a proposal. Bring the list.
