Every AI adoption program reaches the same fork in the road. Either the new way of working keeps spreading on its own, because there are people inside the company who believe in it, understand it, and help their colleagues use it. Or it depends on you, the adoption owner, answering every question, clearing every obstacle, and keeping the enthusiasm alive by sheer personal energy.

The second version does not scale. It also does not survive, because the moment you are stretched thin or reassigned, the program runs on nothing. This lesson is about building the alternative: a deliberate, supported group of champions and power users who carry adoption forward without you in the room.

## Why peer support beats authority for sustaining adoption

People do not adopt a new way of working because a leader tells them to. They adopt it because someone they trust, someone who does the same job they do, shows them it works. That is the quiet engine of all lasting adoption, and it is why a support network is not a nice-to-have. It is the delivery mechanism for the change itself.

There is a sharp version of this truth waiting in the research. John Kotter, whose change framework underpinned Lesson 6, has reported that more than 70 percent of needed organizational change either fails to launch or fails to complete.[^1] A McKinsey global survey reached the same place from the data: less than one-third of respondents said their company's transformation had succeeded at both improving performance and sustaining those improvements over time.[^1] And crucially, the value loss does not stop when the project ships. McKinsey found that 20 percent of value loss occurs after implementation, after the initiatives are fully executed.[^1]

Read that last number carefully. A fifth of the value is lost not during the build or the launch, but afterwards, in the period where the program needs to become routine. That is exactly the window a peer support network operates in. McKinsey found that successful transformations were more likely to have embedded the new discipline into business-as-usual, into planning, review, and performance conversations.[^1] A champion network is one of the most practical ways to embed AI into the daily rhythm of how work actually happens.

## Defining the roles

The names get used loosely, so the definitions matter before you start building anything.

A **champion** is a peer who acts as an advocate for the change and supports the people around them in adopting it. They are not necessarily the most technically skilled. Their value is that they are trusted, they are approachable, and they model the behavior in their own work. A champion answers the "is this actually worth it" question by example, and helps a skeptical or hesitant colleague take the first step.[^2]

A **power user** (or super user) is a person with a deep, practical command of a specific tool or workflow, who is used as a resource to build, test, train, and embed the new system into the way work gets done.[^2] Where a champion's strength is influence, a power user's strength is expertise: they know the tool deeply, they know the business process it serves, and they can troubleshoot the problems a normal user hits.

There is a useful distinction in the consulting literature about how these roles relate to a project. A change agent belongs to the project, and the role ends when the project goes live. A super user belongs to the system, not the project, and the role lasts for as long as the system is in use.[^2] That is the whole thesis of this lesson in one line: the people who carry adoption are tied to the way of working, not to the launch. They are "for life, not just for go-live," in the words of super-user authority Jayne Mather.[^2]

You also want to keep clear that a support role and a governance role are different. A power user may hold extra permissions to administer data, but governing and setting policy is the work of later lessons in this course. For now, think of your network as the human layer that makes adoption feel easy, not the layer that writes the rules.

## How selection makes or breaks the network

The single biggest mistake is choosing champions for enthusiasm alone. The loudest volunteer is not always the most influential person in their department, and influence is the trait that actually moves adoption. The technical guidance is consistent about what to look for:

- **Representation across the whole organization, not just the loudest teams.** A network that covers only the departments that were already excited leaves the hesitant departments with nobody trusted to turn to.[^2]
- **Communication and leadership ability, adaptability, and a learning mindset.** You want people who can explain, not just people who can do.[^2]
- **Existing influence and team-building skill.** The person whose colleagues already come to them for help, formally or informally, will convert more adoption than the person who is simply enthusiastic.[^2]
- **Bandwidth.** A champion who has no time to actually help becomes a champion in name only, and their department quietly gets no support.[^2]

A practical rule of thumb from champion-network guidance is one to three champions per department, with larger departments getting more, and not so many that the network becomes unmanageable.[^2] Select too few and you have single points of failure. Select too many and coordination, training, and consistency collapse.

There is also a staffing question worth deciding explicitly: do your champions support this, and are they recognized for it, or is it an unpaid burden dropped on top of their real job? In formal super-user models the people are either seconded to the project with their day job backfilled, or they are given protected time to support their peers.[^2] Whatever you choose, say it out loud. A support role with no time and no recognition will not survive past the first month.

## Structuring the network so it runs without you

An informal network that is never actually structured tends to dissolve. The counter is a small, regular rhythm, not a heavy bureaucracy. Three structures do most of the work:[^2]

**A regular gathering.** A recurring meeting, monthly is common, where champions get updates, learn the latest capabilities, ask questions, and hear from leadership. This is the place the network stays trained and stays connected.[^2]

**A defined support role between meetings.** Between gatherings, champions answer their colleagues' questions, troubleshoot, and act as the bridge between the employee base and whoever runs the program. Their value is that they are a peer, so the question that feels too basic for a formal channel gets asked and answered safely.[^2]

**An ongoing, post-launch purpose.** The network does not dissolve at go-live. After implementation, champions escalate unresolved issues, share quick wins and early successes, monitor engagement and usage, encourage people to use the job aids, and feed continuous-improvement ideas back up.[^2] This is the structure that keeps the 20 percent of value from leaking out after the project ships.

## Bringing it back to your adoption metrics

In Lesson 3 you built the metrics, and in Lesson 5 you set the north-star and in Lesson 7 you read the dip. The support network is the human machinery that moves those numbers. Champions feed it in a direct way: they are the reason the usage metric climbs without a launch event behind it, and they are the reason churn stays low, because every colleague who can ask a trusted peer a question without friction is a user who does not fall back to the old way.

There is a feedback loop worth naming explicitly. The network produces wins, you surface those wins (the Lesson 6 and 7 work), the wins attract more users, and more users create more evidence for the champions to point to. Champions are the flywheel, and your metrics are the gauge that tells you whether the flywheel is actually turning. If reach is static in a department despite a champion being named, the champion is not working, and the fix is usually selection or bandwidth, not more enthusiasm.

## Worked example: Priya builds the network that survives her

Priya is the director of operations at a regional health-care staffing agency with about 300 people. She owns adoption of an AI tool that drafts shift-scheduling and credential-verification notes, a workflow that touches every coordinator in the company. She set a north-star in Lesson 5: the share of scheduling notes started from an AI draft, with a target of 75 percent in two quarters.

The first six weeks go well. The novelty push from Lesson 7 carries usage up, coordinators try the tool, and Priya sees the spike she expected. But she knows the spike is not the program, and she knows she cannot personally answer every question from three hundred people. So she builds the network before the dip arrives.

She starts with selection. Instead of taking the three loudest volunteers, she maps the org chart and picks coordinators from each staffing desk: the hospital-desk lead, who is known informally as the person everyone asks about scheduling; a newer coordinator from the nursing-home desk who happens to be a fast and patient explainer; the training specialist who already owns the onboarding checklist; and the data coordinator, who handles the credential-verification logs. That gives her four champions, each covering a distinct area, plus natural representation across shifts because the desks genuinely differ. She checks bandwidth with each manager first, so none of the four is signing up for a burden they cannot carry.

Then she lays out the three structures. A monthly champion circle, an hour, where she demos the newest capability and the champions share what they are seeing on their desks. A standing agreement that questions flow to the champions first, not to her. And a defined post-launch job: each champion watches for the repeated friction on their desk, flags it, and shares the wins so they spread organically.

The network pays off at the moment Priya expected it to hurt. In week seven, usage dips, exactly as Lesson 7 predicts. But the dip bottoms out higher than it would have, because the champions absorb questions in real time. The newer coordinator on the nursing-home desk finds that a specific phrasing gets the AI draft accepted almost every time, and she writes it up for her desk. That one embed, done by a peer the coordinators already trust, keeps that desk from sliding back to manual.

By the end of the quarter the north-star clears 75 percent and keeps climbing. The reason is not that Priya pushed harder. It is that she built a network of people tied to the way of working, not to the launch, and gave them the time, the structure, and the recognition to carry it. When Priya is later pulled onto another project for six weeks, the number does not collapse. The champions keep playing their rhythm, because the support role belongs to them, not to her.

## Try it: draft your support network on paper

Take the adoption effort you are running or about to run, and answer five questions in writing. You do not need anyone's approval to draft this, but the answers should be specific enough that you could act on them tomorrow.

First, your selection map. Draw your org chart and mark the people who genuinely hold influence in each area, not the loudest or the most excited. Who does everyone already go to for help? Those are your candidate champions. Now check bandwidth with each manager before you assume they are available.

Second, your split of roles. Who on your map is a champion, whose strength is trust and influence, and who is a power user, whose strength is deep expertise in a specific workflow? You likely need both, and one person can hold both, but name the primary value each person brings so the role stays clear.

Third, your representation check. Which department or desk has nobody on the map? That silence is a gap, because the moment someone there hits a friction point, they fall back to the old way with nobody trusted to stop them.

Fourth, your structure. What is the regular gathering, monthly or biweekly, and what does a champion actually do between meetings? If you cannot name the rhythm, the network will dissolve when you get busy.

Fifth, your post-launch job. What specifically do your champions do after go-live to stop the value from leaking: escalate issues, surface wins, monitor usage, feed improvements up? Write the one recurring duty each one owns, because a support role with no ongoing job is a launch artifact, and launch artifacts end at launch.
