Your policy is drafted. Your data-safety rules are written. Your vendor rubric is scored. And none of that matters yet, because the tools people actually use did not wait for any of it. They signed up with personal accounts, pasted real data in, and never told you, because nobody asked and the default answer would have been no.

This is shadow AI, and it is the reason a governance program that only reacts is a program that is already behind. [^1] By the time a new AI tool shows up in your budget or your help desk tickets, your people have been using it for weeks. The discipline you built in the previous lessons only earns its keep if you can see what is actually running in your organization. This lesson gives you the seeing.

## The words you need

**Shadow IT** is any technology an employee or team uses without the knowledge, approval, or governance of the organization's IT or security function. The term is older than AI: personal file-sharing accounts, unauthorized SaaS signups, and unofficial cloud storage are classic shadow IT. The defining feature is not that the tool is bad. It is that nobody responsible for it has seen it, vetted it, or agreed to carry its risk.

**Shadow AI** is shadow IT in the specific form of AI tools, models, and services used without organizational approval. It matters more than classic shadow IT because AI does not just store your data, it processes it, and in many cases retains it, trains on it, or sends it to a model provider you never reviewed. A document sitting in an unauthorized folder is a leak waiting to be found. Text pasted into an unapproved AI tool is already outside your perimeter the moment it is sent. [^2]

**Sanctioned** means approved by your process. A tool is sanctioned when your organization has evaluated it, agreed to its data handling, and told people it is an official option, usually through your acceptable use policy. **Unsanctioned** means everything else: no evaluation, no agreement, no policy coverage. The word carries no moral judgment. It is a governance status, and it is the single most useful word in this lesson.

**Acceptable use policy** is the written rule for what employees may and may not do with company technology and data. Your AI policy from earlier in the course is the AI-specific extension of it. Shadow AI thrives in the gap between what the policy covers and what people actually reach for, so the policy and the discovery loop have to be updated together, not one after the other.

**Data exfiltration** is the movement of data out of your control, whether the person moving it intends harm or not. For shadow AI the exfiltration channel is mundane: copy, paste, upload, a browser extension reading the page. The data leaves through a path your security controls never see.

**Discovery tooling** is the set of signals you use to see unsanctioned usage: expense and procurement records, the log of who granted access to which third-party app through your identity provider, network and DNS traffic to known AI services, browser extensions on managed devices, and plain employee surveys. No single signal is complete. You combine several, because shadow AI moves through many channels at once.

## Why banning makes it worse

The instinct when you discover unsanctioned AI is to block it. Turn it off, lock it down, tell people no. The research says the instinct backfires. [^3]

When UpGuard surveyed 500 security leaders and 1,000 employees, it found that 45% of workers simply find a workaround when an application is blocked. [^1] You do not eliminate the usage by prohibiting it. You push it further underground, past the few signals you had, and you lose the visibility that was your only real defense. The blocked tool comes back as a personal account on a personal device, invisible to your network entirely.

The same study found the scale of the problem: 81% of employees and 88% of security leaders themselves reported using unapproved AI tools. [^1] Security leadership is not exempt. If the people in charge of defense are part of the shadow, then the impulse to treat this as a discipline problem, as bad employees to be caught, is wrong on the evidence.

Gartner's November 2025 analysis is blunter about the consequence. A survey of 302 cybersecurity leaders found that 69% of organizations suspect or have evidence that employees are using prohibited public generative AI, and Gartner predicts that by 2030 more than 40% of enterprises will have experienced a security or compliance incident linked to unauthorized shadow AI. [^4] This is not a fringe concern. It is the default state, and it is forecast to become more costly, not less.

So the working assumption is not "our people follow the rules." It is "our people use AI, and we do not yet know all of it, and finding it is our job, not their crime."

## The governance loop: discover, triage, govern, repeat

Shadow AI is not a thing you fix once. It is a condition that recurs, because new tools launch constantly and employees adopt them faster than any review cycle. Treat it as a loop that never stops, and give it a schedule.

![The governance loop: discover builds the inventory constantly, triage rates each tool by what data it handles and whether that data may leave, govern routes each tool into approve, restrict, or replace, and repeat runs the loop on a cadence tied to the AI council](</diagrams/shadow-it-discovering-and-governing-unsanctioned-ai/1-governance-loop.png>)

**Discover.** Build the inventory constantly. Pull your expense and procurement records for AI subscriptions people billed through. Check your identity provider's logs for which employees and teams have authorized which third-party applications, because a huge share of shadow AI arrives as an OAuth grant the user clicked without reading. [^5] Review network and DNS traffic to known AI services, and look at browser extensions on managed devices. Ask. A short, anonymous, repeatable survey of what tools people actually use, and why, gets you the tool names no log will show, because many people use their personal account on their personal phone.

**Triage.** Rate every discovered tool the way your vendor rubric taught you, but faster and lighter. The question is not whether the tool is good. It is what data it handles and whether that data is allowed to leave. A translation extension that reads the page you are on is different from a coding copilot that ingests your source code, and both are different from a marketing writing assistant that only touches drafts. Your data-safety rules from earlier in the course are the triage scale: what category of data does this tool see, and is that category permitted to go to a model provider you did not vet?

**Govern.** Route each triaged tool into exactly one of three states, and make the states visible. Approve the tools that are safe and useful, and name them in your acceptable use policy so people no longer have to guess. Restrict the tools that are useful but risky, by defining what data they may see, and enforce it. Replace the dangerous ones by providing a sanctioned alternative that actually meets the unmet need, because a need does not disappear when you block the tool that was meeting it. [^6] If your only answer to "we need a shorter way to draft a client email" is "no," the employee will find the workaround anyway. Give them a safe path and most will take it.

**Repeat.** Run the loop on a cadence, and tie it to your AI council from later in the course. Shadow AI is a moving target, so the inventory must be refreshed and the policy must be updated from what the refresh finds.

NIST's AI Risk Management Framework gives this the shape professionals recognize: GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE, and MANAGE, four functions you cycle through rather than complete once. [^7] Discovery is your MAP and MEASURE. Triage and governance are your MANAGE. And none of it works without a governing body that holds the loop, which is your council and your policy owner. Note that NIST's AI RMF 1.0 is itself being revised as part of the White House AI Action Plan, so treat the framework's structure as stable and check for the current version of the standard before your program depends on a specific clause. [^7]

## Worked example: a mid-size firm finds its shadow

Let me run the loop on a concrete case. A 240-person services firm has an AI policy, a data-safety rule that forbids client data in unapproved tools, and a security team that believes the policy is working. It is not.

Last quarter a marketing coordinator signed up for a writing assistant on a personal account and pasted client proposal text into it, because the coordinator was behind on a deadline and the tool was one click away. Meanwhile the engineering team, 12 people, installed a coding copilot extension on their managed machines through a self-service grant the identity provider logged but nobody reviewed. And the finance team started running weekly forecasts through an AI spreadsheet tool, also unsanctioned, also fed with real budget numbers.

The first trigger was not a security alert. It was procurement, when five separate reimbursement requests for three different AI subscriptions landed in the same month. That got the security lead to run the discovery signals at once: identity grants reviewed, network logs to known AI services checked, a five-question anonymous survey sent to staff.

The inventory came back with 17 distinct AI tools in active weekly use, only four of which were sanctioned. Triage sorted them fast:

![Worked example triage: 17 tools in active weekly use, 4 sanctioned, 13 unsanctioned and triaged; the marketing writing assistant is restricted, the coding copilot is approved, and the finance spreadsheet tool is replaced](</diagrams/shadow-it-discovering-and-governing-unsanctioned-ai/3-worked-example-triage.png>)

- The marketing writing assistant handled drafts and some client-facing copy. Client copy is sensitive. It became a restricted tool: allowed for drafts, blocked from client data, replaced by a sanctioned alternative that the vendor evaluation had already approved for exactly this use.
- The coding copilot ingested source code, which for a services firm is the crown jewels. It was the highest-risk find. It was evaluated against the vendor rubric, and once its data-handling answer passed, it was approved and moved into the acceptable use policy. Until then, engineering was told in plain terms what to stop pasting in.
- The finance spreadsheet tool used real budget data. Finance is regulated-adjacent and its numbers feed client billing. It was pulled from use until the vendor answered where the data went, and the people who needed it got a temporary sanctioned path so the work did not stall.

The outcome was not a round of punishments. It was an inventory, three clear governance decisions, one replaced tool, and a slower, more honest process. And the firm scheduled the same review next quarter, because it knows the next writing assistant is already being signed up somewhere by someone who has not asked yet.

## Try it: find the shadow AI in your organization

Run the discovery loop on your own organization this week, and I do not mean a meeting about it. Produce the actual inventory.

Start with the four cheapest signals. Pull the last three months of procurements and reimbursements for anything that looks like a subscription to an AI service. Open your identity provider's list of third-party apps your staff have authorized, and count the ones nobody in IT recognizes. Ask your security or IT team what their network logs and managed-device browser extensions show about traffic to known AI tools. And send a short, anonymous survey to your staff asking three questions: which AI tools do you use for work, which of those are provided by the company, and what task are you trying to get done.

Then triage what you find. For each tool, ask two questions: what does it process, and is that data allowed to leave. Sort into three buckets: approve, restrict, replace. Name one tool in each bucket, and write the single governance decision that puts it there.

Finally, answer honestly about your own reflexes. When you found tools you did not approve, your first thought was either to block them or to judge the people using them. Both are the wrong move on the evidence, because blocking pushes usage underground and judgment ignores the fact that your staff were meeting real needs you had not met for them. [^3] The point of this exercise is not to catch anyone. It is to get to a place where the tools you govern are the tools people actually use, so that the policy and the inventory describe the same organization.

Write your inventory and your three decisions down. They are the raw material for the most important governance question your organization will face next: when the data flowing through these tools is governed by law, not just by your policy.

## What's next

You just built the inventory: what tools people use, what data they move, and which of it is approved, restricted, or replaced. That inventory is about to meet the law.

Lesson 21 is Privacy compliance: GDPR and CCPA and your AI use. Your shadow AI triage decided what data can leave your organization under your own policy. Next lesson introduces the layer above your policy: the regulations that decide what data may be collected, processed, and stored at all, and what your people must be told when it happens. GDPR and CCPA and the laws like them change the stakes of every inventory line you wrote in this lesson, because a tool that is fine under your policy can still violate a data subject's legal rights. Next lesson walks you through the privacy obligations a non-technical AI owner actually has to meet, and how to make your shadow AI governance loop run inside the law rather than beside it.

For now, hold the core idea: the tools are already there, prohibition makes them harder to see, and the answer is a repeatable loop of discovery, triage, and honest governance that keeps your inventory and your policy describing the same organization.
