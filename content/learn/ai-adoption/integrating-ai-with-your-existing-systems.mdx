Every AI program has a demo and a Monday. The demo runs on one clean file in a shared folder, and it looks like the future. Monday runs on the same question asked across four systems that disagree, by a person who does not have permission to see two of them.

The gap between those two days is not a model problem. It is an integration problem, and it is the reason a program that thrilled everyone in March is being quietly defended in September. This lesson is about closing that gap as the person who owns AI in the organization, which means you will not build connectors. You will decide which ones exist, who answers for them, and what they are allowed to touch.

## The evidence says the plumbing decides

The most quoted number in enterprise AI is also the most misread. MIT's Project NANDA report put enterprise generative AI investment at 30 to 40 billion dollars and found 95 percent of organizations getting zero return, with only 5 percent of integrated pilots extracting meaningful value. Read the finding rather than the headline: the report says the divide "does not seem to be driven by model quality or regulation, but seems to be determined by approach." Its plain-language version of the cause is that generic tools are widely used by individuals, while custom solutions stall "due to integration complexity and lack of fit with existing workflows."[^1]

That report is preliminary, it is not peer reviewed, and its percentages come from a modest sample of interviews and surveys. Treat it as a directional signal rather than a measurement. The direction is consistent with everything else in this space.

RAND reached a compatible conclusion from a different angle. It repeats the widely cited estimate that more than 80 percent of AI projects fail, twice the rate of non-AI IT projects, and its own interviews with 65 practitioners put inadequate infrastructure for managing and deploying models among the leading root causes, alongside a more basic one: teams optimizing a model for the wrong metric because nobody defined which business workflow it was supposed to sit inside.[^3]

The operational detail comes from people who sell integration software, so read it with that in mind. MuleSoft's 2026 Connectivity Benchmark, a survey of 1,050 IT leaders, finds the average organization managing 957 applications with only 27 percent of them connected to each other. Eighty-two percent of those leaders name data integration as one of the biggest challenges they face in using AI. Eighty-six percent agree that without proper integration, AI agents add more complexity than value. And IT teams report spending 36 percent of their time designing, building, and testing custom integrations between systems.[^2] That last figure is the one to bring to a budget conversation. A third of your technical capacity is already spent on the seams, before any AI is added to them.

The vendor incentive behind the survey does not change the arithmetic of your own estate. You can count your own systems and the connections between them in an afternoon, and you will find a similar shape.

## Six terms to settle first

**Integration surface.** Every place where AI can see your data or change your systems. The chat box is one. A connector into your CRM is another. So is the AI feature that arrived inside an accounting package nobody told you about. The surface is what you govern. The tool is only where it surfaces.

**Connector.** A pre-built path that lets an AI system read from or act on another system, such as your document store, your email, your CRM, or your ticketing queue. Underneath, every connector is an API call with a credential attached to it. When someone says the assistant "has access to SharePoint," they mean a credential exists that can read it.

**API.** The programmatic door into a system, distinct from the screen people use. Integration always goes through a door like this, whether or not anyone shows it to you. The practical question for an owner is never "is there an API," it is "who holds the key, and whose permissions does it carry."

**Retrieval (RAG).** The pattern where the AI fetches relevant passages from your content at question time and puts them in front of the model, instead of having "learned" your data. The original retrieval-augmented generation work described exactly this: a model combined with a retrieval index that supplies current documents.[^8] Two consequences follow for you. The index can be stale, and the index can contain things the person asking should not see.

**Read scope versus write scope.** Whether the connection can only fetch, or fetch and change. Nearly every frightening story about AI in an enterprise is a write-scope story wearing a read-scope costume in the retelling. Keep them in separate sentences whenever you brief anyone, because conflating them is how a reasonable pilot gets cancelled for the wrong reason.

**Source of truth.** The one system whose version of a fact wins when two systems disagree. Integration disputes are almost always source-of-truth disputes that were never settled. Deciding which system owns which field is unglamorous work, and it prevents more bad outputs than any prompt improvement will.

## The integration ladder: five rungs, five decisions

Integration is not a switch. It is a ladder, and each rung multiplies both the value and the blast radius. Move one rung at a time, per use case, never for the whole company at once.

Rung one is manual. A person copies text into an assistant and copies the answer back out. There is no connection to govern, which is why this rung is underrated for sensitive work: the human is the integration, and the human is also the control. The cost is that the data leaves the building through a person, so the rung is only acceptable where the content is low sensitivity or the person is trusted with it anyway.

Rung two is retrieval over managed content: policies, rate sheets, meeting notes, the wiki. The AI reads a curated corpus. Your decisions are which corpus, who curates it, and what is deliberately excluded. This rung produces most of the visible value in most programs, and it is where a surprising number of failed programs should have stopped.

Rung three is read access to live systems of record: the CRM, the ticketing queue, the order system. The answers get current, and two new questions appear. Whose permissions does the connection use? And how do you know the data you fetched was the right version?

Rung four is write access. The AI creates, updates, or sends. Now a wrong output is a business event, not a bad paragraph. Your decisions shift from content to consequence: what is the maximum damage from one wrong write, who approves before it lands, what is the cap per day, and how do you reverse it.

Rung five is embedded integration, where the AI lives inside the software where the work already happens and the vendor's terms decide how your data is used. This is the fastest spreading rung and the least visible, because it usually arrives as an upgrade rather than a purchase. Gartner expects 33 percent of enterprise software applications to include agentic AI by 2028, up from less than 1 percent in 2024, which means most of your future integration surface will be installed by your existing vendors, not chosen by you.[^7]

The ladder has one rule worth writing on a wall: a use case may climb one rung per quarter, and it must demonstrate value at the rung it currently occupies before it is allowed to climb. Programs that skip from rung two to rung four in a single enthusiasm cycle are the ones that produce an incident report instead of a case study.

## The gate: five questions before any connection is built

This is the artifact that makes you useful in a technical conversation you cannot win on detail. Ask these five, in this order, and record the answers.

What is the source of truth for this data, and is that the system we are connecting? If the answer is "it depends which field," the connection is not ready. You are about to build a fast path to a disagreement.

Read or write? If write, what is the worst realistic outcome of one wrong write, and who approves before it lands? Rank by reversibility, not by likelihood. A wrong internal summary is a wrong internal summary. A wrong rate quote to a customer is a commitment.

Whose permissions does the connection use? Two answers exist. Mirroring means the AI can only reach what the asking user could already open, which is the safer default and harder to build. A shared service credential is easier and usually over-permissioned, and it will eventually surface something to someone who should not have seen it.

What happens when this breaks, and how would we know within a day? Every connection breaks: tokens expire, schemas change, a vendor renames a field. The question is whether the assistant answers confidently from a stale index for three weeks while nobody notices. Ask for a named breakage signal, not a promise of uptime.

Who owns this row in twelve months? The owner is a person, not a team, and the row carries a review date. An unowned connection is integration debt with a dashboard.

Add a sixth question for anything that can act: what could this be tricked into doing by the content it reads? OWASP's entry on prompt injection is blunt about the mechanism. Indirect injection arrives through external content such as files or web pages, and among the listed impacts is executing arbitrary commands in connected systems. The recommended mitigation is not a better prompt. It is least privilege: give the application its own tokens, restrict the model's access to the minimum necessary, and handle sensitive functions in code rather than handing them to the model.[^6] Read scope plus human approval on writes is the owner-level version of that advice.

## The inventory: one row per connection

You cannot govern a surface you have not written down, and the inventory is the only artifact in this lesson that you will still be using in two years. One row per connection, nine columns, no exceptions.

- System: the thing being connected, in the name your staff uses for it
- Direction: read, write, or both
- Scope: which objects or folders, described narrowly enough that a new joiner understands the boundary
- Identity: mirrored user permissions or a shared service credential, named
- Data class: public, internal, confidential, regulated
- Frequency: real time, hourly, nightly, on demand
- Owner: one person
- Approved on: the date, and by whom
- Review on: a date in the next twelve months

Nine columns is deliberately small. An inventory that requires a data catalogue to fill in will not get filled in. A spreadsheet with nine columns gets finished on a Thursday afternoon, and a finished inventory is worth more than a thorough one nobody maintains.

Two columns do the quiet work. Identity tells you where your permission leaks will come from. Review date tells you which connections are drifting toward being nobody's job. Sort by review date once a quarter, open the top five rows, and ask whether each one still earns its place.

## Five failure modes the inventory will not catch

The inventory tells you what exists. These are the ways an inventoried, owned, approved connection still goes wrong.

A stale index answers with confidence. The connection works, the sync runs, and the content is four months old because the corpus stopped being curated when the person who built it changed teams. Fix it with a freshness expectation per corpus and a visible last-updated stamp in the answer. If the assistant cannot say when its source was last refreshed, you have a rung two problem disguised as a rung three success.

A permission leak hides behind a working demo. The demo used an executive's account, so everything worked. Test with a genuinely restricted user, then ask the assistant for something that user cannot open. Our own delivery pipeline taught us the same lesson in a different form: the failures we chase are almost never a stage producing bad prose, they are a stage receiving a truncated payload and cheerfully acting on it. The join between two systems is where the silent damage happens, and it does not announce itself.

A duplicate field lets the AI pick a side. Two systems hold a customer's credit limit and they disagree by eleven percent. The connection points at the one that syncs nightly from finance, and the other one is what sales has been reading for years. The AI is now confidently wrong in a new place. Name the system of record per field before the connection is built, and write it down.

Write amplification turns a small bug into a cleanup project. A loop in a workflow creates duplicate records faster than a person can notice, which is the entire point of automation and the entire risk of it. Caps, deduplication on a natural key, and an approval step for anything that leaves the building solve most of it.

Silent breakage produces a slow drift in trust. Nobody files a ticket, because nobody knows the answers stopped being current. They just stop asking, and adoption quietly falls in a way your usage dashboard will show you two months later. A canary question checked weekly by a named person costs four minutes and catches this class of failure before your users do. Standing rule: watch the answers, not the uptime.

## Worked example: Thea buys one connection and refuses another

Thea runs operations at a freight brokerage with 1,240 employees, 60 account managers, and a leadership team that has asked twice when the company is getting "the AI thing where you can just ask our systems anything."

She does not buy that. She measures first. Nine volunteer account managers keep a two-week time log of every lookup they make: a rate sheet, a customer's special terms, a shipment status, an SOP. The average is 38 minutes a day spent finding things that already exist somewhere. Across 60 people that is 38 working hours a day of retrieval, which explains the frustration better than any survey could.

Then she places her use cases on the ladder. Rate sheets and customer terms are rung two, since they live in documents that can be curated. Shipment status is rung three, a live system of record. Quoting a customer is rung four, because it writes a commercial commitment into the system.

Here is the honest version of what happens next, including the part most write-ups leave out.

The rung two connection takes 18 working days of a data engineer's time, internal cost 14,040 dollars, plus 340 dollars a month for a connector add-on that covers 100 users. Twelve account managers pilot it for two weeks. Three of them get no benefit at all in the first six days because their accounts sit in the wrong security group, which is the single most common way an integration pilot under-reports its own value.

Eleven minutes per person per day is the measured saving once permissions are right. Model it forward at 70 percent adoption across 60 people, which is 42 users, and you get 462 minutes a day, or 7.7 hours. At a loaded cost of 46 dollars an hour across 21 working days, that is roughly 7,400 dollars a month and 89,000 dollars a year. She then plans against 60 percent of that modelled number, 53,500 dollars, because she has watched two previous business cases overstate themselves by exactly this much.

First-year costs come to 20,200 dollars: the build, the platform fee, and maintenance at 15 percent of the build. The gap is about 33,300 dollars of defensible value, from one connection.

Then she tries rung three, and the test finds the thing that matters. She asks the assistant, as a restricted account manager, to summarize an account that user cannot open. The assistant does it, because the connector was configured with a shared service credential that reads the whole CRM. Nothing has leaked yet. Everything would have. The connector is rebuilt on mirrored permissions, which costs another four days, and the acceptance test becomes permanent: three questions the connection must answer correctly for a named restricted user, and one it must refuse. A connection that cannot refuse is not ready.

Rung four is refused for now, and the reasoning is the part worth copying. The worst realistic outcome of one wrong write is a rate quote committed to a customer, which is a commercial commitment, not an internal annoyance. She is not unwilling to get there. She is unwilling to get there before the read tier has three months of clean answers behind it. So quoting runs with eight users, approval before send, and a fifty-record daily cap for one quarter. If the approval rate stays above 95 percent and the error rate stays under 2 percent, the cap lifts. If not, the use case stays at rung three and someone saves time instead of the company taking risk.

The full first-year shape:

- Rung two, documents: approved, funded, 14,040 dollars build, 340 dollars a month
- Rung three, shipment status: rebuilt on mirrored permissions, no additional platform cost
- Rung four, quoting: approved as a capped pilot with a written exit condition
- Everything else in the ask-our-systems request: deferred with a date, not rejected with a shrug

## The order of work, and the budget line nobody writes

Most integration programs fail at prioritization, and they fail because the list is built from an architecture diagram instead of from the work. Rank candidate connections by three things, in this order.

First, reversibility of the worst likely failure. Read-only connections to curated content go first, almost always. Write connections to anything that leaves the company go last, without exception.

Second, size of the named population that benefits. A connection used by 60 account managers daily beats a connection used by three analysts brilliantly. Adoption is the multiplier on everything else, and narrow deep wins over wide shallow only when the depth is spectacular.

Third, whether it removes a step rather than adding one. The tell for a connection that will not get used is that it requires a person to copy something somewhere new in order to benefit. If the answer does not arrive where the work already happens, you have built a destination, not an integration.

Then budget the seams explicitly. A working number: integration work runs 25 to 40 percent of the value you expect from a use case, and it recurs every year as maintenance. Programs that budget the tool and forget the seam end up with a licence nobody can connect to anything, which is the most expensive form of shelfware because it renews.

MuleSoft's figures suggest why this line gets neglected: 27 percent of APIs in the survey are ungoverned, and 68 percent of leaders say they struggle to keep up with emerging standards for agents.[^2] The standards are genuinely moving. The Model Context Protocol, a common way to connect models to tools and data, was published in November 2024, passed 10,000 published servers within a year, and was donated to the Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation in December 2025 with Anthropic, Block, and OpenAI as founding contributors.[^4] Adoption in practice is slower than the announcement cycle suggests: Postman's 2025 survey of over 5,700 developers found 24.3 percent designing APIs with agents in mind, but only 10 percent using MCP regularly.[^5] So treat protocol news as a reason to keep your connections vendor-neutral, not as a reason to rebuild anything this quarter. The question to ask a vendor is whether their integration is a standard protocol with a documented data flow, or a proprietary path you cannot leave.

## What to hand IT in one page

You will not design the connection. You will write the request, and a good request is one page with four parts: the six gate answers, the named owner, the acceptance test (three correct answers for a restricted user, one correct refusal), and the breakage signal with the person who checks it. Add the review date at the bottom.

That page changes the conversation. It moves the discussion from whether AI is safe in general, which nobody can answer, to whether this connection, for this population, with this scope, is acceptable, which is a decision a competent engineer can make in a meeting.

## Try it: five days, one hour a day

Day one, list every system where a decision gets made in your organization, then mark the ones that hold the source of truth for money, for customers, and for commitments. Most companies find between eight and fifteen of those, which is a far smaller number than their application count and a far more useful one.

Day two, send one message to whoever runs IT: for each system on that short list, is there a supported API, who owns it, and does it enforce user-level permissions? The answer to the third question decides your architecture more than the first two.

Day three, take the top use case from this quarter's list and place it on the ladder. Write the rung number down. If you cannot name the rung, the use case is a wish.

Day four, fill in the six gate answers for that one use case, including the one question the connection must refuse. The refusal question will feel awkward to write and it is the most valuable sentence you will produce all week.

Day five, open the inventory with the rows you can already fill in, and put a review date on each. Then book thirty minutes in your calendar ninety days out to open the top five rows by that date. That half hour is the entire maintenance program for your integration surface.

## What's next

You now have the piece most programs skip: an honest map of where AI can reach into your systems, what it is allowed to touch there, who answers for each connection, and how you will notice when one goes quiet. That map is the raw material for the decision that comes next, because every connection you want to build costs money and time in a year that has finite room for both. Lesson 35 builds the annual AI roadmap against your organization's actual capability, which is a different document from the wish list most roadmaps turn out to be.
