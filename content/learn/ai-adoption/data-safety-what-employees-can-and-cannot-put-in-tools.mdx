Most data-safety conversations about AI start in the wrong place. They start with the tool: is this chatbot safe, is that assistant approved, which one can we trust. That framing misses the actual question. The thing that decides whether an AI use is safe is not the tool, it is the data you put into it. A tool is safe or unsafe relative to what you feed it. Paste a public product description into a free chatbot and nothing much happens. Paste an employment contract into the same chatbot and you have just moved a confidential document outside your organization, where you can no longer control what happens to it.

This lesson is about that difference. By the end you will be able to look at any daily work task and state, out loud, whether the data in it is safe to put into a tool, what needs de-identifying first, and what must never go in. You do not need to be a security engineer to do this. You need one clear mental model, a vocabulary for the data your org actually holds, and a decision routine simple enough for an employee to run in the five seconds before they paste.

## What happens to the data when you hit send

Here is the fact that changes everything, so state it plainly to your team: there is no such thing as an AI tool that runs entirely inside your head. When an employee pastes text or uploads a file into an AI assistant, that data leaves the company's systems and travels to the vendor's servers.[^1] What happens after that is governed by the vendor's policy, not yours, and it is rarely nothing.

At minimum, the input is processed on outside infrastructure you do not control. It may then be stored. It may be retained for a fixed period, often thirty days or more, even after the employee closes the chat window and deletes the conversation, because the vendor keeps a copy for safety or abuse monitoring.[^1] And it may be used to train the model itself. A Stanford HAI study of the privacy policies of six leading US AI developers, including Anthropic, Google, Meta, Microsoft, and OpenAI, found that all six use user conversations to train their models by default, with some keeping the data indefinitely, and that Microsoft collects data from children under 18.[^2] The study's lead author put it directly: if you share sensitive information in a dialogue with a frontier model, it may be collected and used for training, even if it is in a separate file you uploaded during the conversation.[^2]

None of this is an accusation aimed at any vendor. It is a description of the default behavior of consumer-grade tools, and it is why the first skill of data safety is not choosing a tool, it is deciding what the tool is allowed to see. The practical consequence is blunt: anything your team pastes into a public, consumer-grade AI tool may leave the company permanently, and "I deleted the chat" does not put it back.

## The real world cost: the Samsung example

This is not a theoretical risk, so here is a documented case. In March 2023, engineers at Samsung were using a popular chatbot to help with coding tasks. In doing so, they pasted in sensitive company source code and internal meeting notes. The chatbot retained the input, the data was exposed to the service, and Samsung identified at least three separate incidents of leaked corporate information before it banned employee use of generative AI tools entirely.[^3]

Read that example twice, because it is the failure mode your data-safety policy exists to prevent and it is not exotic. Three separate leaks from ordinary engineers helping themselves to a helpful tool, exactly the behavior that sounds harmless in the moment and is impossible to undo after. The engineers were not acting maliciously. They were doing what the tool asked (help fix code, help draft a note) and the data was gone the moment they pasted it. No amount of warning after the fact recovered it.

Your job as the adoption owner is to make "would this be safe if it left the building" the automatic thought before anyone pastes anything, so the Samsung moment does not happen on your watch.

## Define the terms: data classification, PII, and retention

To apply data safety, you need a shared vocabulary. Three terms carry the whole discussion.

**Data classification.** The practice of sorting the information your organization holds into levels by how much harm its exposure would cause. Most organizations use four levels, and they are worth memorizing because they become the common language between you, your team, and your security people.[^4]

- **Public.** Information that can be shared outside the organization without harm. Press releases, published product documentation, marketing materials. An AI prompt built from these is low risk.
- **Internal Only.** Information for use within the organization that is not highly sensitive but should not be public. Employee handbooks, internal policies, project documentation, standard operating procedures. Exposure would not cause severe harm but could reveal operational details or business priorities.
- **Confidential.** Information that could harm the organization, its employees, customers, partners, or shareholders if disclosed. Customer records, employee personal information, contracts, financial records, legal documents, partner agreements, sensitive business plans. This needs protection: encryption, access controls, restricted access to people with a business need.
- **Restricted.** The most sensitive category. Exposure could cause severe financial, legal, regulatory, operational, or reputational harm. Trade secrets, source code, intellectual property, board materials, active M&A documents, privileged legal communications, critical credentials, regulated personal information. This needs the strongest controls and the smallest access circle.

**PII (personally identifiable information).** Any information that can be used to distinguish or trace an individual's identity, either alone or combined with other data that is linked or linkable to that person. The US National Institute of Standards and Technology defines it to include name, social security number, date and place of birth, biometric records, and linked personal information such as medical, educational, financial, and employment records.[^5] PII is the category your compliance obligations care about most, because exposing it can trigger legal and regulatory consequences, not just embarrassment.

**Data retention.** How long the vendor keeps a copy of what you send it after processing. Retention is the reason "I deleted my chat" is not a control. The vendor may keep a copy for a fixed window (often thirty days) for safety or abuse monitoring even after you delete the conversation on your side.[^1] A zero-retention guarantee, where the provider deletes inputs and outputs in real time after processing, exists but is usually approval-gated and limited to specific eligible enterprise endpoints, not the default consumer experience most employees use.[^1]

## The decision routine: three questions before you paste

Now you have the vocabulary. Here is the routine that puts it to work, and it is the transferable skill of this lesson. Before anyone puts data into an AI tool, they answer three questions in order.

**Question one: what level is this data?** Name the classification. If the employee cannot confidently name it, that is the answer in itself, and it means slow down. Most of the "should I paste this" anxiety resolves the moment the person can say the actual level.

**Question two: is this confidential or restricted?** If yes, the default answer is no, it does not go into the tool, full stop, unless the approved path applies (an approved enterprise tool you have vetted and configured, which is the subject of lesson seventeen). Public and Internal Only data may go into an approved tool. Internal Only data should still be trimmed first: remove direct identifiers, cut anything that narrows the source, paste a short excerpt rather than a full export.

**Question three: which tool, and does it train on my data?** The same data can be safe in an approved, properly configured enterprise deployment and unsafe in a free public chatbot. The difference is what the vendor does with the input: whether the organization has a contract, whether training is turned off, whether retention is minimized. If you cannot answer "who signed what, and what does it do with my data," it is a public tool and it trains by default.[^2]

When the employee cannot answer question two or three, they default to treating the data as restricted and they stop. That default is the entire point of the routine. It converts a vague worry into a five-second decision, and it means a confidential document only leaves the building when someone has made the deliberate choice that the approved tool and the approved path make it safe.

## Why public and approved tools are not the same risk

The biggest mistake in an AI data-safety policy is treating "a chatbot" as one uniform risk and banning or permitting all of them together. The reality is a spectrum, and the spectrum is set by the vendor relationship, not the brand name.

A free, consumer-grade tool is the highest-risk case. You likely have no contract, the vendor trains on user conversations by default, retention runs on the consumer schedule, and the input crosses into an environment the organization does not control or audit.[^2]

An approved enterprise tool, properly configured, is a different case entirely. You have a contract, you can set the policy so your organizational data is not used to train the model, and you can limit retention. But notice what the word "approved" demands: it is not a label you grant by familiarity with a brand, it is a relationship you verify. Someone in your organization has to confirm the vendor's actual data-handling commitments, which means reading the thing that matters and comparing it to the data you will put in. That is the work of vendor evaluation, which is the next lesson, and it is exactly the reason a data-safety rule and a vendor-selection process belong together.

The practical instruction to your team follows from the distinction: put Internal Only and carefully trimmed data only into tools your organization has approved and configured for business use, and never paste Confidential or Restricted data into a consumer chatbot no matter how good it seems.

## Worked example: Malik sets the rule at a design agency

Malik owns AI at a mid-sized design agency that handles branding work for clients. The team has started using AI to draft concept notes, summarize briefs, and generate early layout ideas. Nobody has a rule, and Malik has watched people paste everything into a public chatbot, including one anxious afternoon when a copywriter pasted the full terms of a client NDA to ask the chatbot to "summarize the tricky clauses."

Malik starts with the four-level classification and teaches it in a thirty-minute team session, mapping each level to real things the agency holds. Public is their published portfolio and case studies. Internal Only is their internal process documents and meeting notes. Confidential is client contracts, pricing, briefs that name a client before launch, and internal financials. Restricted is the client NDA, unreleased campaign concepts, trade-secret production techniques, and any data that identifies an individual client contact.

Then he writes the rule as the three-question routine. Before anyone puts something into a tool, they name the level, check whether it is confidential or restricted, and check that the tool is on the approved list. He configures the agency's approved enterprise tool so the agency's data is not used to train the model, and he tells everyone they may use it for Internal Only work. He adds one hard line to the policy: no client NDA, contract, pricing, or unreleased campaign ever goes into any tool that is not on the approved list, and the approved list does not include consumer chatbots.

Two weeks later the copywriter is drafting a concept note from a trimmed brief. She pastes the concept direction (Internal Only, notes only) into the approved tool, but she first deletes the client's name and the launch date, pasting "the [client] retail launch this quarter" instead of the actual details. That is the habit Malik wanted: the person can do a real task, and the reducing-the-exposure step happens without anyone policing it, because it is now the obvious thing to do.

The test comes when a junior designer almost pastes a client's confidential performance data into the consumer chatbot to "find insight." The routine stops them: they name the level (confidential), the second question says no, and instead of pasting they strip the client identifiers and run only a de-identified summary through the approved tool. No data left the building that should not have. That is data safety working, and it cost nothing but a taught routine.

## Try it: run the routine on your own daily work

Pick one document you handled in the last two days and classify it under the four levels. Name its level, and then run the three-question routine on it honestly: could it go into your current approved tool, or is it confidential or restricted and therefore out? Then take the next step a data-safety owner actually takes. Sit down with your security person or IT lead and get two answers in writing: which of your tools are on the approved list and configured so your data does not train the model, and what each tool's retention policy actually is. If the tool you told your team they could use turns out to train on their input by default, you have found the gap this lesson is meant to close, and you now know exactly what to fix before it becomes a leak.

If you cannot get those two answers, that is not a failure of your effort. It is the reason lesson seventeen, vendor evaluation, exists, and it tells you the next tool you bring in should not be chosen on reputation alone.

## What's next

This lesson gave you the mental model for data safety: everything you put into a tool leaves the building, and the way to keep data safe is to classify it, protect confidential and restricted data, and give your team a five-second routine that runs before anyone pastes. But a data rule is only as good as the tools behind it, and the rule depends on knowing which tools are actually safe to use in the first place. The next lesson, lesson 17 of 40, is vendor evaluation: assessing AI tools before you purchase them. You will learn how to vet a vendor's data-handling commitments, compare retention and training policies, and make a defensible procurement decision so the approved list you build your policy on is one you can actually trust.
