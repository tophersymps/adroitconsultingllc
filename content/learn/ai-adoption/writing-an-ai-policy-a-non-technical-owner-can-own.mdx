When you are the person who owns AI in your organization, at some point someone hands you the policy question. It comes in a few forms. Legal sends over a twenty-page draft and asks for your sign-off. A manager asks what the team is actually allowed to paste into a tool. An employee confesses they have been using an AI assistant for months and wants to know if they broke something. None of those moments are clean, and most of them trace back to the same root: the organization does not have a policy that a non-technical owner can read, understand, update, and defend.

This lesson is built around that gap. You will learn what an AI acceptable use policy actually is, why most of them fail in practice, the five pillars that make one work, and a data rule simple enough that your team can remember it on a bad Tuesday. By the end you should be able to write a one-page policy for your own organization and know how to keep it alive as the tools change.

## What an AI acceptable use policy actually is

An AI acceptable use policy, usually shortened to AUP, is a set of rules that defines how employees may use generative AI tools and services on the job. The point is not to ban AI. The point is to say what is allowed, what is not, what data can go into the tools, and what happens when someone crosses a line. Tenable, which writes an unusually practical guide to these policies, defines an AUP as a formal set of rules governing employee use of generative AI tools and agents, built to balance innovation against protecting sensitive company data.[^1]

If that sounds close to a regular computer-use policy you already have, it is, with one important difference. An AI tool can copy, retain, and recombine whatever you feed it in ways that a spreadsheet cannot. A document you upload may be stored on the vendor's servers, used to train their model, or surfaced to another customer. So an AI policy has to do something your old IT policy never had to: it has to make decisions about what data the organization is willing to send to an outside system. That single shift is why you cannot just copy your existing policy and change a few words.

The scale of the problem is not theoretical. In 2025 the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis measured that 54.6% of US adults had used generative AI, including 37.4% who used it for work, within the previous twelve months.[^2] Adoption is no longer a niche experiment; it is the default for more than a third of the working population. Now pair that with the oversight figures. A 2025 EisnerAmper survey of US full-time desk workers who used AI at work found that only 36% said their company had a formal AI policy, only 41% told their manager or asked permission before they used AI, and 22% said their employer actively monitored their AI use at all. In the same survey, 84% of managers said they were aware of some AI use on their teams.[^3] Read that together and you get an organization where most people are using a powerful, data-hungry tool and almost nobody has a written rule, a named review, or even a monitoring glance to keep it safe. That is the gap this lesson closes.

## Why most AI policies fail

The typical failure is not that the policy is wrong. It is that the policy is unreadable, unusable, or unowned. Here are the three ways policies die.

First, legal writes a document in the register of defensive law. It is long, it is full of carve-outs, and it opens with "shall" and "whereas." Nobody reads it. The employees who are actually using AI every day never get a rule they can apply in the moment, so they fill the gap with their own judgment, which is how sensitive data ends up pasted into a free public tool because nobody told them not to.

Second, the policy is static. The AI tools your company uses change monthly. A policy written for the chatbot your company adopted last spring says nothing about the coding assistant that just appeared, the AI features inside your documents app, or the agent you are piloting this quarter. When the policy does not cover what people are actually using, it quietly stops meaning anything, and everyone knows it.

Third, and this is the one that matters most for you, the policy has no real owner. It lives in a folder, signed once, and forgotten. Nobody is tasked with keeping it current, nobody can answer the "is this allowed" question, and nothing happens when someone violates it. A policy without an owner and without consequences is not a policy; it is a hope.

Your job as the adoption owner is to be the person who fixes all three of those failures. That means you do not need to write legal-grade prose. You need to write a document that is short enough to read, specific enough to apply, current enough to matter, and attached to a person who will update it.

## The five pillars

Tenable's guide identifies five essential elements of a generative AI policy, and they translate directly to something a non-technical owner can write and maintain.[^1] They are your scaffold.

The first pillar is scope and roles. Say who the policy covers, which is typically all employees, contractors, and anyone else with access to company systems, and who does what. Name the person who owns the policy, the person employees ask when they are unsure, and who handles violations. When a question lands, the answer depends on knowing exactly who decides, and a policy that names that person saves a day of emails.

The second pillar is the approved and prohibited list. Keep a short list of AI tools the security and legal teams have vetted and approved for business use, and name the categories that are off limits, such as unapproved public tools for sensitive work or any use that is illegal or violates company standards. The list does not need to be long. It needs to be current, which is why the next pillar exists.

Pillar three, and the one this lesson spends the most time on, is data handling. This is where you answer the question your old IT policy never did: what data can and cannot go into an AI tool. You will write this as a rule in the next section.

Pillar four is the governance and change process. This is the mechanism that keeps the policy alive. Employees need to know they can request a new tool for review, and you need a defined path for how that request gets evaluated against data security, privacy, vendor stability, and data residency. Without this, people either hide their tools or adopt them in the open and the policy falls behind reality.

Pillar five is acknowledgment and enforcement. People should confirm they have read the policy, ideally with a short training and a sign-off at least annually, and the policy should state plainly what happens when a line is crossed. This is not about punishment. It is about making the policy real enough that a violation is identified and addressed rather than silently absorbed.

## The data rule your team can remember

The hardest part of a data policy is that employees cannot apply a rule they cannot hold in their head while they work. So steal the three-tier framework security vendors like Zscaler use and compress it into a color rule.[^4] Red data never goes into a public or unapproved AI tool. Yellow data may go in, but only with safeguards. Green data is fine under normal policy.

Red is anything that would cause real damage if it left the building: credentials and secrets, regulated personal information, customer records, employee personal data, internal financials, legal and business-sensitive material, security details, and source code. This category is the non-negotiable. No document that contains it goes into an unvetted tool, period.

Yellow is internal material that is not secret but is not public either. Project summaries, internal meeting notes, drafts, and non-sensitive process documents. This data may go into an approved tool when the employee first reduces the risk: remove names, account numbers, and other direct identifiers, cut anything that could narrow the source, and paste a short excerpt instead of a full export. That act of de-identification is the safeguard.

Green is low-risk work any employee can do normally: brainstorming, drafting generic content that does not need confidential inputs, summarizing non-sensitive notes, translating non-sensitive material. This keeps the policy from being a wall. There is a large set of tasks where AI genuinely helps and no data risk exists, and the policy should say clearly that those are fine so people do not feel like every use is a test.

The color rule is memorable, which is the point. An employee about to paste a block of text into a tool can ask one question: which color is this? If they cannot answer confidently, they default to treating it as red and stop. That one question does more than a twenty-page document ever will.

## A worked example: Nora writes her policy

Nora runs operations for a regional logistics firm of about 140 people. She owns the AI adoption program and nobody has given her a policy yet, so she decides to write one. She has no legal training and no security team beyond one part-time IT person, so she keeps it to one page.

She opens with scope and roles. "This policy covers everyone with company accounts, including contractors. It applies to AI tools used for any company work, on any device. The owner of this policy is Nora. If you are unsure whether something is allowed, ask Nora or file a request in the #ai-tools channel. First violation is a conversation; repeated violations go to your manager." That single paragraph answers the who and the what and the escalation path.

She writes her approved list. Three tools have been vetted, and she names them. Then she adds the sentence that carries the most weight: "Anything not on this list is not approved for company work until you request a review." That closes the shadow-IT door without banning experimentation, because pillar four lets anyone bring a tool forward.

Her data rule is the color rule. "Red never goes in. Yellow goes in only after you remove names and identifying details and paste a short excerpt. Green is fine. When in doubt, treat it as red." She includes the concrete red examples: credentials, customer records, payroll, contracts, internal pricing, source code. She does not try to list every sensitive document type; she lists the ones her team actually touches.

Finally she writes the review rhythm. "This policy is reviewed the first week of each quarter. New tools can be submitted any time through the same channel." That turns the document from a static file into a living one, and it gives her the calendar slot to keep the approved list honest.

She runs the draft past the IT person to check the approved-list details, then past legal once to confirm she has not said anything dangerous, then she publishes it, records a two-minute walkthrough, and requires a sign-off from every employee in the quarterly adoption conversation she already runs. The whole thing took her an afternoon, and she now has something she can defend in a leadership meeting because she can say exactly what is allowed, what is not, and who owns it.

## What a non-technical owner should actually do

The worked example is the shape of the work, but the transferable habits are these. Keep it to one page and write in the language people speak at work, not the language of legal filings. Anchor the whole thing to your data classification, which is the shared language of what your organization is willing to expose (the common four-level model is Public, Internal Only, Confidential, and Restricted).[^5] Then name a person, not a committee, as the owner of the document. A committee can advise, but the moment someone asks "is this allowed," there has to be one person who answers.

Make the approval path visible and cheap. The #ai-tools channel, a form, a one-question email to a named owner; the lower the friction, the more likely people use the official path instead of hiding. Build a review rhythm into your calendar rather than waiting for an incident. And be careful not to confuse a real policy with a décor one. A policy that nobody signed, nobody can cite, and nobody updates is decoration, and it gives leadership a false sense that the governance problem is solved.

One caution worth stating plainly. A policy you write overnight does not replace the professionals who should sanity-check it. The color rule and the five pillars are governance hygiene you can own, but the moment your org handles regulated personal data or serious contracts, the final review has to pass through the people who know the compliance obligations of your industry. Your job is to make the policy readable and owned; their job is to make sure it is not dangerous.

## Try it

Pick a real part of your own organization and draft a one-page AI acceptable use policy using the five pillars. Do not research for an hour; use what you already know about your tools and your team. Write the scope, an approved list of the tools you actually use, the color data rule, a short change process, and a one-line consequence. Then test it. Give it to one non-technical coworker and ask two questions: can you tell me what is allowed, and what would you do right now if a tool you wanted was not on the list? Where they hesitate, your policy is thin, so fix those gaps before you take it further.
