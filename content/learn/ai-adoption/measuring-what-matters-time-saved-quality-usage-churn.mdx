# Lesson 3: Measuring What Matters: Time Saved, Quality, Usage, and Churn

In Lesson 2 you judged where AI has a real chance of improving work, and you picked a pilot. This lesson answers the question you need before you invest another dollar: how do you know it is actually working? The honest answer in most organizations is that nobody knows, because almost nobody measures.

The gap is not a small one. McKinsey's 2025 global survey found that only 39 percent of organizations attributed any financial impact to AI, and most of that minority said the impact was under five percent of earnings. [^3] Yet nearly everyone is using the technology. That is the same picture Lesson 2 drew, near-universal use, rare value, and here is the finding that should shape your whole approach: of the twelve adoption and scaling practices McKinsey measured, the one with the most impact on financial results was tracking well-defined KPIs for AI solutions. [^3] Measuring is not the administrative tail end of the program. It is the highest-leverage practice you have. An organization that defines what matters and checks it is already beating most of its competitors whose dashboards are empty.

This lesson gives you the measurement framework: four metrics that together tell you whether a use case is real. They are time saved, quality, usage, and churn. You will learn to define each one, to tell the outcome metrics from the activity metrics, to avoid the trap of measuring speed without quality, and to read the four together instead of chasing any one of them. By the end you will be able to turn the pilot you picked in Lesson 2 into something you can actually prove.

## First, the words we are using

A **metric** is a number you define and track that tells you something about whether a use case is working. The word is easy; the discipline is not. A metric only does its job if you decide, before you collect anything, what it means and what a good result looks like. Leave that vague and you will be surprised at how a number can mean whatever you want it to.

Two of the four metrics are **outcome metrics**. They ask whether the work produced more value. **Time saved** measures whether the same output takes less effort than before. **Quality** measures whether the output is as good as or better than what one person produced without the tool. These two are the ones that connect to the cost and revenue columns from Lesson 2.

Two are **activity metrics**. They ask whether anyone is actually using the thing. **Usage** measures how many eligible people use the tool and how often. **Churn** measures the reverse, who has quietly stopped, and it is the earliest warning that a tool is not earning its place.

Hold the split in mind, because it is the whole lesson in miniature. Activity metrics tell you people are moving. Outcome metrics tell you whether that movement produced value. You can light up an activity dashboard with zero outcome to show for it, and you can have genuine outcomes that a handful of people never scale. The adoption owner reads all four, and refuses to celebrate any of them alone.

## Time saved: measure the work, not the feeling

Time saved is the metric people reach for first, and it is the one easiest to get wrong. The most reliable evidence that AI saves real time comes from a controlled experiment. Researchers at GitHub split 95 professional developers into two random groups and timed both as they wrote an HTTP server in JavaScript. The group using the AI pair programmer finished the task 55 percent faster, about one hour and eleven minutes versus two hours and forty-one minutes, and it had a higher completion rate, 78 percent versus 70 percent. [^1] That is a real, observed speed gain, not a guess.

Now read that result honestly, because it is both less and more than it looks. It is less than it looks because it measured one task, in one language, in a controlled setting, on a problem chosen because it fit the tool's strengths. A 55 percent gain on that task does not generalize to every hour of every developer's week. It is more than it looks because it shows why a controlled baseline matters: the only reason we trust the number is that the study measured the same task with and without the tool, so the gain is attributable to the tool and not to luck or a faster Monday.

That is exactly what your organization will not have, which is why most time-saved claims deserve skepticism. Self-reported time savings are unreliable. People are bad at estimating how long their tasks take, and someone who is enthusiastic about a new tool will tell you it makes them faster whether or not it does. GitHub's own productivity framework is explicit that an individual's sense of productivity has as much to do with satisfaction and a good day as with speed. [^1] If your only evidence of time saved is a survey where people say they feel faster, you are measuring a feeling, and feelings are not a P&L line.

Measure time saved at the level of the work instead. Pick a defined task, one that produces a defined output, and time it before the pilot and again after, using the same measurement each time. Or count throughput over a fixed window, how many of those outputs the team produces in a month. Or sample: watch a handful of workers for an hour and record where their time actually goes. Any of these beats a self-report, because it gives you a before-and-after you can attribute.

![Self-reported time savings are a feeling that tracks enthusiasm and mood, not a P and L line; measure the work itself with a controlled before and after, throughput, or time sampling, and trust numbers that come from a controlled baseline.](</diagrams/measuring-what-matters-time-saved-quality-usage-churn/2-measure-work-not-feeling.png>)

There is a second subtlety McKinsey's survey surfaces. Even when time is genuinely saved, the value depends on what the time becomes. Respondents most often said employees spent the saved time on entirely new work or on more of their existing work; only at larger organizations did the survey find frequent headcount reduction as a result. [^3] Time saved is not automatically value. Reading that allocation is part of the adoption owner's job, because an hour saved and then spent scrolling is not a financial result, and an hour saved and spent on a second product line might be.

## Quality: the speed trap

Time saved is dangerous on its own, because speeding up the wrong work produces confident error faster. The cleanest evidence of this comes from a field experiment run with Boston Consulting Group consultants, who were scored on realistic knowledge tasks. For tasks inside a range the researchers called the jagged technological frontier, tasks where the AI was genuinely capable, people using it completed about 12 percent more tasks, did the work about 25 percent faster, and produced results that were roughly 40 percent higher quality than the control group. [^2] Same tool, same people, and the work got better.

Outside that frontier, the same tool did damage. On a task beyond the AI's competence, participants using it were about 19 percent less likely to arrive at the correct solution than those without it, even though they finished more quickly. [^2] The consultants were faster to the wrong answer. That is the quality trap in one sentence: measure speed only, and you will optimize for getting to bad outcomes sooner.

![Inside the jagged technological frontier the tool raises throughput, speed, and quality, while outside the frontier it finishes faster but lands on the wrong answer; measuring speed alone optimizes for reaching bad outcomes sooner.](</diagrams/measuring-what-matters-time-saved-quality-usage-churn/3-quality-speed-trap.png>)

Quality is hard to measure because it lives in the work, not in the tool. It has two layers, and you want both. **Output quality** is how good the produced artifact is, judged by the same standard a good reviewer would use. **Error containment** is how often mistakes are caught before they reach anything that matters. Lesson 2 named the cheap-check test: the best AI use cases are ones where a person can tell, quickly, whether the output is right. Quality measurement leans on that. The question is not only whether the AI output is good, it is whether every piece of output is checked and whether anything wrong survives the check.

A concrete quality metric is the **acceptance rate**: the share of AI suggestions a person actually keeps and uses. GitHub's own guidance on its usage dashboard reads a rising acceptance rate as growing trust and usefulness, and a drop as mismatched suggestions or workflow friction. [^4] Acceptance rate is a warning system, not a trophy. A rate climbing because people rubber-stamp everything is quality theater, which brings us back to error containment: someone has to verify, and the verification has to be real.

## Usage: does anyone actually stay on it

Usage is the activity metric everyone can see, and it is meaningful only because its absence is fatal. A use case with nobody using it produces nothing. So you track how many eligible people actually use the tool and how often: active users against the eligible population, the daily, weekly, and monthly active counts, how frequently people come back, and which features they actually touch.

The important discipline is to count people, not clicks. A tool is adopted when a meaningful share of the people who could use it, do use it, and come back on a rhythm. GitHub's enterprise dashboard, for example, tracks active users and flags a drop in daily active users as a signal worth acting on, possibly a configuration issue or reduced relevance of what the tool suggests. [^4] A usage drop is an early sign that something in the work-solution fit has broken.

But here is where the activity-outcome split bites. High usage is not proof of value. A heavily used tool can burn hours on problems that do not matter, or produce output nobody checks. Usage answers "are people showing up?" and it is silent on "is showing up worth anything?" Keep the two questions separate, and do not present usage charts as if they were the business case.

## Churn: the quiet signal

Churn is usage wearing its other face. It is the share of people who were using the tool and stopped. Where usage tells you adoption reached some people, churn tells you whether it held. It matters because software adoption is not a one-time event: everyone who starts using a tool is on a clock, and a tool that stops feeling worth it gets abandoned.

The signal you care about most is **quiet churn**, the people who stop without telling anyone. They do not file a complaint. They just stop opening the tool, and they drift back to the old way of working, or they quietly buy their own subscription and run their own shadow version. That rebound is not just lost value from your pilot. It is the beginning of the shadow-IT problem this course will deal with directly in a later lesson, tools running outside your governance because the approved one stopped earning its place.

Read churn the way a doctor reads a fever, as a symptom, not the disease. When you see usage slide, the question is not "how do we make them open it again?" It is "what made this tool no longer worth it?" Sometimes the answer is training, sometimes the answer is that the tool is genuinely not good at this task, and sometimes, and this is the hardest outcome to accept, the honest answer is that the use case was never going to work and you should stop spending on it. Churn is how an organization avoids throwing good money after bad, if it is willing to listen to what the number says.

## A worked example: Lucia's ledger

Lucia runs operations at the freight-brokering firm Lesson 2 introduced, and she picked accounts-payable invoice pre-fill as her pilot. Twelve thousand invoices a month stream in from carriers, and before the pilot four clerks keyed each line by hand. Before she turns the pilot on, she writes down her four metrics, a baseline, and a threshold for each. This is her worksheet, and the numbers are an illustration of the method, not a result from a real deployment.

**Time saved.** Definition: minutes to code a batch of one hundred invoices, measured exactly the same way before and after. Baseline: eighteen minutes per batch, from a week of timing. Threshold: ten minutes per batch within ninety days, with at least half of that time visibly reallocated to something the department values, not just to idle minutes.

**Quality.** Definition: error rate, the share of coded lines a reviewing clerk rejects or corrects, and the containment rate, whether every error is caught before payment. Baseline: two errors per thousand lines, all caught in review. Threshold: no rise in the error rate, and the containment rate stays at one hundred percent. She does not accept faster coding if the errors start slipping past the clerks and into payment, because a payment mistake multiplies the cost of any time saved.

**Usage.** Definition: adoption share, the number of the four eligible clerks using the pre-fill daily, and their weekly active count. Baseline: zero, the tool is not on yet. Threshold: all four using it most days within a month. She judges the pilot on people, not on clicks.

**Churn.** Definition: the count of clerks who used it in a week and then went a full week without. Baseline: zero, nothing to churn from. Threshold: if after the first month any clerk has gone dark for two straight weeks, that is a flag she investigates immediately. She treats a single quiet-dropout as a crisis to understand, not a rounding error to ignore.

Now watch how reading the four together changes the decision. Suppose a month in she sees that time saved is on target, all four clerks are using the tool daily, and nobody has churned, but the error rate has drifted up to eight per thousand and two slipped into a payment that got corrected. The activity metrics look like a celebration. The quality metric says stop, find out why the checking step degraded, and fix it before scale. If she had measured only usage and time, she would have declared victory and scaled a process that was quietly corrupting the ledger. That is the lesson: the four metrics only protect you when you read them as a set.

![Lucia's worksheet lays out a definition, baseline, and threshold for each of the four metrics, then shows a month in where the activity metrics celebrate while the quality metric objects and stops the scale.](</diagrams/measuring-what-matters-time-saved-quality-usage-churn/4-lucias-ledger-worksheet.png>)

The template carries to any use case. Every pilot you run gets a one-line definition, a baseline, and a threshold for each of the four. The baseline matters as much as the threshold, because without a before-you-cannot-prove an after, which is the entire reason the GitHub experiment was credible and your colleague's feeling was not. [^1]

## Try it

Take the pilot you shortlisted in Lesson 2, the candidate that passed the most tests and had a credible answer to the workflow question. For that one use case, write a worksheet like Lucia's, a single-line definition for each of the four metrics.

For **time saved**, name the exact task you will time, the unit of output, and how you will measure it identically before and after the pilot. For **quality**, name the output standard and the error-containment step, who checks the AI output and how you will know whether anything wrong escaped. For **usage**, name the eligible population and which rhythm you will track. For **churn**, name what counts as "stopped," and who raises the flag.

Then, hardest of all, write the baseline for each one, what it is today before the tool changes anything. If you cannot state a baseline because nobody has ever measured it, write "to be captured" next to it and make capturing it the first task of the pilot. A measurement you cannot baseline is a number you cannot trust.

The result is your pilot's measurement plan. Hold on to it. It is exactly the tool you will refine when Lesson 4 has you audit your whole organization's current state, and it is the evidence you will need when someone asks, in a budget meeting, whether any of this actually works.

## What's next

You now have the four metrics that turn adoption from a belief into a number: time saved and quality, your proof that the work produces value, and usage and churn, your proof that people are actually staying with it. You know the trap, that speed without quality just reaches bad outcomes sooner, and you have a worksheet that measures any pilot against all four before you scale it.

That is the measurement of a single use case, and it is only half of what you need. Measuring a pilot tells you whether that one bet paid off. It does not tell you the state of your whole organization, who is using AI where, which of it is sanctioned, which of it is happening in the shadows, and what your baseline across the whole company actually is before you start changing any of it. That is the subject of Lesson 4, which takes you from measuring one pilot to auditing where your organization actually is, so the program you are building starts from a true read of the ground instead of a guess. Bring your measurement plan; it becomes the template you will use to survey the whole organization.

For now, hold the two ideas that carry this lesson. Measure the work and the outcome, not the feeling, which is why a controlled before-and-after beats a survey of enthusiasm. And read all four metrics together, because a dashboard that celebrates activity while the quality slips is how an organization scales a mistake.
