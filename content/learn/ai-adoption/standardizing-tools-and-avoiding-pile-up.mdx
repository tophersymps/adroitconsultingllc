Ask five departments which AI tools they use and you will get five short lists. Ask finance for the card statements and you will get a longer one. The gap between those two answers is the subject of this lesson.

Pile-up is not a discipline problem. It is the predictable result of a system where buying takes a credit card and an afternoon, while governing takes a committee and a quarter. Every individual decision looks reasonable. A support lead buys a transcription tool because the vendor does one thing well. Marketing buys a design generator because the general assistant's image output is mediocre. An engineer expenses an assistant because the enterprise one does not read their codebase. Six months later the company pays for eleven AI subscriptions, four of them overlapping, three of them with no owner, and the person responsible for AI adoption is handed a spreadsheet and asked to fix it.

This lesson is about fixing it in a way that survives contact with next quarter. You will build a standard stack: a short, named, owned list of primary tools mapped to the jobs people actually do, with one written path for exceptions. Then you will set up the small amount of machinery that keeps the list from refilling.

## Five terms to settle first

**Tool pile-up (sprawl).** The accumulation of overlapping tools across an organization, usually bought by different teams for the same underlying job, with no single owner of the whole list. The defining feature is not the count. It is the absence of a decision about the count.

**Standard stack.** The short list of tools an organization has chosen as its defaults, one primary per job category, published where employees can find it. A standard stack is a decision, not an inventory. The inventory tells you what exists. The stack tells you what you are funding on purpose.

**Job category.** The unit you standardize around: the task a person is trying to finish, not the feature list of the product. "Turn a meeting into decisions and owners" is a job category. "AI meeting assistant with speaker diarization" is a product description.

**Exception (specialist tool).** A tool allowed outside the standard stack because it does something the primary genuinely cannot, for a named team, with a named owner and a review date. An exception is a decision with an expiry. An unmanaged tool is not an exception, it is drift.

**Consolidation.** Retiring overlapping tools, migrating their users to the primary, and ending the contracts. Consolidation happens at renewal dates, because that is when ending a contract is cheap and starting one is negotiable.

## Why the pile keeps growing

Four mechanisms drive pile-up, and each needs a different counter.

The first is that buying is now the default path. Menlo Ventures puts enterprise generative AI spend at thirty-seven billion dollars in 2025, up 3.2 times from the prior year, and reports that seventy-six percent of enterprise AI use cases are purchased rather than built internally.[^1] When the answer to a workflow problem is a purchase, the number of vendors in the building rises with the number of workflows people notice.

The second is that a large share of that buying never passes through procurement. Menlo estimates twenty-seven percent of AI application spend arrives through product-led growth, roughly four times the rate for traditional software, and that the share approaches forty percent once you count employees putting tools on personal cards, with about twenty-seven percent of consumer assistant usage being work-related.[^1] A purchase that never touches a purchase order never touches a review.

The third is that AI keeps arriving inside software you already own, which raises cost without adding a line item anyone can see. Zylo's 2026 index found average annual SaaS spend at 55.7 million dollars, up eight percent year over year, while portfolio size stayed flat at 305 applications, down 0.07 percent. The report's conclusion is blunt: growth is no longer coming from more software but from how that software is priced, packaged, and expanded. In the same survey, seventy-eight percent of IT leaders reported unexpected charges tied to AI features or consumption-based pricing in the past year, and sixty-one percent had cut projects because of unplanned SaaS cost increases.[^2] Cledara documents the pattern in individual contracts: Notion folded its AI add-on into its Business tier, effectively doubling cost for many teams; Canva raised Teams pricing by up to three hundred percent citing AI features; Salesforce priced Agentforce at 125 dollars per user per month on top of existing licenses.[^3] None of those are new tools. All of them are new spend.

The fourth is that tools multiply faster than people learn them, so capability falls while the bill rises. In Zapier's survey of 550 US enterprise leaders at companies with at least a thousand employees, twenty-eight percent said they run more than ten different AI applications, and seventy percent had not moved beyond basic integration between them. The reported costs were concrete: thirty-six percent cited increased security and privacy risk, thirty-four percent said tool sprawl made training employees harder, thirty percent said they were wasting money on redundant software, and twenty-nine percent said employees were losing time to manual data transfers between tools.[^4] That last number is the one to sit with. The tools were bought to remove busywork, and someone is now paid to carry data between them.

## You will not standardize down to one tool

There is a tempting version of this lesson where the answer is a single platform that does everything. Resist it, for two reasons.

The first is that consolidation is not what the near-term market is doing. Gartner's 2026 Hype Cycle for AI in IT Operations makes the point explicitly: many narratives promise tool consolidation, and Gartner predicts the opposite outcome in the near term, with more layers, more control points, and more specialized observability, orchestration, and management tools, until vendor consolidation eventually reduces the footprint.[^5] You are not going to beat that tide with a policy memo. You are going to pick which seams you police.

The second is that a specialist tool is sometimes the only thing that works. A general assistant produces mediocre output for a narrow, high-volume job, and the team that owns that job knows it. Forcing them onto the standard tool does not create standardization, it creates a shadow subscription and a resentful team. The goal is a stack where every tool has a job, an owner, and a reason, not a stack with the smallest possible number of logos.

## The method: inventory, classify, choose, consolidate, govern

![The five-step standard-stack method: inventory every AI charge from the last twelve months, classify by job category rather than feature, choose one primary per category on four written criteria, consolidate at renewal dates, and govern the front door with an intake form, a one-in one-out rule, and a quarterly review](</diagrams/standardizing-tools-and-avoiding-pile-up/2-five-step-method.png>)

### Step one: inventory what is actually running

You cannot standardize what you have not counted. NIST's AI Risk Management Framework treats this as a formal governance requirement rather than housekeeping: its Govern function calls for mechanisms to inventory AI systems, resourced according to organizational risk priorities, and the playbook is specific about what that means in practice. An AI system inventory is an organized database of artifacts for each system, it should name the individual or team responsible for maintaining it, and organizations should define in policy which systems are inventoried and which attributes are captured.[^6]

Translate that into a first pass you can run in a week. Pull every AI-related charge from the last twelve months, including team cards and expense reports, not just procurement. Then add the tools nobody paid for directly: the AI tiers inside existing software, the free trials that became habit, the browser extensions, and the assistants embedded in products the company already licenses. Then ask each department head for their own list and compare. The gap between their answer and the card statements is your real inventory.

For each entry, capture six things: the tool, the vendor, the job it does, the team using it, the monthly cost, and the renewal date. The renewal date matters more than the cost, because it decides when you can act.

### Step two: classify by job, not by feature

Group the inventory by job category. Most organizations land between six and ten categories: a general assistant for writing and research, meeting capture and notes, coding assistance, design and image generation, sales and outreach drafting, data analysis and reporting, customer support assistance, and translation or transcription.

Now put the tools under the categories and look for two things. Overlap, where two or more tools serve the same job category for the same population. And orphans, tools that serve no category anyone can name, which are usually the cheapest to cut and the easiest to defend cutting.

Two cautions on classifying. Do not classify by vendor suite, because a suite that covers five categories badly will look efficient on paper and produce four shadow tools in practice. And do not classify by feature checklist, because vendors converge: the feature that justified a purchase eighteen months ago is frequently in the primary tool now, and nobody sent an email about it.

### Step three: choose one primary per job category

For each category, pick a primary. The selection criteria should be boring and written down: does it clear the security and data-safety bar from earlier in this course, does the team that does the work actually prefer it, does it integrate with the systems where the work already lives, and what does it cost per active user. Preference of the people doing the work belongs in the criteria, because a tool nobody opens is not cheaper than the tool they use.

Publish the result as the standard stack: one page, category, primary tool, owner, and where to get help. That page is the artifact that ends most future pile-up, because it gives a new manager an answer that is not "buy something."

### Step four: consolidate at renewal, not someday

Consolidation has a natural rhythm and it is not the rhythm of your enthusiasm. Contracts end on dates. Migrations take a quarter to settle. Attempting a forced cutover mid-contract pays twice for the same job, once to the old vendor and once to the new one, and it teaches the organization that consolidation is painful.

So build a renewal calendar from the inventory and work it. For each tool being retired: name the migration owner, move the users to the primary tool, export and archive what needs keeping, ask the vendor in writing to delete the company data, and end the contract before the auto-renewal date rather than after. Then say so publicly. A retired tool that nobody announces comes back under a different name in six months.

### Step five: govern the front door

Consolidation without an intake process is a cycle, not a fix. Put three small controls in place.

An intake form with six fields: the job you are trying to do, the tool, the cost, the data it will touch, the owner, and the review date. A form that takes four minutes gets used. One that requires a business case gets routed around.

A one-in, one-out rule for new tools in an existing category: a new tool enters the stack only when another leaves it or the requester can show it does a job the primary cannot. This is the rule that stops the pile from refilling, because it makes the cost of addition visible at the moment of addition.

A quarterly fifteen-minute review of the stack: what entered, what left, what is up for renewal, what is unused. Fifteen minutes quarterly is enough to catch drift. An annual audit is enough to discover it a year late.

## Worked example: Dhara rebuilds the stack

Dhara owns AI adoption at the eight-hundred-and-twenty-person company from the budgeting lesson. She has a budget cap and a monthly review, and both are being eaten by tools she did not approve.

Her inventory takes nine days. It finds twenty AI tools in use: eleven paid, nine free or in trial. Two are on the marketing team's card and never touched procurement. Three are AI tiers that arrived inside existing software during a renewal, priced as an upgrade. Four are individual assistant subscriptions expensed by people who did not know the company already had an enterprise assistant.

She classifies them into six job categories and the picture gets simple fast. Five tools do general writing and research. Three do meeting capture. Two do coding assistance. Three do design and image generation. Three do sales outreach drafting. Three do analysis. One tool does nothing anyone can name; it was bought for a pilot that ended in the spring.

Here is the shape of it, with monthly costs rounded to the nearest ten dollars:

- General assistant: five tools, 1,180 dollars, keep one enterprise assistant with data controls at 620 dollars
- Meeting capture: three tools, 640 dollars, keep one at 290 dollars
- Coding assistance: two tools, 780 dollars, keep one at 420 dollars
- Design and image generation: three tools, 510 dollars, keep one at 180 dollars
- Sales outreach drafting: three tools, 690 dollars, keep one at 310 dollars
- Analysis: three tools, 380 dollars, keep two at 260 dollars
- Unnamed pilot tool: one tool, 250 dollars, cancel

That is 4,430 dollars a month across twenty tools. The standard stack she publishes has seven tools, six of them paid, at 2,080 dollars a month. The difference is 2,350 dollars a month, or 28,200 dollars a year.

![Consolidation category by category in the worked example: twenty AI tools at 4,430 dollars a month reduced to seven tools at 2,080 dollars, a monthly difference of 2,350 dollars and an annual difference of 28,200 dollars](</diagrams/standardizing-tools-and-avoiding-pile-up/3-consolidation-math.png>)

Two decisions in that table are worth explaining, because they are the ones a reviewer will challenge.

She keeps two analysis tools. The spreadsheet copilot and the warehouse query tool look like the same category on a feature grid, but the first is used by finance on files that never reach the warehouse and the second is used by operations on data that never reaches a spreadsheet. Collapsing them would have saved 120 dollars a month and cost two teams their workflow. That is a bad trade, and the standard stack says so in writing, with a review date.

She does not cut the coding tool to save the meeting tool. Coding is 780 dollars of the 4,430, the largest single category after the general assistant, and it is also the one with the clearest measured output in her program. The name of a category and its price do not decide the cut. Utilization and outcome do.

Then the part everyone skips: she spends the savings on purpose. Sixteen thousand dollars of the 28,200 goes into training and a shared prompt library organized around the seven tools in the stack, and the remaining 12,200 dollars stays as headroom against the next embedded AI price increase. Announcing that split is what makes the exercise survivable politically, because the teams that lost a tool can see where the money went.

The controls come last and they are small. An intake form. One-in, one-out in each category. A renewal calendar built from the inventory so that the next consolidation is a scheduled event instead of a project. And a quarterly fifteen-minute review where the stack page either gets confirmed or edited.

Nine months later the count is seven, not twenty, and Dhara can name the job, the owner, and the cost of every one of them. That is the whole outcome. Not a small number. A known one.

## What not to standardize away

Standardizing tools without standardizing practice just moves the mess into the tools you kept. Three things belong in the standard alongside the stack.

The first is a shared prompt and template library, organized by job category, so the meeting-notes prompt that works is not locked in one person's chat history. The second is one place to ask for help, which for most organizations is the champions network from the earlier lesson rather than a ticket queue. The third is training that teaches the standard tools first, because training people on a tool you are retiring at renewal is money spent twice.

## Try it: build the standard stack in a week

Block an hour a day for five days.

Day one, pull twelve months of AI-related charges from finance, plus expense reports, plus the software the company already pays for. Do not filter for "AI" in the merchant name, because the assistant tiers hide inside ordinary SaaS renewals.

Day two, send one short message to every department head asking for the AI tools their team uses, including free ones. Compare the answers to the charges and note every gap in both directions.

Day three, classify everything into job categories. For each category, write the one-line job description in plain language: "turn a customer call into a summary with owners," not "conversational intelligence."

Day four, pick a primary per category using four criteria: security review passed, team preference, integration with where the work lives, cost per active user. Write down every exception with an owner and a review date, using the test in the next paragraph.

Day five, publish the one-page stack, build the renewal calendar from the dates you collected, and write the intake form.

For every exception you keep, the test is four sentences: this tool does this specific job, for this team, because the primary cannot do this specific thing, and we will review it on this date. If you cannot fill in all four, the tool is drift.

## What's next

You now have a standard stack and a way to keep it standard: an inventory you can defend, categories organized around jobs, primaries chosen on criteria rather than enthusiasm, consolidation scheduled to renewal dates, and an intake process that makes adding a tool cost something. The remaining problem is the one that follows every standardization decision in a company of any size: getting the right people onto the right tools, in the right order, with the right permissions, without a six-week queue at IT. Lesson 32 takes that on directly, with the access and licensing rollout at scale.
