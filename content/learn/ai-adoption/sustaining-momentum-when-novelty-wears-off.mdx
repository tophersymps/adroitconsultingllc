Every tech launch has the same shape. Week one, curiosity carries the program. Everyone tries the tool, the dashboard lights up, and the early numbers look great. Week four, the curious people have gone back to their habits and the usage curve bends downward. Week eight, either the program has found its real users and settled into a steady rhythm, or it is quietly dying.

That downward bend right after launch is not a symptom that adoption failed. It is the novelty effect wearing off, and it happens to every change in every organization. The programs that survive are not the ones that avoid the dip. They are the ones whose leaders planned for it and knew what to do at the lowest point. This lesson gives you the discipline of sustaining momentum when the easy enthusiasm is gone.

## The novelty effect: why the spike is not the signal

The novelty effect is a well-documented pattern in how people take up technology. In human-computer interaction research it is defined as a person's "first responses to using a technology, not the pattern of usage that will persist over time as the product ceases to be new to him or her."[^1] In plain terms, the early enthusiasm is real, but it is driven by the fact that the thing is new, not by the fact that it is useful. Curiosity produces the spike. Usefulness produces the plateau.

The evidence for this pattern is consistent across very different products. One study of activity tracker users in the United States found that over half of users had discontinued their devices and one-third had stopped within six months.[^1] In the same line of research, a mixed-methods study of 23 Fitbit users found two distinct stages in how people used the device: a novelty period of roughly three months and a long-term use period after that.[^1] The people who kept the device past the novelty period were not the ones who found it most exciting at first. They were the ones who found a concrete job it did for them.

Read what that means for your AI program. The spike in week one tells you almost nothing about whether AI will stick. It tells you that your people are curious, which is useful, but curiosity is not commitment. The number that matters is the one you measure after the novelty period has passed, when using the tool is no longer interesting and is just work. If people use AI at that point, they are using it because it helps them, and that usage is the only kind that compounds.

## Real momentum versus a one-time spike

Because the novelty spike is universal, the hardest skill for an adoption owner is telling the difference between a gain in real momentum and a spike that will not last. The two look identical in the first few weeks. They behave very differently after that.

A spike is a burst of usage driven by a single event: a launch, a training day, a new feature that everyone wants to try. It is front-loaded. It peaks fast and it decays fast, because nothing changed about why people work the way they do. The event created the usage, and when the event ends, the usage ends with it.

Real momentum is different. Momentum is a self-reinforcing pattern where each gain makes the next gain easier. A team gets a task done faster with AI, that visible win pulls in a neighboring team, the bigger user base produces more success stories, and those stories pull in the next group. Momentum has a compounding shape, not a spike shape. It grows slowly, survives small setbacks, and keeps climbing after the launch event is a memory.

Here is the practical test that separates them. A spike depends on the thing that started it. If the usage collapses when you stop running the launch event, or when a single champion goes quiet, what you had was a spike. Momentum depends on nothing external. It runs on its own returns. If usage holds steady or climbs after the novelty window closes, without you pushing every day, it is becoming real.

Two more signs separate compounding momentum from a spike, and both come from the adoption metrics you built in Lesson 3.

**Depth over breadth.** A spike shows up in breadth, many people trying once. Momentum shows up in depth, the same people returning because the tool now sits inside how they do a specific job. Watch the usage metric and the churn metric from Lesson 3, not just the raw count. High reach with high churn is a spike. Steadier reach with low churn is momentum forming.

**Quality over activity.** Momentum shows up in the outcome metrics, time saved and quality, not just in usage. Spikes are activity. Momentum produces results that you can point to. When you have a number that says a team cleared work in a quarter of the time, that number is durable fuel. A raw usage count is not.

## The four-part cadence for sustaining momentum

Once you can read the difference between a spike and momentum, the work is simple to state and hard to keep doing: you need a cadence that keeps converting curiosity into habit, week after week, past the point where anyone is excited anymore. Four moves, done on a rhythm, do most of the work.

**1. Resurface the outcomes on a fixed rhythm.** The people who use AI every day will forget why it was worth it. Not because they are ungrateful, but because a tool that works becomes invisible, and an invisible tool stops being a reason to change how you work. The fix is a fixed, recurring moment where the outcome is named again: at a monthly review, a weekly standup, a quarterly all-hands. You are not celebrating. You are reconnecting behavior to its payoff so the reason to keep using the tool stays present. The rhythm matters more than the format. A monthly number that always shows up beats a brilliant presentation that happens once.

**2. Remove friction from the top complaint.** The novelty period hides a lot of friction, because people tolerate annoying tools while they are curious. The moment curiosity fades, that tolerance ends, and every small annoyance becomes a reason to fall back to the old way. Build a standing list of what the real users complain about, and clear the top friction item on a regular cadence. Each removal is adoption you stop having to argue for, because you have made the new way genuinely easier than the old way. Lesson 6's friction list is the raw material for this move.

**3. Expand into the next workflow with the same people first.** Momentum is easier to deepen than to start. The people already using AI for one part of their job are the cheapest place to get the next win, because the tool is licensed, the access is set up, and they already know how to prompt it. Before you chase new users, look at the existing users and find the next adjacent workflow where the same tool can help. Breadth through depth, not depth through breadth. Each adjacent workflow makes the tool more central to that person's day, and the more central it is, the more it survives the novelty effect.

**4. Reward the specific adoption behavior, not the vague enthusiasm.** Reinforce the exact behavior you want more of. When a team uses AI to clear a backlog, name the team and say what they did, not "nice work on AI." When a person finds a way to use the tool that saves real time, surface it and show how they did it. Specific reinforcement tells every other team what adoption actually looks like in practice, which is far more useful than an abstract target. Vague praise is a spike. Specific recognition of a specific result is momentum.

These four moves are not one-time actions. They are a cadence you commit to running after the launch, through the dip, and past it, for as long as the program exists. The launch bought you curiosity. The cadence is what converts it into habit.

## Worked example: Mateo reads the dip and works the cadence

Mateo owns AI adoption at a mid-sized customer-support software company with about 400 people. In Lesson 5 he set a north-star metric: the share of support tickets where an agent starts with an AI-drafted response, with a target of 70 percent by the end of the second quarter.

The launch goes well. In the first two weeks, usage spikes: 61 percent of agents try the tool at least once. Mateo is tempted to read that as success. But he has planned for the novelty effect, so he watches the two metrics that separate a spike from momentum: churn and depth. In week two, 61 percent reach, but only 12 percent of agents have used the tool on more than half their tickets. Reach is high. Depth is low. That is a spike, and Mateo expects it to fade.

It does. By week six, daily reach has fallen to under 30 percent. A leader who read the spike as adoption would panic and start a second launch campaign. Mateo instead reads the curve against what he knows about the novelty effect: the dip is normal, and the question now is which users are still here and why.

He looks at the 12 percent who stayed deep. They are not the loudest voices in the org. They are the agents who handle the most repetitive ticket types: password resets, billing questions, account status. For them, the AI draft is not interesting, it is faster, which is exactly the outcome metric that survives novelty. Mateo finds his momentum at the bottom of the dip, hiding inside a repeatable workflow.

Then he runs his cadence. On a fixed weekly rhythm, he resurfaces the outcome: the deep agents now close password-reset tickets in about half the time, and he names that number and that team in the weekly operations wrap. He builds the friction list from what the real users tell him, and the top item is clear: the AI draft is too long for short tickets, so agents spend time trimming it. He works with the settings so the draft length adapts to the ticket type. That one removal takes the nag out of the rest of the dip.

Next, he expands with the same people first. The billing team already watches the password-reset win, and billing tickets share a shape with account-status tickets, so that is the cheaper next target than chasing the marketing department, which has no ready-made AI workflow. He gives the billing team access, reuses the same settings, and the depth transfers.

And he rewards the specific behavior. When a billing agent finds that a particular phrasing makes the draft stick more often, Mateo puts that agent's phrasing in the team notes, with the agent's name on it, as the example of what works. Specific recognition, not a vague "great AI work" banner.

By the end of the quarter, the north-star metric clears 70 percent. It did not happen because the launch was brilliant. It happened because Mateo treated the novelty dip as the real program: he read depth, not reach; he removed the top friction; he deepened usage with his existing users before chasing new ones; and he ran the cadence long after the launch was over.

## Try it: read your own curve and plan the cadence

Open your adoption data from Lesson 3, or the report you would get if you had it, and answer four questions in writing.

First, separate the spike from the momentum. What was your peak reach in the first weeks, and what is your reach now? More usefully, what is your depth: of the people who tried AI, how many use it on most of their relevant work, and what is your churn? Write both numbers side by side. If reach is high and churn is high, you have novelty usage, and you now know what you are actually managing.

Second, find where the momentum is hiding. Who is using the tool consistently right now, not who is loudest? What specific, repeatable workflow are they using it on? That workflow, not the launch, is your real adoption story, because it is the one that survives the novelty effect.

Third, write your top friction item. Ask the people who actually use the tool what makes it harder than the old way, and pick the single most repeated complaint. Commit to clearing it within a set window. One removed friction is worth more than three new launch emails.

Fourth, pick your cadence. What is the fixed rhythm, weekly or monthly, where you will resurface the outcome, and what specific number or team will you name? If you cannot name the metronome, the momentum will depend on your energy, and your energy is a spike.
