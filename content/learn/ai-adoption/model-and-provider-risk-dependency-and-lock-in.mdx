In the last lesson you built an incident response plan for when an AI tool fails. That plan assumes you can act on the tool. But the uncomfortable fact is that most of the AI in your organization, the part that actually does the work, is not something you run. It is a model someone else trained, served through someone else's infrastructure, priced on someone else's terms, and retired on someone else's schedule. You are renting capability, not owning it.

That is not inherently bad. Renting a frontier model is how a 200-person firm gets world-class capability it could never build itself. The bad version is renting it in a way that makes you unable to leave, and unable to absorb the changes the provider is free to make. This lesson is about the difference. You will learn where the dependency actually lives, what lock-in genuinely costs a non-technical owner, and a concrete set of moves that keep your AI program resilient when the thing you rely on changes. The framing to hold from the start is this: dependency and lock-in are two different claims. Dependency means you need the thing. Lock-in means you cannot leave it, not without paying a price you did not budget for. Most organizations discover they were locked in only when they try to leave.

## The words you need

**Model provider** is the company that trained and operates the AI model you use, in contrast to the vendor that may sell you access to it. A provider like the one behind the frontier model your team calls by name owns the model, the infrastructure that runs it, and the terms under which you use it.[^1] The distinction matters because your renewal contract may sit with a reseller, while the real risk sits with whoever can change the model, the price, or the service. Know which one you actually depend on.

**Foundation model** (also called a general-purpose AI model) is a large model trained broadly, not for one task, that is then adapted or steered for specific jobs. It is the "general" layer under most of the generative AI your people use. Its defining feature for your purposes is concentration: a small number of these models power a very large share of AI use, which means your dependence and your provider's power both rise together.[^2]

**Dependency risk** is the possibility that a change or failure in something you rely on disrupts you. Every AI use case has one, and the question is never whether you have it, it is how concentrated it is. Relying on one model through one provider for one high-value workflow is a much different risk than using several providers across many discretionary tasks.

**Vendor lock-in** is the condition where switching becomes so costly or disruptive that you stay out of inertia, not because the current choice is still the best one. The cost is called **switching cost**: everything it takes to move a working AI workflow from one provider to another, in re-testing, re-tuning, re-integration, data migration, and retraining people. Lock-in is the point where switching cost has grown large enough to trap you.[^3]

**Model deprecation** is a provider's formal notice that a model will stop working on a set date. **Model retirement** is the date it actually does. These are the events that force a switch whether you are ready or not. Providers publish deprecation policies precisely so you have notice, and the notice is both a courtesy and a risk: it tells you the clock is running, and the work you must do in that window is entirely yours.

**Portability** is how easily your AI work could move to an alternative: prompts, formatting, output, stored data, and the human process around the tool all moving without expensive rework. **Exit plan** is the written answer to "what would we do if this provider changed the model, raised the price, went down for a day, or shut down." You will build one in the try-it exercise at the end of this lesson.

## Why a single model and a single provider are two different risks

The cleanest way to see model and provider risk is to stop treating "the AI" as one thing and separate the two layers, because they fail and they trap you differently.

**Model risk** is the risk carried by the specific model you use. The model your team has learned to trust, tuned its prompts to, and calibrated its judgment against can be retired and replaced by the provider. When it is, the behavior changes: a newer model is not simply better, it answers differently, rates things differently, and formats output differently. Work that was validated against the old model's behavior needs re-testing even when the replacement is "better." A model can also degrade quietly over time as the provider adjusts it, which is a drift you may detect only when an output that used to be reliable stops being so.[^4] Model risk is real, but a model is replaceable in principle.

**Provider risk** is the risk of depending on the company itself. Providers can change pricing with limited notice, change their terms of service, reduce the availability commitments they will sign, discontinue a product line, be acquired and have their roadmap redirected, or fail. They can also experience outages that take their service down for hours. Provider risk is stickier than model risk, because even a perfect replacement model does not help you if the outage is at the provider layer, and your contract and your data may both be entangled with the specific provider you chose.[^5]

The two risks compound into lock-in. You do not get locked into a model, you get locked into the web of assumptions, integrations, stored outputs, and trained behaviors that have grown around a provider's particular way of doing things. A December 2024 incident illustrates the fragility: a single power failure in a cloud provider's data center caused more than 90 percent error rates across a major provider's chat, API, and other products for hours.[^6] Any team running a critical workflow solely on that provider had no fallback that day. The failure was not the model's fault and not the software's fault. It was pure provider concentration, and no amount of prompt tuning fixes that.

## Regulators already treat the biggest models as a special risk

You do not need to become an expert on AI regulation to run your program, but one fact changes how you should think about model and provider dependency, so it is worth holding. The largest general-purpose AI models are now a named, regulated category. Under the EU AI Act, a general-purpose AI model is presumed to pose "systemic risk" if it was trained with more than 10 to the 25th floating point operations, roughly the compute that only the frontier providers can deploy, and providers of such models carry extra obligations: assess and mitigate systemic risks, run model evaluations including adversarial testing, report serious incidents, and secure an adequate level of cybersecurity.[^7][^8]

Why does this matter to you as a non-technical owner? Two reasons. First, it confirms legally that the frontier models you rely on are a concentrated, high-impact resource controlled by a small number of firms, which is exactly the dependency you are managing. Second, it means the largest providers operate under a changing regulatory regime that can reshape their roadmaps, their costs, and their available models. Regulation is not a reason to avoid using providers. It is a reason to treat the dependency as something to manage deliberately, with alternatives in mind, because the provider's own constraints are not something you control and can change.

## The events that actually force a switch

Dependency risk is abstract until one of four real events lands. Each one is documented and each has happened in the last few years. Name them so you can plan for them.

**The model you rely on is retired.** Providers retire models on published schedules. One major provider documents a minimum six months notice before retiring a generally available model, and three months for specialized variants, and publishes its deprecation table, including, as one example, the retirement of an earlier GPT-4 snapshot on a 2026 shutdown date.[^9] Another major cloud provider publishes model retirement dates and auto-upgrade rules that can change the behavior of a deployed model even without a full retirement.[^10] The point is not which models, it is the pattern: specific versions you rely on stop working on a date, and your re-testing and migration live inside the notice window. If you pinned your whole workflow to one retirement-prone snapshot, the clock is real.

**The provider goes down.** Outages at the provider layer take your tool down no matter how good your model is. The December 2024 multi-product outage and a January 2025 ChatGPT outage are on the public record, and both affected enterprises relying on the service.[^6][^11] For a non-technical owner the lesson is operational, not technical: do you have a fallback for the hours your primary tool is down, or does the whole department stop?

**Pricing changes.** Providers and the resellers around them change price. When a price increase lands mid-program, a team with no alternative either absorbs the new cost or scrambles. Because the switch is costly, you have weak negotiating leverage once you are deeply integrated.[^3] The discipline of checking alternatives regularly is what keeps a price change a negotiation instead of a demand you have to accept.

**Terms change, or the provider stops being a going concern.** Contract terms can change what you can and cannot do, and any vendor can fail or be acquired and redirected. These are harder to predict than a retirement, which is why your plan has to treat them as possible rather than unlikely.

## Worked example: the firm that built a town around one model

Let me run this through a concrete case. It is an illustration, not a real company, but the shape is common.

A mid-size services firm, the same roughly 200-person firm from the incident response lesson, decides to use AI to draft responses for proposals and client questions. The team picks a single well-known model through a single provider because it was fast to start and produced good results. They build directly on that provider's interface and save their outputs there. They tune a set of prompts over three months until the answers are reliably good. They integrate the drafts straight into their proposal process, and after a quarter the senior proposal writer says it has cut drafting time by about a third. Leadership is happy. Nobody has asked the question this lesson teaches: what would we do if this specific thing changed?

The provider announces that the exact model snapshot the firm built around is being retired in six months, replaced by a newer model with a different name and different behavior. To continue, the firm must migrate.

The migration bites because the firm never separated its work from the provider's way of doing things. The prompts were tuned to the old model's quirks and answer poorly on the first attempt with the replacement. The saved proposal output, which is genuinely good and is now part of the firm's template library, is stored in a way that does not export cleanly into another tool. The integration that feeds drafts into the proposal process is written to the old model's call format and needs rework. The team's intuition about how to guide it, which is the hardest thing to rebuild, was calibrated to the retiring model. The six-month notice is not six months of leisure, it is six months of re-testing, re-tuning, re-integrating, migrating records, and retraining, all on top of the day job, while the price for the new model may differ.

Now run the counterfactual. Suppose from the start the firm had treated the model as interchangeable. It kept its prompts generic enough to port, saved drafts to its own document store in a standard format, used a thin internal layer so the call format was not written deep into every integration, and, twice a year, tested its two most important workflows against a second provider to confirm an alternative existed and roughly what switching would cost. When retirement came, the work would be days of re-testing instead of months of rebuilding. The firm would also know, from those regular checks, whether the alternative was close enough to actually switch if the price moved. That is model and provider resilience in practice, and it did not cost the firm a single extra ability to take advantage of the frontier model while it worked well.

The lesson is not "avoid providers." It is "use them with the exit in view." The firm's mistake was not renting the model. It was building everything else, the prompts, the output, the integrations, the trained judgment, so tightly around one provider that the provider had all the leverage and the firm had none.

## Where risk management frameworks put vendor dependency

The two most relevant frameworks agree that dependency on third parties is not an afterthought, it is a named category of risk you manage continuously. NIST's AI Risk Management Framework, the voluntary US standard, is explicit that AI risks extend to systems you did not build. Its Govern function requires policies and procedures that address AI risks arising from third-party software and data, and critically, contingency processes to handle failures or incidents in third-party data or AI systems deemed high risk.[^12] Its Map function requires mapping risks for all components of an AI system, including third-party software and data, and identifying internal risk controls for third-party AI technologies.[^12] Its Manage function requires that risks and benefits from third-party resources be regularly monitored, with risk controls applied and documented.[^12]

Read those three lines as one instruction for your program. Map the dependency (know every model and provider you rely on and what it does). Plan the contingency (know what you do when one fails or changes). Monitor continuously (re-check the dependency, the alternatives, and the changing terms, on a schedule, not once at purchase). For a non-technical owner this is not code, it is an inventory, a plan, and a recurring review, and it maps directly onto the moves below.

## The resilience playbook: what a non-technical owner actually does

You do not need to understand APIs to reduce dependency risk. You need six moves, each one a governance activity, not an engineering one.

**Inventory your dependencies.** Write down every meaningful AI use case and, for each, the model and the provider behind it. You cannot manage a dependency you have not named. The inventory is the same discipline you built in the shadow IT lesson, applied to the models themselves.

**Find the single points of failure.** For each use case in your inventory, ask: is this the only provider doing this job in our organization, and is the job critical? A single critical workflow on a single provider is your highest dependency risk. Flag it. The point is not to eliminate single providers, it is to know where you have no slack when they change.

**Test one or two alternatives, regularly.** A few times a year, have the team run your two or three most important workflows against a second provider just to confirm an alternative exists and to size the switching cost. This is the single most valuable habit for managing provider risk, because it converts "a switch would be unthinkable" into "a switch would take us about two weeks and here is what it would touch."

**Keep your work portable.** Store the outputs that matter in a format and a place you control, in a standard format that exports cleanly, not locked inside a provider's interface. Keep your prompts documented and written to be model-agnostic rather than tuned so hard to one model's quirks that they fail elsewhere. The less your value is entangled with one provider's way of doing things, the more leverage you have.

**Put the terms in the contract.** When you procure, the things worth negotiating are not just price. Worth asking for: reasonable notice before a model retirement or a material price change, and confirmation that your stored data is exportable and will be returned or deletable on exit. These are real contract terms that turn an unpredictable dependency into a known one. Your procurement owner is the person to write these, and the vendor evaluation and licensing lessons in this course already gave you the buying vocabulary.

**Write the exit plan for the few things that would hurt most.** For the one or two AI use cases where a provider change or outage would genuinely hurt the business, write a one-page answer to: what would we do if this model is retired, if the price doubles, if the service is down for a day, and if the provider shuts down. Name the trigger, the fallback, the owner, and the rough cost. You do not need this for every tool. You need it for the few that matter, and it turns a vague worry into a document you can act on.

## Try it: turn your top AI tool into a one-page dependency and exit plan

The best way to learn this is to do it on the tool your organization depends on most. Pick the single AI use case from your inventory that would hurt the most if it changed or failed. It might be the assistant everyone routes client work through, or the tool that drafts the documents your revenue depends on. One use case, the one that matters.

First, write the dependency map. Name the model and the provider behind it. Then separate the layers: model (which specific model or version), provider (which company and whether you buy direct or through a reseller), and integration (everything your team has built around it: saved outputs, tuned prompts, workflows, stored records). Write one line for each layer. The layer that surprises you is usually the integration line, because that is where the real switching cost hides.

Second, score the single-point-of-failure risk. Is this the only provider doing this job in your org? Is the job critical? If the answer to both is yes, you have a concentrated dependency. That is the use case that gets an exit plan.

Third, write the exit plan. Four short answers, one per risk: what you would do if the model is retired, if the price rises materially, if the service is down for a day, and if the provider shuts down. For each, name the trigger, the fallback, the owner, and the rough cost or effort. You will probably find that for the retirement and price cases you already have an answer, it is just unwritten, and that writing it down is what turns a worry into a plan. For the shutdown and outage cases you may find a blank, and that blank is your real deliverable.

Finally, test your portability assumption honestly. Ask the team: if we had to move this one workflow to a different provider, what would we have to re-test and re-tune, and how long would it take? If the honest answer is "we would have to rebuild it," you have found your lock-in, and now you know exactly what the next few quarters should reduce.

Keep the page where your program documentation lives, and review it each time you do your regular vendor health check. A dependency that is flagged, alternatived, and planned for stops being a silent risk and becomes a managed one.

## What's next

You now know where model and provider dependency lives, what lock-in really costs, and how to build resilience so a provider's model change, outage, price increase, or shutdown does not become your incident. You mapped one tool and wrote an exit plan. But there is a second dependency hiding alongside the technical one, and it is about ownership: the work your AI produces and the data it was trained on, who owns it, and who can use it.

Lesson 24 is Intellectual property: your data, your outputs. It picks up where this lesson stops. The resilience moves you just built make sure you can leave a provider. The next lesson makes sure that when you do, you are not leaving behind your rights. Your prompts, your outputs, your confidential data, and the training your vendor may have done on it, these are all ownership questions a non-technical owner has to answer, and most contracts are silent on them until you ask. That is the natural next step: dependency is about being able to leave, and intellectual property is about what you get to take with you.

For now, hold the core idea: renting capability is sensible, and getting locked in is not. Use the frontier models freely, but keep the exit in view, map the dependency, test an alternative on a schedule, keep your work portable, put the terms in the contract, and write the one-page plan for the few that would hurt. A program you can leave is a program you can run with leverage instead of anxiety.
