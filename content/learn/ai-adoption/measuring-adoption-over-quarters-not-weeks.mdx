Last lesson you built the machinery that turns a license into a login and a login into a habit: entitlement groups, a provisioning chain, waves with gates, and a reclamation loop with a date on it. All of that produces numbers. This lesson is about which of those numbers you are allowed to trust, and how long you have to watch them before you say anything out loud.

Start with the failure this lesson exists to prevent. Your Q1 report says 41 percent of licensed users were active. Your Q2 report says 46 percent. Five points of growth, a clean slide, and a program that appears to be climbing. Then you open the weekly series and find that Q1 ended in a launch spike that decayed over three weeks, that Q2's number was propped up by 120 new hires who used the tool heavily for a fortnight, and that the people active in January were 30 percent less likely to be active in June. The quarterly comparison was arithmetically correct and completely wrong about your program.

This is not a hypothetical trap. The NHS Institute for Innovation and Improvement documented the same error in a guide on run and control charts. An immunization program compared two periods, 79 percent take-up then 86 percent, and concluded that a new recall system had produced a significant improvement. Plotted as a time series, take-up had been climbing steadily before the change and fell steadily after it. Same data, opposite conclusion, and the only difference was whether anyone looked at the sequence or at two summaries.[^1]

Every AI adoption review runs that risk, and quarterly reviews run it more often than weekly ones, because distance makes summaries look like evidence.

## Eleven terms to settle first

**Measurement window.** The period a metric covers. A number without a window is not a metric, because 71 percent over seven days and 71 percent over 90 days are different claims about different things.

**Denominator.** Who is counted in the base. Adoption numbers are meaningless until you say whether the base is everyone eligible, everyone licensed, everyone assigned a seat, or everyone who could plausibly use the tool in their actual job. Most inflated adoption figures are denominator errors rather than counting errors.

**Reachable population.** The subset of licensed people whose work can use the tool today: they have the access, the workflow, and the manager support. A warehouse associate with no desk and no mobile app is on the license list and is not in the reachable population. Separating the two turns an apparently failing program into an accurately scoped one, and occasionally the reverse.

**Habit threshold.** The amount of repeated use that counts as a habit rather than a trial. It must be defined before you look at the data, in weeks rather than days, because a habit is a pattern and a pattern needs a period.

**Cohort.** A group defined by when they started, not by where they sit. The March wave is a cohort. The sales department is a segment. Cohorts answer "did people keep using it"; segments answer "who is using it". You need both, and they answer different questions.

**Cohort retention curve.** The share of a cohort still using the tool in week one, week four, week twelve, and so on. This curve is the single most honest artifact in an adoption program, because it cannot be inflated by adding new users.

**Plateau.** A period where the aggregate number stops rising. It has at least two very different causes, and telling them apart is most of this lesson.

**Decay curve.** The shape of use after an initial burst for a cohort that never formed a habit. Steep decay in the first three weeks is normal. Continued decay at week twelve means the enablement did not land.

**Leading and lagging indicator.** A leading indicator moves before the outcome you care about (returning users, workflows in use per person). A lagging indicator confirms it later (hours saved, cycle time, cost per case). Programs that report only lagging indicators discover problems a quarter after they happened.

**Seasonality.** Calendar effects that move usage for reasons unrelated to your program: fiscal quarter ends, holidays, summer, annual audit periods, major product launches inside the business. Seasonality is predictable, which means it is adjustable.

**Signal and noise.** A signal is a change caused by something specific that you could name and act on. Noise is ordinary variation around a stable level. The whole discipline of reading a series is refusing to call noise a signal, and refusing to call a signal noise.

## Why a week cannot carry this decision

Four reasons, in rough order of how often they cause a bad call.

**Small denominators make large percentages.** A 40-person pilot moves 2.5 percentage points when one person takes a day off. At that size, a weekly percentage chart is a random number generator with a company logo on it.

**Onboarding waves create a sawtooth.** Every new cohort starts below the organizational average, because novices use the tool less than habitual users. So a program that is onboarding aggressively will show a flat or falling aggregate while every individual cohort improves. This is the most common false alarm in an AI rollout, and the cure is cohort curves, not a better slide.

**The first fortnight is novelty.** Launch energy produces a spike that says nothing about whether the workflow stuck. If your reporting cadence is weekly, you will spend the first month of every wave celebrating a spike and the second month explaining its absence.

**Instruments lag, and instruments disagree.** Measurement windows and data delays are not trivia. Microsoft's Copilot Dashboard reports adoption over the previous 28 days with up to a six-day delay, while the Microsoft 365 admin center uses rolling windows of 7, 30, 90, or 180 days and produces its report within 72 hours. Microsoft's own documentation names this as a reason the two surfaces show different numbers for the same tenant.[^2] If your two dashboards disagree, the most likely explanation is that they are measuring different windows with different delays, and the least likely explanation is that your users changed their behavior on Tuesday.

Note what the vendor chose to build for reading trend: a six-month view, aggregated weekly. That is the scale at which the signal is larger than the weekly noise.[^2]

## Three numbers for the steering agenda

A quarterly review needs a small number of numbers, stated with their denominators, and it needs them to be the ones that resist gaming. These three do the work. You can add a value number and a quality counter-metric on top, but these are the floor.

**One: habitual use rate.** The share of the reachable population that meets your habit threshold over a 12-week period. Microsoft's own product definitions are a useful benchmark for what a defensible habit threshold looks like in a general assistant: a habitual user is one who averaged between 1 and 14 Copilot actions per week and used Copilot in at least 9 of the past 12 weeks, and a power user averaged 15 or more actions per week across at least 9 of 12 weeks.[^3] Two things are worth stealing from that definition. It is expressed in weeks, not days. And it separates frequency from volume, so a person who uses the tool twice a week every week is not confused with a person who used it forty times in one afternoon.

**Two: week-over-week returning rate.** The share of this week's active users who were also active last week. Microsoft exposes this as the percentage of returning Copilot users.[^3] It is the closest thing to a retention signal available inside a quarter, and it moves before the habitual rate does. A program with rising active users and falling returning users is churning in place, replacing users rather than keeping them.

**Three: depth per active user.** Actions or sessions per active user per week. Activity counts tell you how many people showed up. Depth tells you whether the tool became part of the work or a tab someone opens on Monday. Depth combined with the habitual rate separates a wide shallow program (many users, thin use) from a narrow deep one (fewer users, real dependence).

Then the pair that keeps the three honest. A **value line** for one named workflow, measured the same way every quarter, with the method written down ("proposal first draft cycle time, sampled from the document system, median, n per quarter"). And a **quality counter-metric**: override rate, edit distance, rework rate, or escalation rate on that same workflow. Speed that damages quality is not progress, and a quarterly review without a quality counter-metric will eventually reward the wrong behavior.

The number that does not belong on the steering agenda is "percent active in the last 30 days". It is a coverage measure. It rises when you onboard a wave and falls when you reclaim dormant seats, and both movements can happen while nobody's behavior changes. Keep it in the operating report. Do not put it on the decision slide.

## Reading a plateau without panicking

Aggregate adoption curves flatten. Every one of them, eventually, and often long before the program has failed. An S-curve, the shape that diffusion of innovation models describe, has three parts: a slow start while the first cohorts learn, a steep middle while peer influence and workflow fit do the work, and a top that flattens as the reachable population runs out. The plateau is not the model failing. The plateau is the model finishing.

The problem is that three different situations produce a flat line, and they call for opposite decisions.

**Saturation.** You have covered the people who can use the tool in their work. Remaining non-users are structurally outside the reachable population. Response: stop buying seats, and shift effort from coverage to depth and to new workflows.

**Stall.** Habit formation broke before it completed. Cohorts decay instead of flattening. Response: fix enablement, not licensing.

**Noise plateau.** The level is stable and the trendline looks flat because quarterly aggregates hide weekly variation. Response: nothing, but check the composition of the base.

You can tell them apart with three diagnostics, and they cost an afternoon.

**Fan the cohorts out.** Plot retention curves for the February, May, and August waves on one chart. Curves that rise toward a similar asymptote indicate saturation. Curves that start at the same height and then descend faster in later waves indicate enablement debt: your training got worse as you scaled. Curves that flatten lower than earlier ones indicate the later cohorts have harder jobs, which is a scoping fact, not a performance failure.

**Recount the reachable population.** Divide habitual users by the reachable population, not the licensed population. A program reporting 43 percent against 620 licenses may be at 71 percent of the 375 people whose jobs can use the tool today. That reframing is not a trick if the denominator was wrong from the start. It is a correction, and it should be presented as one, with the earlier figure restated.

**Ask the non-users one question.** Not a survey. A conversation with six of them, chosen at random from the reachable population. The useful question is not "why don't you use it" but "walk me through the last task you did where it would have helped, and tell me what you did instead". The answers separate lack of fit from lack of skill from lack of time, and those three have different owners.

A fourth diagnostic for the impatient: compare this quarter to the same quarter last year, if you have it. Most AI programs do not yet, which is exactly why the quarterly habit matters. Every quarter you measure properly is a baseline you will need in two years.

## Separating seasonal noise from a real decline

When the number drops, run these six checks in this order and stop at the first one that explains it. Order matters, because the cheap explanations are also the most common, and a program that opens with a strategy debate about a data defect wastes a quarter of goodwill.

**Check composition.** Did a wave launch, a reorganisation, an acquisition, or a wave of hiring change who is in the base? A 900-person company that hires 90 people in a quarter has diluted every percentage it reports, without anyone behaving differently.

**Check the window and the delay.** Confirm the comparison uses the same window length and that neither period straddles a data delay. A month of reporting against a 28-day window with a six-day lag will produce phantom declines in the first week of every quarter.[^2]

**Check the instrument.** Did the definition change, did the vendor change a metric, is there a known defect? This is not theoretical: Microsoft's metric documentation records a computation issue that understated specific Copilot metrics, including Generate email draft and Summarize email thread activity plus Copilot assisted hours and value, from June 1, 2025 through February 18, 2026, with a fix from February 18, 2026 and historical data to be restored.[^3] An organization reading that series in January 2026 would have seen a real decline in a number that was never measured correctly. Before you explain a fall with behavior, rule out arithmetic.

**Check the calendar.** Fiscal year end, budget freeze, holiday weeks, summer, annual audit, a major internal launch. Seasonality is not an excuse, it is an adjustment. State the adjustment and the reason in the same sentence.

**Apply the run chart rules.** On a run chart with the median as the centre line, a shift is six or more consecutive points above or below the median, a trend is five or more consecutive points moving in one direction, and an astronomical point is one so far outside the pattern that it demands a specific explanation.[^1] These rules exist so that a person does not have to decide, week by week, whether a wobbly line means something.

**Apply the control chart rules if you have enough history.** With a proper baseline you can draw limits and use the standard pattern tests, which include eight consecutive points on one side of the centre line, two of three points beyond two sigma, and four of five beyond one sigma.[^4] Know the cost before you adopt them: a plain three-sigma chart signals a false alarm about once every 371 points, and adding the pattern rules raises false alarms to roughly once every 92 points, so you will investigate more often and should plan for it.[^4] For a quarterly review with twelve data points a year, use the rules to decide whether to look, never to decide what to conclude.

Then apply the decision rule that keeps the whole thing sane. **A single quarter with a movement gets a note. Two consecutive quarters with a sustained shift, plus a mechanism you can name, gets a decision.** If you cannot name the mechanism, you have a hypothesis and a chart, not a finding.

## The two headline numbers, and what they do not tell you

A lesson about measurement has to apply its own standard to the numbers it quotes, and the two most quoted numbers in this field are both weaker than their repetition suggests.

Gartner's forecast that at least 30 percent of generative AI projects would be abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025 was a prediction, made in July 2024, based on poor data quality, inadequate risk controls, escalating costs, or unclear business value as the causes.[^5] It is a forecast about a population of projects, which means it says nothing about yours. The same press release contains the flattering counterpart, and it carries the same caveat: a survey of 822 business leaders conducted between September and November 2023, in which earlier adopters self-reported average improvements of 15.8 percent revenue, 15.2 percent cost savings, and 22.6 percent productivity.[^5] Self-reported, from organizations that had already succeeded, averaged across use cases. Useful as a reference range. Not usable as your business case.

The MIT NANDA report is the other one: 95 percent of organizations getting zero return from generative AI, with about 5 percent of pilots producing rapid revenue acceleration. The research behind it was based on 150 interviews with leaders, a survey of 350 employees, and an analysis of 300 public deployments.[^6] That is a substantial piece of work and a small sample for a claim about the global population of enterprises, which is why the finding is best read as a description of the organizations studied rather than a failure rate for yours.

The lesson is not that these numbers are wrong. The lesson is that the question "what is the denominator" applies to everything, including the numbers you use to frighten or reassure your own board. When you present your quarterly figure, present the window, the base, the habit threshold, and the known defects in the same breath. You will be believed more, not less.

## Worked example: Diego's four quarters

Diego runs the AI program at a 900-person logistics company. He has 620 licenses, and after a scoping exercise with each department head he counts 375 people in the reachable population: back office, customer service, sales, and the planning team. The other 245 licensed people are drivers and warehouse staff whose tools are mobile scanners and who were licensed in the first enthusiastic month. This is a constructed example, and the numbers are illustrative rather than drawn from a client engagement.

He reports four numbers a quarter to the steering group: habitual use rate against the reachable population, week-over-week returning rate, depth per active user per week, and median cycle time for one named workflow (customer complaint resolution, from the ticket system).

- Q1: 31 percent habitual (116 of 375), 68 percent returning week over week, 5.4 actions per active user, complaint cycle time 3.8 days
- Q2: 39 percent habitual (146), 73 percent returning, 6.1 actions, 3.4 days
- Q3: 41 percent habitual (154), 72 percent returning, 6.0 actions, 3.3 days
- Q4: 38 percent habitual (143), 64 percent returning, 5.2 actions, 3.6 days

The Q4 column looks like a collapse, and the first version of Diego's slide said so. Then he ran the checks.

**Composition** explained part of it. A November reorganisation moved 60 people from customer service into a regional operations team that uses a different workflow, and 40 of them dropped out of the reachable population without changing their behavior.

**Calendar** explained another part. The company's peak shipping weeks in December leave no slack for learning, and the two holiday weeks always show a dip. The run chart of weekly active users shows the same dip in the previous December, at a lower level.

**Instrument** explained the rest. Two of the four metrics he was reading had been changed mid-year by the platform: a usage report was redefined, and the depth number was pulled from a dashboard whose window differs from the admin center's. Neither change was announced to him, and both were visible only because he kept the definitions in a versioned sheet next to the numbers.[^2]

What Q4 actually showed was a plateau, not a decline. Habitual rate held between 38 and 41 percent for three quarters. Fanned out, the cohort curves told the rest: the February cohort climbed to 54 percent habitual and flattened, the May cohort reached 47 percent, and the August cohort reached 39 percent and was still climbing at the end of the year. Later cohorts were improving but starting lower, which is a training and workflow-fit problem, not a saturation problem.

The decision that came out of the review was therefore narrow and unglamorous. No new licenses for two quarters, because the reachable population had 232 non-users and the cheapest coverage gains were already taken. Instead: two more named workflows for the customer service team, a rebuilt day-one session for new cohorts (the August gap was traced to a kickoff that had been cut from 90 minutes to 20), and one metric sheet with definitions, windows, and owners, so that the next instrument change is caught in a week rather than a quarter.

That is what a quarterly cadence buys. Not fewer numbers. Fewer wrong decisions made confidently.

## Failure modes to design against

Reporting an adoption percentage with no denominator and no window. Anyone who cannot answer "of whom, over what period" in one sentence has not measured anything.

Changing the definition mid-year and comparing across the change. Keep a versioned definitions sheet with a date and an owner for every metric, and treat a definition change as a series break, not an improvement.

Comparing quarters with different window lengths because the dashboard default moved. Pin the window in the report template.

Celebrating a launch spike as adoption, then explaining its absence for the next six weeks. Report cohort curves alongside the aggregate so the spike is visible as a spike.

Reading a saturation plateau as a stall and responding with more training, more licenses, or a new tool. Saturation calls for depth and new workflows. Stalls call for enablement. The two remedies are not interchangeable.

Reading a stall as saturation and declaring victory. If the returning rate is falling while the habitual rate is flat, people are leaving as fast as they arrive.

Reviewing quarterly but reacting weekly. The point of the cadence is that most weekly movements are noise. If you have a quarterly review and a weekly panic, you have the cost of both and the benefit of neither.

Measuring activity only. A program that can prove people clicked and cannot show what changed in a workflow will lose the budget conversation to a program that can.

## Try it: the 90-minute quarterly adoption review

Block 90 minutes, once a quarter, and put it on the calendar for the next four quarters now.

**Minutes 0 to 20: assemble the sheet.** Four columns (habitual rate, returning rate, depth, value line) and three metadata fields per metric: definition, window, owner. If a metric has no owner, it does not go on the slide.

**Minutes 20 to 45: fan the cohorts.** One chart, all cohorts, retention over twelve weeks. Write one sentence about what the shape says: converging, descending, or starting lower.

**Minutes 45 to 65: run the six checks on any movement.** Composition, window, instrument, calendar, run chart rules, control chart rules. Write down which check explained the movement, or write "unexplained" and leave it unexplained. An honest blank beats a story.

**Minutes 65 to 85: write the one-page memo.** Current levels, the movement, the explanation, the decision requested, and the counter-metric that would tell you the decision was wrong. One page. The steering group will read one page.

**Minutes 85 to 90: set next quarter's hypothesis.** One sentence, falsifiable, with the number that would prove it: "If the rebuilt kickoff works, the November cohort's twelve-week habitual rate should land above 45 percent, and the returning rate should stay above 70."

## What's next

You can now measure a program the way an operator should: over quarters, with named denominators, cohort curves behind every aggregate, and a checklist that protects you from calling arithmetic a behavior change. What you have not dealt with is the ceiling that sits underneath all of it. A general assistant sitting beside your systems in a browser tab has a hard limit on how much work it can absorb, because every use has to be carried there by a person copying text in and out. Lesson 34 takes on integration: where AI plugs into your existing systems, what the difference is between a connector, an embedded assistant, and an agent that acts on records, what each one demands from your data and your security posture, and which decisions about integration belong to you as the program owner rather than to IT alone.
