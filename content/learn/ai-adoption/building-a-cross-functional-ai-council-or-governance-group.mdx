Lesson 29 took one proven use case and gave it a path to the whole organization: phases, gates, fixed assumptions, and a portfolio view. All of that work rides on decisions, and every decision currently has one owner. That owner is you. The next step in building the program is to stop being that single point of truth, because a governance discipline exercised by one person, no matter how well designed, does not hold once the company is moving at scale.

This lesson makes the program the organization's. You will learn what an AI council is, what it is not, why most such bodies produce ceremony instead of decisions, and which membership, decision rights, and reporting lines actually make one work. You will build the council that owns the roadmap and the health reviews from here forward, and you will know how to keep it from becoming the thing that slows the company down instead of the thing that moves it forward.

## Define the terms before you argue

An **AI council** (or AI governance group, or AI steering committee; names vary) is a standing cross-functional body that owns the decisions about how AI is developed, deployed, evaluated, and governed across an organization. It is the human accountability structure that sits underneath the technology and gives someone, in the end, the authority to say no.[^1] Its job is not to run AI projects. It is to set the rules, review the use cases, and hold the line when something goes wrong.

The word "council" hides a trap, so separate the two things people usually mean. A **steering committee** sets direction: the vision, the values, the policy, the budget, and the overall roadmap. An **operating body** (sometimes called an implementation committee, or review committee) carries that direction out: it reviews specific use cases, routes them by risk, and tracks outcomes.[^2] Small organizations fold both into one group. Larger ones split them, because the people who should set direction are rarely the ones with time to review every pilot. Know which one you are building, because the required membership and cadence differ.

![A steering committee sets direction (vision, values, policy, budget, roadmap) while an operating body carries it out (reviews use cases, routes them by risk, tracks outcomes); small organizations fold both into one group, larger ones split them.](</diagrams/building-a-cross-functional-ai-council-or-governance-group/1-steering-vs-operating.png>)

The two most important terms are the ones almost no one defines: **decision rights** and **escalation**. Decision rights say who can approve a thing, who can pause it, and who can stop it, and they are useless unless they are written down before an incident happens. Escalation is the path a problem follows when the people closest to it cannot resolve it, and it only exists if it ends somewhere with real authority. An AI council without decision rights and a working escalation path is not governance. It is a meeting series.

## The governance theater trap

Here is the question every serious AI leader eventually asks, and it sounds insultingly simple: when an AI model does something it should not do, who in the organization has the authority to stop it?[^1] Not who gets notified. Not who files the incident report. Who can walk into a meeting and say, we are shutting this down, and have that decision stick.

Most organizations cannot answer the question, and the gap matters because it separates governance from theater. Advisory groups and ethics boards are everywhere and are genuinely necessary: chief AI ethics officers, data governance councils, responsible AI teams. But purely advisory bodies can flag, they can recommend, they can escalate. What they generally cannot do is hit the stop button, because the real authority sits somewhere else, often with a product leader whose incentive structure makes a governance flag an afterthought rather than a mandate.[^1] The infrastructure of governance has proliferated: registries, classification systems, monitoring dashboards, risk councils, policies. What is missing is the governor, the person with the standing and the authority to act.

A useful image: a fire alarm is not a fire department. You can wire every room, change every battery, route every alert to a beautiful dashboard, and still have the house burn down because nobody picked up the hose.[^1] The council is the fire department, and it only works if its members are not structurally dependent on the people whose products they might have to stop.

This is the design constraint the rest of the lesson serves. An AI council does not exist to make deployment easier. It exists to make deployment defensible, and defensibility requires that the person who can say no does not report to the person who benefits from the yes. Adobe, running one of the most mature enterprise governance programs there is, built exactly this: a federated model with a named owner for every AI system, plus a centralized steering committee with escalation authority, reporting into the trust and security organization rather than the product teams.[^1] That reporting line is the whole point. The person whose job it is to say no answers to someone whose success does not depend on shipping every product on schedule.

## Why it must be cross-functional

The instinct when standing up governance is to hand it to the technology or legal people, because AI feels like their problem. It is not, and MIT's guidance is blunt: the issue is too large for any single corporate domain.[^3] AI use cuts across operations, IT, legal, risk, data, privacy, finance, product, and compliance, and a council that omits any of the veto points will make decisions that die in the room it forgot to invite.

The functions matter because each one sees a different face of the same use case. The business owner sees the opportunity. Legal and privacy see what happens when sensitive data goes into a tool. Security sees the threat surface. Finance sees what it costs and whether anyone can prove it earns out. A cross-functional council exists to make those views collide in a room on purpose, so the decision happens before the deployment, not during an incident. The people who owe you a straight answer are the ones who share accountability for the outcome, not the ones who just want the meeting to be comfortable.

There is also a board-level dimension that most mid-size programs skip. Beyond the management-level council, organizations should consider a dedicated board-level AI committee with formal responsibility for oversight, because boards carry legal responsibility for mission-critical risks and increasingly need a named structure to point to.[^3] If your company has a board, part of the council's job is to feed it a report. If it does not, that reporting line goes to the senior leadership who own the risk.

## What the membership actually looks like

A practical cross-functional council is smaller than people expect. Six to nine standing members is usually enough to cover every veto point while still being able to meet on a rhythm and decide.[^2] The core seats are a business sponsor who chairs, a program lead who runs the day to day, and representatives from the functions that can kill a deployment: legal or compliance, privacy or data, security, finance or procurement, and one at-large member who carries the view of the teams doing the work.

Each member needs a defined role in approving, pausing, or rejecting a use case and in accepting its risk. A charter should name who can approve a pilot, who can pause a deployment, who can accept a risk, and who can escalate a decision to the executive sponsor or the board.[^2] Without that, the council becomes a discussion club: everyone has an opinion, nobody has a job.

## Minimum viable governance, so the council does not strangle the company

There is a failure mode on the other side, and it is the reason most councils are quietly hated. MIT CISR's research on minimum viable governance tells the story of FinCo, a global financial services firm that reacted to widespread unsanctioned AI use by building governance in all five domains: principles, policies, people, processes, and platforms. It was thorough by any conventional measure, and it broke the company. The enterprise policy took close to a year to write and was out of date the day it was finished. A low-risk agent prototype involving no sensitive data took six months to get through review. Employees needed sign-off from both legal and the review committee just to access the firm's own secure platform, which had been designed for safe experimentation in the first place. Frustrated teams went back to unsanctioned tools, and the shadow AI the governance was meant to stop spread wider than before.[^2]

The lesson is not that governance is bad. It is that governance calibrated for technologies with fifty-year life cycles does not work when the technology transforms every eighteen months.[^2] What FinCo needed, and what it built too late, is minimum viable governance: the least amount of governance required to manage risk while still letting the organization sense and seize opportunity. MIT CISR identifies four characteristics that make governance survive contact with the technology, and each one is a design test for your council.

First, be **structurally agile**. Governance must be introduced, adjusted, or retired as conditions change, and review intensity should match the risk of the proposal. The strongest organizations classify initiatives into tiers: high-risk use cases get full committee oversight, mid-tier ones proceed on approved platforms with preconfigured controls, and low-risk work proceeds autonomously with approved tools, routed by a simple intake form reviewed asynchronously.[^2] They also build in a look-back, revisiting an initiative after a fixed period to check whether its risk level changed. Do not review a low-risk note-taking pilot with the same ceremony as a hiring or credit model.

![Review intensity routes to risk: high-risk use cases get full committee oversight with a look-back date, mid-tier ones proceed on approved platforms with preconfigured controls, and low-risk work proceeds autonomously with approved tools via a simple intake form reviewed asynchronously.](</diagrams/building-a-cross-functional-ai-council-or-governance-group/2-risk-tier-routing.png>)

Second, build oversight in rather than bolt it on. **Trustworthy by design** means the platform itself enforces the rules: it logs every interaction, filters for policy violations, and flags anomalies, so the council can shift from gatekeeping before action to monitoring and intervening as needed.[^2] The exact opposite failure is gating a platform explicitly built for safe experiments behind layers of sign-off, which is what strangled FinCo. When controls live in the tool, the council reviews what matters instead of rubber-stamping access.

Third, make governance **integrated end to end**. A council that meets monthly is not a governance system on its own, because the risk lives in the day to day, between meetings. The highest-performing organizations embed risk and compliance people directly into the teams doing the work, so governance is part of the initiative from day one rather than an audit applied at the end.[^2] Your council should have real liaisons with the teams building AI, not a wall.

Fourth, and this is the one almost nobody gets, be **opportunity-sensitive**. Governance has a cost of inaction, and the riskiest posture is one where every committee is oriented only toward finding problems. As the governance leader at a large financial services firm put it, the biggest risk is moving too slowly, because a slow and cumbersome oversight process creates a vacuum that other actors, less careful ones, will fill.[^2] The council's job is to tell the organization yes, safely, more than it is to tell it no. Track time to decision alongside incidents, and watch both.

![Minimum viable governance rests on four design tests: structurally agile (governance adjusts as conditions change), trustworthy by design (the platform enforces the rules), integrated end to end (risk people embedded in the teams), and opportunity-sensitive (track time to decision alongside incidents).](</diagrams/building-a-cross-functional-ai-council-or-governance-group/3-mvg-four-characteristics.png>)

## Worked example: Marina stands up the council

Marina runs AI adoption at the professional-services firm Lessons 28 and 29 introduced. Her support-automation pilot has scaled, the portfolio has four more use cases queued behind it, and she has realized that every one of those decisions is hers. She decides this month to build the council, and she does it the way the lesson prescribes.

She starts with the problem, not the charter. Before she books a single meeting, she writes down the question she refuses to be unable to answer: when one of these systems does something it should not, who can stop it, and does that person answer to someone who does not need the deployment to succeed. [^1] The answer determines her membership and her reporting line, so she sets those first.

She names six people: herself as the program lead and chair, the CTO or their delegate for the technology and risk view, the general counsel for legal and procurement, the head of security, the data and privacy lead, and the finance lead who owns the adoption budget. She adds one at-large operations member so the people who actually use the tools have a voice. The council reports to the executive sponsor, not to the product or technology teams who benefit from shipping, so the person who can say no has standing.[^1]

She writes a one-page charter that names decision rights before anything else: she, as the program lead, can approve low-risk pilots on the approved tool set. The full council approves mid- and high-tier use cases on the portfolio gate from Lesson 29. And the executive sponsor, on the council's recommendation, holds the stop authority for any system: the authority, the blessing, and the standing to say we are shutting this down, without reporting to the person who wants it kept live.[^1]

She calibrates the review to the risk, refusing to replicate FinCo's mistake. Low-risk work on the approved platform routes through a short intake form, reviewed asynchronously, and runs. Mid-tier use cases get a monthly council review against the evidence pack from Lesson 28. High-risk cases, anything touching sensitive data, hiring, credit, health, or autonomous action, get full review before they start, and every one of them gets a look-back date on the calendar so nobody forgets to check whether its risk changed.[^2] She blocks tool access at the platform level, logs what people do, and routes violations to the council, rather than requiring sign-off to touch the approved tool at all.[^2]

Then she does the hardest step. She refuses to let the council become a brake. At every meeting she tracks two numbers side by side: how many risks she caught or escalated, and how long it took a good use case to get a decision.[^2] If the second number creeps up while the first stays flat, she treats the council itself as the problem and fixes the process before the teams in the field start voting with their own shadow tools. The council is not the end of her job. It is the mechanism that lets her job survive contact with the whole company.

## Try it: design your council on one page

Build the AI council for your own organization, now, on a single sheet of paper. Start with the question this lesson is built around: when an AI system misbehaves, who can stop it, and does that person report to someone who does not depend on the deployment succeeding?[^1] Write the name and the reporting line before you write anything else.

Then fill in the rest. List the six to nine seats, and for each one name the role in the decisions: who can approve a pilot, who can pause a deployment, who can accept a risk, and who can escalate to the executive sponsor or board.[^2] Write the review tiers, and what each tier is allowed to do without a full meeting. Decide where the controls live so oversight is built into the platform rather than bolted onto access. And commit to tracking time to decision next to risk incidents from day one, so the council can prove it is helping rather than strangling the company.[^2]

When you finish, test it with a specific case. Take one AI use case you actually have or want, name it, and ask: could this council approve it, and could this council stop it? If you can answer both, you have done the harder, less glamorous work of building the human accountability structure that sits underneath the technology.[^1]

## What's next

You now have the council: the cross-functional membership, the written decision rights, the independent escalation line that gives the stop authority real teeth, and the minimum-viable governance discipline that keeps it from becoming a brake on the business. The program no longer depends on you being the single point of truth. It belongs to the organization.

But a council that governs AI use has a hole in the middle of it if the tools themselves are a mess. Right now, approval is one thing and daily reality is another: people across the company are almost certainly buying, subscribing to, and duplicating AI tools on their own, with no shared standard and no way to know what is approved. The next lesson turns the governance lens on the tooling itself, standardizing approved tools and avoiding the pile-up of overlapping, ungoverned subscriptions. Bring your council charter; it becomes the body that owns the approved-tool list.

For now, hold the two ideas that carry this lesson. Governance without decision rights is theater, which is why the stop authority, and the reporting line that protects it, come first. And governance that only says no will be bypassed, which is why the council must be structurally agile and opportunity-sensitive, measuring time to decision as carefully as it tracks risk.
