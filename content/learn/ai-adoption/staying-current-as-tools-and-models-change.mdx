Every AI program gets the same email eventually. Someone forwards a launch announcement with a line like "this changes everything," and the real question arrives underneath it: should we switch?

There are two ways to answer badly. One is to rewrite the roadmap. The other is to ignore the email. Both come from the same mistake, which is treating market news as either an instruction or noise. It is neither. It is data, and like every other input to a governed program it needs a threshold, an owner, and a date attached to it.

This lesson builds the small machinery that turns vendor news into decisions. You are not going to become an industry analyst. You are going to be able to name what genuinely changes, know how much notice your vendors owe you in writing, run a monthly scan in half an hour, and hold one rule that protects the whole plan: nothing on the roadmap moves between decision dates unless a named trigger fires.

The interplay with the previous lesson is direct. Lesson 35 built a year with a decision calendar and a won't-do list carrying revisit dates. This lesson is how you feed that plan without letting it get rewritten by every release note that lands in your inbox.

## Four things about a supplier can change, and only one is hard

Strip away the branding and there are four changes that can reach your roadmap.

**Capability.** The system can now do something it could not do before. This is the change everyone talks about and the hardest to judge, because published scores measure a vendor's chosen tasks, not yours. A capability change only matters to you when it crosses a line: the assistant can now handle a document type that used to force a human handoff, or it can follow a multi-step procedure without losing the thread.

**Price.** The cost of a unit of work. This moves faster than most people assume and in one direction. Epoch AI measured the cost of achieving a fixed level of AI performance and found it falling about 47 percent per quarter since 2023, or roughly 13 times cheaper per year, faster than electricity, compute, batteries, or DNA sequencing ever fell.[^9] Their more useful finding for planning is about the shape of the decay: the price of a capability falls fastest right after it first appears, at around 66 percent per quarter, and settles to about 32 percent per quarter two years later.[^9] The practical reading is that the thing you priced and rejected eighteen months ago deserves a fresh number, and the thing you rejected last month probably does not.

**Lifecycle.** Your supplier will retire the model your workflow runs on. This is scheduled, routine, and completely unsurprising if you read the calendar. It is a governance failure only when it arrives as news.

**Terms and rules.** What happens to your data, whether it trains a model, which regions it crosses, who the subprocessors are, what the incident history looks like, and which regulatory deadlines apply to your sector. These change quietly in contract updates and policy pages, and they carry more risk than any capability gap.

One thing changes constantly and mostly should not move your plan: where a model sits on a public leaderboard. The 2026 AI Index says it plainly in its technical performance overview. Evaluations are being outpaced by the progress they were built to measure, benchmarks face growing questions about their reliability, and the gap between top models is shrinking. With capability no longer a clear differentiator between the leaders, competitive pressure is shifting toward cost, reliability, and real-world usefulness.[^7] That is not a claim that capability has stopped improving. It is a claim that ranking the top of the market by published scores has stopped telling you much about your own work.

That saturation is measurable, not philosophical. The authors of the MMLU-Pro benchmark wrote that performance on the widely used MMLU benchmark had begun to plateau, making it increasingly difficult to discern real differences between models, and they rebuilt the test specifically to restore that ability. Their own numbers show why a leaderboard gap is a weak signal: model scores moved by 4 to 5 percent depending on nothing but prompt style on MMLU, a sensitivity that fell to about 2 percent on their harder replacement.[^8] If the measurement wobbles more than the improvement, the improvement is not the story.

## Seven terms to settle before you build a watch process

**Release cadence.** How often a supplier ships new versions. It sets how noisy your inbox is, and it is unrelated to how much of that noise matters to you.

**Capability frontier.** The best available performance at a given time, and how quickly that best moves. Epoch AI's Capabilities Index puts the frontier's advance at a linear 14 points per year since reasoning models arrived in September 2024, against 6 points per year beforehand.[^5] Two things follow. First, improvement is real and steep. Second, it arrives at a rate you can plan against: about a year of progress per year, not a step change every Tuesday. The release notes are spiky; the trend line is not.

**Model alias versus pinned version.** A moving name such as the newest version of an assistant, against a dated version you have chosen to stay on. Vendors manage one; you manage the other. Which you allow is a governance decision, and the answer is usually both, deliberately: pinned versions for the workflows you have measured, moving aliases for the ones where being current matters more than being stable.

**Deprecation and retirement.** Two different events. A deprecated model still answers requests but is closed to new customers and has an end date; a retired model fails. Microsoft's catalog spells out the difference: existing deployments keep working through deprecation, and retirement returns an error for every request.[^4] You want to hear about the first and never be surprised by the second.

**Notice window.** The guaranteed minimum period between a retirement announcement and the shutdown. This is the number that decides how much of your planning the vendor controls, and it is published.

**Evaluation set.** A small, owned set of real tasks from your workflows with known-good answers. Twenty to thirty items is enough to be useful.

**Trigger.** A pre-agreed condition that authorizes moving the roadmap between its decision dates. A trigger is what separates a governed response from a reflex.

## Your vendor's contract sets your maximum surprise

The notice windows are public, and they differ by an order of magnitude. Read your own vendors, and treat these three as the shape of the market rather than a recommendation.

OpenAI's API documentation commits to at least 6 months' notice before retiring a generally available model, at least 3 months for specialized variants of those models, and warns that preview models may be retired with much shorter notice, such as 2 weeks. It states outright that preview models should not carry business-critical production workloads unless you can migrate on short notice.[^1]

Anthropic commits to at least 60 days' notice before retirement for publicly released models, and defines the lifecycle in four states: active, legacy, deprecated, retired, with requests to retired models failing.[^2]

Microsoft's catalog is the most explicit of the three, because it has to be: generally available models follow a standard 18-month lifecycle with the retirement date set programmatically at launch, at least 60 days' notice before a generally available retirement, and at least 30 days before a preview model is upgraded or retired. Preview models launch with a not-sooner-than retirement date typically 90 days out, and the official replacement for a GA retirement is selected and declared roughly 90 to 120 days before the retirement date. It also notes a variation most owners never check: generally available Anthropic, DeepSeek, Fireworks, and Mistral models in that same catalog follow a 12-month lifecycle instead of the standard 18-month one.[^4]

![Bar chart of guaranteed notice before a model stops answering: OpenAI six months for generally available models, three months for specialized variants, two weeks for preview models, Anthropic and Microsoft sixty days for a generally available retirement, and Microsoft thirty days before a preview model is upgraded or retired](</diagrams/staying-current-as-tools-and-models-change/2-notice-windows.png>)

Two lessons come out of those numbers.

The first is that a retirement is not evidence of bad faith, and you should be able to explain why to your leadership. Anthropic states the reason in its own commitment post: keeping models available for inference scales roughly linearly in cost and complexity with the number of models served, so retiring older ones is what makes room for new ones. The same post commits to preserving the weights of publicly released models for at least the lifetime of the company, which is the closest thing to an archival guarantee in the market.[^3]

The second is operational. Four months of notice is a scheduling problem, not a crisis, and the difference between the two is whether the date sits on a calendar. Put the migration in an existing change slot rather than inventing a new project, and set your decision date a month after the announcement instead of a week before the deadline. Notice windows are long enough to plan inside; the failure is never the window, it is the silence until the last fortnight.

There is also a cheap instrument most organizations never touch. OpenAI exposes a shutdown date field on each model returned by its models endpoint, so a retirement can be detected by a script rather than a newsletter.[^1] Anthropic's documentation walks through exporting your usage from the console and reading it broken down by API key and model, which is a direct answer to the question of what your organization is actually calling.[^2] For a non-technical owner this is the single highest-value hour in the lesson: an export tells you which of your workflows are standing on a model with an end date, and which are already on something nobody remembers approving.

## Six signal classes, and a threshold for each

A watch process is only as good as its triage. Sort what you see into six classes, and agree in advance which of them authorize action on their own.

**Lifecycle notices.** Always act. There is no threshold to debate, because the vendor has already set the date. The default response is to schedule a migration into an existing change slot and confirm the replacement on your evaluation set before the cutover.

**Price changes on your unit of work.** Threshold. Act when the cost of a defined unit of work moves past a number you set in advance, such as a quarter of its current cost, or when it crosses the figure that would flip a previously rejected item's business case.

**Capability step changes, measured on your own set.** Threshold. Act when your evaluation set's pass rate improves enough to unlock something specific, which is exactly what the revisit dates in your won't-do list exist to catch.

**Quiet regressions behind a moving alias.** Threshold. Vendors improve on average, not necessarily on your task. If you allow a moving alias anywhere, your evaluation set is the only thing that will notice a drop.

**Terms, data, and security changes.** Always act. Retention, training use, subprocessors, region, and incident posture are not ranking questions.

**Regulatory and contractual deadlines.** Always act. These arrive with dates and they do not negotiate.

Three of the six carry no threshold, and all three of them are boring. That is the design. The classes that create excitement in a meeting are the ones with thresholds attached, precisely so that excitement has to clear a bar before it can spend a change slot.

## Build the smallest evaluation set that can detect a change

Everything above depends on one artifact, and it is not a benchmark.

Your evaluation set is twenty to thirty real tasks pulled from the workflow you care about, each with a known-good answer or a clear accept-or-reject standard, kept somewhere the people running them cannot see the expected answers in the prompt. Run it monthly. Record two numbers: the share of tasks that pass, and the cost per task.

That pair of numbers answers the only question the market can pose to you: did the change help our work, and what does it cost now? A published benchmark cannot answer it, for the reasons the 2026 AI Index gives, and neither can a vendor case study.

Be honest about what this instrument is. Thirty tasks is not statistics. It is a smoke detector. Its job is to catch a large regression or a large gain, and to give you a defensible sentence when someone asks why you did or did not switch. If you need a ranking between two close models, you need a hundred tasks and a scoring rubric, and at that point you are running an evaluation, not a watch process.

## The watch log, and the one rule that protects the plan

Market news needs somewhere to live that is not the roadmap. That place is a log with eight columns:

Date. Source, meaning the vendor's own page rather than the newsletter that mentioned it. What changed, in one sentence. Class, from the six above. Affected workflows, by name. Whether the threshold was met. The decision, including "no action" as a legitimate entry. And the owner with a revisit date.

Then the rule, which is the whole point of the artifact. **A logged item may not change the roadmap before its revisit date unless its class is one of the three that always act.** Everything else waits for the quarterly decision date already on your calendar.

This is the mechanism that converts an inbox into governance. It also gives you a graceful answer to the forwarded email, and the answer is welcome because it is not a refusal. The item is logged, the date is set, and the person who sent it can see exactly when it will be considered.

## A cadence that costs about an hour a month

**Monthly, thirty to forty-five minutes.** One named person scans the six classes: the vendor deprecation pages, the pricing page, your usage export, your evaluation set results, the terms or trust page, and the regulatory calendar for your sector. Entries go in the log. Anything in an always-act class escalates the same week.

**Quarterly, at a decision date you already have.** Run the evaluation set, decide on every log entry whose revisit date has arrived, and spend the resulting work against change capacity rather than against enthusiasm. This is where last lesson's absorption budget earns its keep.

**Annually.** The roadmap refresh, which is where a year of logged decisions turns into a sequenced plan.

Budget the watch as a run-lane item. The one number worth quoting to a finance reviewer is that the whole process costs about a dozen hours a year plus the migrations it schedules, and its purpose is to make sure the migrations are scheduled rather than improvised.

## Worked example: Owen's second quarter

Owen runs AI for the 1,900-person commercial insurer from the previous lesson. The claims document-intake pilot is running with 60 adjusters, underwriting has a small submission-triage proof in flight, and the build lane delivered training, a data dictionary, and an agent governance update in the first quarter. The figures here are illustrative and checkable, and the method is the real thing.

Four pieces of market news arrive between April and June.

The first is a retirement notice. The assistant platform behind the claims pilot announces that the model version it uses is being retired, with four months of notice. Owen's response takes fifteen minutes. He confirms the notice window in the vendor's own documentation, checks that the recommended replacement can run read-only against the same document store with mirrored permissions, and schedules the migration as a single change unit in the third quarter, where claims operations has room. He sets a decision date thirty days out to approve the replacement, not at the deadline. No roadmap change, and the migration is inside the capacity budget instead of on top of it.

The second is a capability release. The vendor ships a newer model, and the published scores are better across the board. Owen runs the monthly evaluation set on the twenty-two real claim documents in it. Pass rate moves from 78 percent to 81 percent, and cost per document barely moves. The pilot's kill criteria are about sampled extraction accuracy above 85 percent and weekly active use above 60 percent, and the binding constraint is the permission model and the training, not the model. Decision: logged, no action, revisit at the fourth-quarter decision date.

The third is a competitor announcement in the trade press about agentic claims handling. It names no dates, no permission model, and no failure handling. Class: capability claim with no threshold met. Owen logs it with a revisit date in the fourth quarter and writes one line of reasoning, which is that the same three conditions that sent agentic adjudication to won't do at the annual plan still hold: the oversight model is not mature, the permission model cannot support delegated authority, and the failure cost is a wrongly denied claim.

The fourth is a price change, and this one crosses a threshold Owen set in advance. The vendor cuts the price of the model class behind the underwriting triage proof by more than 30 percent in the quarter, which is past the 25 percent line he wrote into the watch log in January. Re-run against the new unit cost, the business case for extending triage from 25 underwriters to 60 now clears, and underwriting has change capacity left in the fourth quarter. Owen schedules the extension, puts the decision on the calendar, and can point at the number that changed rather than at the announcement that contained it.

One decision from four events, taken on evidence, inside a capacity slot, in a quarter where the roadmap was not rewritten. The watch log closes the quarter with four entries: one migration scheduled, one extension approved, two deferrals with dates and reasons. Total meeting time, under an hour across three months.

The counter-example is the program that holds no evaluation set. It receives the same four items and switches twice, because a benchmark score and a competitor's press release are the only evidence available to it. Two migrations consume the change capacity that the underwriting extension needed, and nobody can demonstrate that either switch improved anything, because there was never an instrument that could tell.

## Try it: your first scan, in one sitting

Work through these in order. Most owners finish in about ninety minutes, and the first two steps are the ones that change behavior immediately.

Pull the deprecation page for every vendor in your stack, from the vendor's own documentation rather than a summary. Copy every date into a single calendar. If a vendor publishes no notice window, ask the account team for it in writing, because an unstated window is your risk, not theirs.

Export your usage from each vendor console, or ask whoever holds the billing admin to run it, and read it by model. Circle every model that appears on a deprecation list. This is the step that usually produces a surprise, and the surprise is better found by you than by your users.

Write down the notice window and the models behind your top three workflows. That sentence is what tells you whether you are planning or reacting.

Build the evaluation set. Twenty to thirty real tasks, known-good answers held separately, and two numbers you can recompute: pass rate and cost per task. Run it once today to get a baseline.

Open the watch log with its eight columns and put one entry in it now: the most recent thing someone forwarded you. Log it, classify it, set a revisit date, and notice how much easier it is to answer the email when the answer is a date rather than an opinion.

Fix the cadence. A thirty-minute monthly scan on a named person's calendar, and a quarterly decision date that already exists in the roadmap. If nobody owns it yet, you own it.

## What's next

You now have a way to watch the market without joining it: four things that can change, six signal classes with thresholds, a vendor notice window you can quote, a small evaluation set that measures your own work, and a log whose rule is that nothing moves the plan early.

What that leaves is the rest of the program. A watch process covers the outside world, and the thing that quietly drifts inside your organization is everything else: access that was never revoked, licences nobody uses, costs that crept, incidents nobody recorded, and decisions that came due without a hearing. Lesson 37 is about running a periodic AI health review, the recurring checkup that pulls your metrics, your risks, your access, your spend, and your watch log into one sitting, and turns the deferrals you have been collecting into answers.
