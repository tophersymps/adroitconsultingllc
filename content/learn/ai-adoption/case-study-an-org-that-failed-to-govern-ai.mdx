# Lesson 39: Case study: an org that failed to govern AI

Lesson 38 showed you an organization that scaled AI well. This lesson is the mirror image, and the point of pairing them is that the two organizations are not different kinds of company. Same size bracket, same budget, same vendors, roughly the same starting point. The difference is four decisions that were never made.

This one hurts more to write, and it should hurt more to read. Failure cases in this subject tend to get dressed up as technology problems, which lets everyone in the room off the hook. What follows is a governance failure in a company that was buying perfectly good tools and running them competently at the team level. Nobody in this story was reckless or dishonest, and several people were diligent in the narrow sense of doing the job they had been given. The program still cost more than it earned.

As with lesson 38, the organization here is a composite built from published accounts and our own work, and the figures are illustrative and checkable. Unlike lesson 38, this one has real, named, cited precedents behind it, because the failures described here have already happened to real companies and left real orders and real penalties behind. You will meet three of them.

## Define the terms

**Model inventory.** The list of AI systems your organization actually operates, with an owner, a data classification, a support path, and a decommission date for each. It is the artifact every other control depends on.

**Shadow AI.** AI use that nobody approved and nobody is tracking, usually because the approved alternative did not solve the problem in front of the person using it.

**AI washing.** Claiming AI capability in marketing, filings, or a sales conversation that the organization cannot actually demonstrate. It is treated as a misrepresentation in financial services and increasingly elsewhere.

**Unsupported representation.** Anything a system you operate tells a customer, a patient, or a regulator that a human in your organization would have to stand behind. Responsibility for the statement does not move because a machine made it.

**Incident disclosure.** The named path and time limit for telling someone inside or outside the organization that an AI system produced harm, disclosed data, or gave advice that was acted on.

**Concentration risk.** The exposure that comes from many workflows running on the same model or the same vendor at once, so that a single change, outage, or defect lands everywhere simultaneously.

## The org, and where it started

Dana is the CIO of a 1,200-person specialty foods manufacturer. She was handed AI eighteen months ago on top of fourteen other responsibilities, which is the most common arrangement in mid-market companies and the first structural problem in this story. There was no dedicated owner, no budget line, and no meeting.

Six months in, the picture actually looked good, and this is the part people skip when they tell failure stories. Assisted order entry was live with 210 customer service representatives at 88 percent weekly active use. Two forecasting pilots were showing promise. Total spend was modest. The CEO mentioned AI in a town hall and got applause.

Three things were missing, and none of them showed up in any metric Dana was reporting.

Nobody had ever built an inventory. Dana could name the three tools she had approved. The real number, once her team ran a scan of expense records and browser telemetry nine months later, was 47 distinct AI tools, 31 of them unapproved. Two of the unapproved tools were being used by people handling consumer complaint data. Nobody knew that on the day it started. Nobody knew it for nine months.

Nobody owned the customer-facing assistant. The marketing team had added one to the website under a small content budget, and it answered questions about policy using a knowledge base that had last been refreshed in a meeting nobody in operations attended. It was not on Dana's list because it was not an operations system. The company had a document about who owns what, and the assistant did not appear in it.

Nobody had written down what the program could claim. The sales team was enthusiastic, the marketing team was under pressure to differentiate, and there was no rule that said any AI claim had to be checked against something real before it shipped.

## The five failures, in the order they compounded

The order matters here, because each one made the next one harder to see.

**Failure one: no inventory.** This is the root of everything below it, and it is the least exciting control in the course. Without a list, none of the other questions have an address. You cannot review access to systems you have not counted. You cannot retire a system nobody has recorded. You cannot classify data in a workflow you do not know exists. The NIST AI RMF playbook puts the outcome in the Govern function as a single line: mechanisms are in place to inventory AI systems and are resourced according to organizational risk priorities, with decommissioning handled under a separate outcome requiring processes and procedures for phasing out AI systems safely.[^1] That is the whole control. In Dana's company, 31 unapproved tools were doing work for months, and the reason nobody could answer a question about them is that nobody had a list to answer from.

**Failure two: access granted by exception, never recertified.** The manufacturer had a working access process for its core systems, with annual reviews. AI tools bypassed it, because most of them were bought on a card by a team lead, which meant access was effectively self-service. When Dana's team finally reviewed the assistant platform, they found 34 people with administrator rights, of whom nine had left the company or moved departments, and two had shared credentials with a contractor who was no longer engaged. Excess access is a textbook finding in identity governance for a reason: it produces both compromise and audit failure, and the standard remedy is a recurring review whose frequency you choose.[^2] In this company the review did not exist, so the frequency was never chosen, and the default frequency was never.

**Failure three: the assistant answered a policy question.** This is where the composite meets the real world. A wholesale customer asked the website assistant whether seasonal price protections ran from order date or delivery date. The assistant said protections extend 60 days past delivery. Company policy said order date, and the two answers were worth about $19,000 on the customer's next order. The customer, reasonably, quoted the assistant back at their account manager and asked for the adjustment. The company refused, pointing at the contract. The relationship went bad enough that the customer's procurement team escalated it as a formal dispute, and the resolution landed at roughly $40,000 in credits and concessions on a $180,000 order.

The instinct in the room was that this could not be the company's problem, because a machine had said it, and the machine was a third-party product. That instinct was tested in front of a decision-maker in Canada in 2024 and did not survive. In Moffatt v. Air Canada, a passenger relied on the airline's website chatbot, which told them bereavement fares could be claimed retroactively, which was not the airline's policy. Air Canada argued it could not be held liable for information provided by its chatbot, effectively treating the chatbot as a separate legal entity responsible for its own actions. The tribunal member called that a remarkable submission and rejected it on the plain ground that the chatbot is part of the airline's website. The decision is blunt about the consequence. It makes no difference whether the information comes from a static page or a chatbot, and the airline was found to have failed to take reasonable care to ensure its chatbot was accurate.[^3] The airline was ordered to pay the passenger $812.02, made up of $650.88 in damages, prejudgment interest, and tribunal fees. Tiny amount, enormous precedent: the statement your system makes is your statement.

**Failure four: a claim nobody could support.** Six weeks after the price-protection dispute, the manufacturer's marketing team published a page describing how the company "uses machine learning to guarantee allergen-safe substitutions in every order." The quality team could not demonstrate it. Substitution decisions were still made by a human following a documented allergen protocol, and the model they were using had no role in the final check. The claim was live for eleven days before someone on the quality team read it.

The relevant precedent is not obscure. In March 2024 the SEC announced settled charges against two investment advisers, Delphia and Global Predictions, for making false and misleading statements about their purported use of AI, with the firms agreeing to pay $400,000 in total civil penalties. The enforcement director's statement is the sentence to pin above your marketing team's desk: if you claim to use AI in your investment processes, you need to ensure that your representations are not false or misleading.[^4] Read that as a pattern rather than a sector rule. Claims about capability are testable, and the test is whether you can produce the evidence.

**Failure five: no incident path.** Here is the sequence that ended the program's honeymoon. A category manager, trying to speed up a negotiation brief, pasted the company's full supplier cost schedule into a public assistant. They did not think of it as a disclosure, because the tool felt like a text box. Nobody had told them otherwise, which is a weakness in the tool policy, and nobody had given them a safe alternative, which is the actual cause.

The manager told their director, who told a colleague in IT, who asked Dana's team whether it needed to be reported. Nobody could answer that in under a minute, because there was no defined path. It took eleven days to establish who inside the company needed to know, whether any customer or supplier obligation applied, and whether anything had to go in writing to anyone outside. The generative AI profile attached to the AI risk management framework treats incident disclosure as one of only four primary considerations that shape its suggested actions, alongside governance, content provenance, and pre-deployment testing, and it defines risk as the combination of how likely an event is with how bad it would be.[^5] Eleven days is not a disclosure path. It is a policy absence with a calendar attached.

The same profile names something that had quietly been building underneath all five failures. It calls the risk algorithmic monocultures: when many actors rely on the same algorithm for consequential decisions, correlated failures get more likely, because a single model's weakness lands everywhere at once.[^5] Dana's company had 47 tools, but the work that mattered most ran through two of them.

## What it cost

| Item | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Price-protection dispute resolution | 40,000 dollars |
| Supplier cost schedule disclosed, margin lost in two negotiations | 250,000 dollars |
| Twenty-nine dormant licences reclassified and cancelled | 14,000 dollars per year recovered |
| Compliance review and customer questionnaires | 62,000 dollars |
| Dana's time on remediation across six weeks | approximately 0.15 FTE |
| Program savings to date | 210,000 dollars |

The program was net negative. That sentence is the lesson. The tools worked. The order-entry rollout hit 88 percent weekly active use, which is better than most programs manage. The savings were real. What did not exist was anything that kept the program inside its own boundaries, and the cost of that absence was roughly twice the value the program had produced.

Two softer costs outlasted the spreadsheet. Two wholesale customers added a written AI assurance clause to their renewal paperwork, which now requires the manufacturer to answer a twenty-two-question questionnaire every year. And Dana's team lost the ability to say no. When you have spent a quarter fixing your own undisclosed tool usage, you do not have standing to decline a request from sales.

## The gap the numbers leave open

It would be tidy if this story were rare. The survey evidence says the shape is common. McKinsey's 2026 global survey found 80 percent of respondents reporting that AI has improved their own individual productivity, while the share attributing at least some EBIT impact to AI held flat at 37 percent, and the proportion of high performers, meaning at least 5 percent of EBIT attributed to AI with the impact described as significant, stayed at about 6 percent of all respondents.[^6]

Dana's company is what the middle of that distribution looks like from the inside. Individuals were more productive. The enterprise was worse off. Nothing in the productivity number would ever have surfaced the price-protection answer, the unapproved tools, or the supplier cost schedule, because productivity is measured on the work people did and not on the exposure the work created.

## The five controls that would have caught it

| Control | Failure it catches | What good looks like | The cheapest first step |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Model inventory | Failure one | Owner, data classification, support path, and a decommission date for every system | Reconcile the card statement against the approved list this month |
| Access recertification | Failure two | A recurring review with a named reviewer per system | Pull the admin list and try to name a manager for each row |
| An owner for every customer-facing system | Failure three | A named human who reads the knowledge base quarterly and signs off | Put the website assistant on somebody's job description today |
| A claim rule | Failure four | No AI capability claim ships without evidence attached | Require one line of evidence for any AI claim in marketing or sales |
| Incident disclosure path | Failure five | Who to tell, in what format, within what window, decided in advance | Write the path on one page and test it with a harmless scenario |

Add the sixth control if you have the appetite for it, which is a concentration limit: no more than some agreed share of consequential work running on a single model or provider, reviewed quarterly. It is not standard practice yet, and it is the control that would have been most valuable at month nine.

## Common failure modes

**Treating this as a technology failure.** Every one of the five failures is a document, a decision, or an owner. The model behaved as a model does.

**Assuming a small incident is not governance work.** The Air Canada order was $812.02. The precedent was free to the passenger and expensive for everyone else.

**Reading compliance as the boundary.** Nothing in this story broke a law on day one. Everything in it broke a commitment, a control, or a claim, and those are the things that become legal problems.

**Waiting for the inventory to be perfect.** Dana's real inventory started as a spreadsheet with 47 rows and a lot of blanks. A partial inventory beats a perfect one that arrives after the disclosure.

**Responding with a ban.** The manager who pasted the cost schedule needed a safe tool that answered the question they had. A ban removes the incident and leaves the need.

## Try it: run the failure case against your own program

Answer these five questions on paper, honestly, and you will have a decent idea of how far your program is from Dana's version.

List every AI tool in your organization by reconciling three sources: the approved vendor list, the corporate card statement, and your browser or network telemetry. Write the count, and note how many you can attach an owner to.

Pull the administrator list for your two most-used AI systems. For each row, write the name of the manager who would defend that access. Whatever you cannot fill in is a live finding, not a hypothetical one.

Find every customer-facing system that can state a policy, a price, a deadline, or a compliance fact. For each one, name the human who reads the knowledge base and signs off on it, and when they last did.

Open your marketing site and your sales deck and circle every claim about AI capability. For each circled claim, write the one line of evidence that supports it, or remove the claim.

Write your incident disclosure path on a single page: who to tell first, what gets recorded, what the external obligation is, and how fast. Then rehearse it once with a scenario that will never happen, so the first real use is not also the first rehearsal.
