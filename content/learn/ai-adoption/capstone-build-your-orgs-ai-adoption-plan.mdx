# Lesson 40: Capstone: build your org's AI adoption plan

This is the last lesson of the course. There is no lesson 41.

Forty lessons across five parts come down to one artifact, and it is the artifact you have been building toward since the first lesson drew the line between a rollout with a finish line and a program with no end date. The baseline audit told you where you actually are. The goal-setting and measurement lessons gave you numbers worth trusting. The policy, data safety, security, vendor, privacy, and incident lessons built the control set. The roadmap, capacity, and health review lessons built the calendar. The two case studies showed you the same set of pieces assembled well and assembled badly.

The capstone is where those pieces stop being lessons and become one document with your organization's name on it.

This lesson does not summarize the course, because a summary would be worth nothing to the person who has to brief a chief financial officer on Tuesday. It also does not hand you a template, because a template filled in with generic language is how an organization ends up with a policy nobody follows and a roadmap nobody believes. What follows is a structure, a build order, and a worked example, and the content has to come from your org.

One practical note before the structure. The plan is a living document. The framework it draws on explicitly describes itself as one, with a formal community review expected no later than 2028,[^1] and the management system standard behind it is built around continually improving rather than reaching a final state.[^2] Write your plan expecting to revise it twice in the first year. A plan you are afraid to change is not a plan, it is a hostage.

## Define the terms

**Adoption plan.** The single document that states what your organization will do with AI, who owns it, what is allowed, what is being built this year, how success is measured, and when it gets reviewed. Six sections, and if it runs past twenty pages you have written a strategy document by accident.

**Program charter.** The one-page section that grants the program its authority: the owner's name, the decision rights, the funding line, and the group that approves exceptions.

**Operating model.** The answer to who decides what, expressed as a set of decision rights with names attached and thresholds stated in money and risk, rather than as an org chart with an AI box drawn on it.

**Control library.** The list of standing controls, each with a named owner and a review frequency: inventory, access recertification, vendor review, incident path, claim rule, and data classification.

**Portfolio.** The year's work sorted into committed items, capped experiments, and deliberate refusals with revisit dates. The refusals are part of the portfolio, not an absence from it.

**Review calendar.** The fixed dates on which the plan gets checked: the monthly watch scan, the quarterly health review, and the annual roadmap refresh, each with a named convener.

## The six sections

Six sections, in this order, because each one depends on the answers above it.

**1. Scope and outcomes.** What AI means inside this organization, what year one covers, what year one explicitly does not cover, and two or three outcomes with numbers and dates. The exclusions do more work than the inclusions, because they are what lets a team say no without escalating.

**2. Operating model.** Who owns the program, who sits on the approving group, what each person can decide alone, and what requires a group. Decision rights stated as thresholds, so nobody has to guess whether a 40,000 dollar tool is a team decision. Accountability is the principle doing the work underneath this section. The OECD AI Principles, the first intergovernmental standard on AI and now adhered to by 47 countries, list accountability alongside transparency and explainability, robustness, security and safety, human rights and democratic values, and inclusive growth, which is a formal way of saying that a named person has to be answerable for what a system decides.[^3]

**3. Policy and controls.** The policy itself, kept short enough to be read, plus the control library it points at. The integrated management system approach is the right mental model here: policies and objectives, then processes to achieve them, with an explicit improvement loop rather than a one-time publication.[^2] If it helps to have a model written for non-specialists, the UK government's AI Playbook sets out 10 principles for civil servants using AI and covers the same ground this section has to cover, including what AI can and cannot do and how to select, buy, and deploy it.[^4]

**4. Portfolio and roadmap.** The year's committed work, the capped experiments, and the refusals with dates. Sequenced against your measured change capacity rather than against enthusiasm. Kill criteria written at approval, not written after a failure.

**5. Measurement.** The scorecard rows, their written definitions, a baseline from your own data, and a target. One number per instrument, and at least one that measures cost per unit of work rather than cost per seat. This section earns its own page because the two numbers that matter most are the two that pull apart. McKinsey's 2026 survey found 80 percent of respondents reporting that AI has improved their own productivity, while 37 percent attribute any EBIT impact to it and only about 6 percent meet its definition of a high performer.[^5] The distance between the individual number and the enterprise number is partly an adoption problem and partly a measurement design problem, and your scorecard is where you decide which one you are managing.

**6. Review calendar.** The dates and the conveners. A monthly scan, a quarterly health review, an annual roadmap refresh, and the decision dates in between.

Laid out as a table, the whole plan looks manageable, which is the point.

| Section | The question it answers | The artifact | Realistic length |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Scope and outcomes | What are we doing, and what are we not doing? | One page plus two or three numbered outcomes | 1 page |
| Operating model | Who decides what, and at what threshold? | A decision-rights table with names | 2 pages |
| Policy and controls | What is allowed, what is prohibited, and who checks? | Policy plus the control library | 4 to 6 pages |
| Portfolio and roadmap | What ships this year, and what waits? | A sequenced table with kill criteria | 1 to 2 pages |
| Measurement | How will we know, and against what baseline? | Scorecard with definitions and sources | 1 page |
| Review calendar | When does this get checked, and by whom? | A dated calendar with named conveners | Half a page |

The whole document should fit inside twelve pages, and the twelve pages should be readable by someone who has never opened the tools.

## Build it in four weeks

Four weeks, one section cluster per week, roughly four to six hours of your time each week. Do not delegate this and do not stretch it to a quarter, because a plan that takes a quarter to write is stale the day it is published.

**Week one, the baseline.** Run the inventory reconciliation from lesson 37: approved list, card statement, telemetry. Pull the licence utilisation report. Write the current scorecard with whatever definitions you can defend today, and write next to each row whether the number came from data or from a guess. Deliverable: one page listing what you cannot currently answer. This is the most valuable page in the whole exercise, and it is also the page most owners skip because it is the least flattering.

**Week two, the decisions.** Write scope and outcomes, then the operating model. Name the owner. Name the approving group and keep it to four or five people, because a governance group of eleven is a governance group that meets twice. State the decision thresholds in money and in risk. Deliverable: two pages that answer who decides what.

**Week three, the controls.** Write the policy and the control library. Four items on the prohibited list is a good target, because a prohibited list of twenty is a list nobody remembers. Put a name and a frequency against each control: inventory quarterly, access recertification quarterly, vendor review before purchase, incident path tested annually, claim rule always. Deliverable: a policy short enough to be read in one sitting and controls specific enough to be audited.

**Week four, the portfolio and the calendar.** Sequence the year against measured change capacity. Write kill criteria for every committed item at approval. Write the refusals down with revisit dates. Fix the scorecard definitions. Put the three recurring dates on named people's calendars before you publish anything. Deliverable: a dated plan and three calendar invites that already exist.

The order matters. A plan written as policy first and baseline last produces controls aimed at nothing, which is the most common defect in AI governance documents.

## Worked example: Marcus's one-page plan

Marcus runs AI for a 600-person professional services firm. His full plan runs nine pages. Here is the one-page version he uses to brief the partnership, reproduced with the specifics intact.

**Scope.** AI means the assistant platform, the three approved vendor tools, and anything a team buys on a corporate card. Year one covers order-to-cash, resourcing, and bid production. Year one explicitly excludes anything that produces client-facing advice without a named human reviewer, and anything that makes a decision about a person.

**Outcomes.** Cut bid production time from 14 days to 6 by the third quarter. Hold non-billable hours flat while billable hours rise 8 percent. Reach zero unapproved tools handling client data by the second quarter.

**Operating model.** Marcus owns the program. The approving group is four people: Marcus, the chief operating officer, the head of risk, and the data protection lead, meeting quarterly for ninety minutes. The group approves anything touching client data, anything client-facing, and any spend above 25,000 dollars. Everything else proceeds inside the control library without asking.

**Policy.** Six pages. Prohibited list is four items. Data rule is three tiers: public, internal, and client confidential, with client confidential barred from every tool that does not have a signed data processing agreement.

**Portfolio.** Committed: bid production redesign, resourcing assistant. Capped experiments: two, each capped at eight weeks and 3,000 dollars. Refused with revisit dates: client-facing drafting without a named reviewer, revisit in the third quarter; performance review summarization, refused permanently and recorded as such.

**Measurement.** Six rows with written definitions, baselined in week one: weekly active use by workflow, bid cycle time, cost per bid, licence utilisation, open findings, and unapproved tools detected.

**Calendar.** Watch scan monthly, owned by Marcus. Health review quarterly on the first Tuesday, ninety minutes, pack circulated forty-eight hours ahead. Roadmap refresh each November.

Nine pages, one owner, four decision thresholds, four prohibitions, three dates. That is what a finished plan looks like, and none of it required a data science team.

## The first ninety days after you publish

Publication is the middle of the work, not the end.

In the first two weeks, brief every team lead individually, not in a group email. The plan's operating model only works if the people who have been buying tools on a card understand what changed and what did not. Expect two or three to tell you about tools you did not know about, and treat those as wins rather than violations, because the alternative is that they keep quiet next time.

In the first month, run the inventory reconciliation for real rather than as an exercise, and put the count in front of your approving group. Then revoke something. A program whose first act is a finding, a date, and a revocation has demonstrated that the plan is real, and that single act does more for adoption than a launch email.

In the first quarter, hold the health review on the date you published, with the pack circulated on time. The first review sets the standard for every one after it, and the fastest way to lose the program's credibility is to move the first one because something came up.

## When a section has no good answer yet

You will reach week four and find one section you cannot fill in honestly. It is almost always measurement, and the runner-up is scope, because scope requires somebody to say out loud what the organization is not doing this year.

The instinct is to write a placeholder and move on. Do not. A placeholder in a plan is a promise nobody has agreed to keep, and the next reader will treat it as either a commitment you failed to deliver or a permission you accidentally granted. Write the gap into the plan, with a date for closing it. "We have no baseline for cost per unit of work. The finance lead will produce one by the end of the second quarter." That sentence is more useful to your approving group than a number you invented, and it tells everyone which part of the plan is still thin, which is information they need.

There is one exception worth making. If the missing section is the operating model, stop and fix it before you publish anything else, because every other section depends on knowing who decides. A plan with a vague owner and a sharp scorecard is a plan that gets rewritten by whoever is loudest during the first disagreement.

## Common failure modes

**Twelve pages that nobody reads.** If the policy is longer than a lunch break, nobody will have read it. The plan's credibility comes from being short enough to quote from memory.

**A plan written without a baseline.** Outcomes with no starting measurement are aspirations. The week-one page exists so that your targets have somewhere to be measured from.

**The approving group that never meets.** Four or five people, quarterly, ninety minutes, pack circulated ahead. Anything larger is a committee, and committees defer.

**Refusals left unwritten.** A refusal that exists only in a meeting is a refusal that gets relitigated in eight weeks. Write it down with a revisit date and the reasoning that produced it.

**Controls with no owner and no frequency.** Every control in the library needs a name and a cadence, or it exists only in the document.

**Publishing and then not reviewing.** The whole apparatus of this course points at the quarterly date. Miss the first one and you have written a document, not a plan.

## Try it: the capstone

Work through this list in four weeks. It is the shortest version of the whole course, and if you have read the previous thirty-nine lessons, most of these will feel like remembering rather than learning.

1. Reconcile three sources and write down how many AI tools your organization actually operates.
2. Write the one page of what you cannot currently answer.
3. Name one owner for the program, in writing, with a title.
4. Name the approving group, four or five people, and write the three thresholds that require them.
5. Write scope and outcomes, including at least two explicit exclusions.
6. Write or rewrite the policy at six pages or fewer, with a prohibited list of no more than four items.
7. Build the control library with an owner and a frequency for each control.
8. Measure change capacity across your teams and write the total down.
9. Sequence the year's work against that total, with kill criteria written at approval.
10. Write the scorecard definitions and a baseline for every row.
11. Put the monthly scan, the quarterly health review, and the annual refresh on named calendars.
12. Brief every team lead individually within two weeks of publishing.

## What's next

The course is complete. That is the honest answer to this section at lesson 40 of 40, and there is no lesson 41 to promise you.

Where the series goes from here is back to the beginning. Lesson 1, The adoption owner's job: from rollout to running program, is written for someone who has just been handed AI alongside nine other responsibilities, and it is a different lesson the second time through. You now know what the rollout-versus-program distinction costs when you get it wrong, because you have read a case study in which a program with no owner, no inventory, and no calendar went net negative on a competent team. Rereading that first lesson with those two case studies in hand is a better use of an hour than most new material would be, and it is also the fastest way to brief the person who replaces you.

If you take one thing out of the last forty lessons, take the shape rather than the specifics. Name an owner. Count what you have. Write down what is allowed. Put dates on the calendar and keep them. Every failure in this course, and every success, came down to whether those four things were done.
