# Lesson 5: Setting Realistic Goals and a North-Star Metric

Lesson 4 gave you the hard part: ground truth about where your organization actually stands on AI, recorded as four lines you can hold in one hand. This lesson turns those four lines into something you steer the whole program by. Goals, a north-star metric, a guardrail, and realistic targets, all defined first, then built for you in a worked example.

Start with the trap. "We want more people to use AI" is not a goal. It is a wish with no direction and no number. Left that way, it produces a dashboard of activity that everyone feels good about and nobody can act on, and it falls apart the moment anyone asks "how much, by when, toward what." This lesson replaces that wish with a small, precise system you can actually run.

## Define the terms first

Five words get used interchangeably when you need to build something together. They are not the same, and the difference is what makes the system work.

A **goal** is a desired outcome stated in words. "Reduce the time brokers spend writing post-meeting notes" is a goal. It names the direction you want to move.

A **metric** is a number that tracks progress toward a goal. For that goal, the metric might be the average minutes spent on a set of notes. The goal says where you want to be, the metric is how you know you are moving.

A **target** is a specific value on a metric by a specific date. "Cut average minutes per notes set from forty to twenty by the end of the quarter" is a target. A goal without a target cannot be checked, and a goal that cannot be checked cannot be managed.

The **baseline** is the starting value, the current reading of the metric before you change anything. Lesson 4 existed to hand you this number. If you set a target of twenty minutes without knowing you sit at forty today, the target is a guess. If you have the baseline, the target is a decision.

And the **north-star metric** is the single metric that best captures the value your program delivers, the one number you keep in view when every other dashboard number is shouting for attention. It is not a target you hit and forget. It is the long-term orientation that tells you whether the whole program is working. It gets the rest of this lesson, because it is the heart of the system.

One more word matters: **realistic**. Realistic does not mean easy. It means grounded in the evidence you actually hold, and reachable with normal effort rather than heroic luck. The whole trick of this lesson is setting targets that are ambitious enough to matter and honest enough that a reasonable team can hit them. [^2]

## A north-star is one number, not a dashboard

The idea of a north-star metric comes from product teams, where it was popularized as a single measure of whether a product is actually delivering value, and it has been described by analytics company Amplitude as the single metric that best captures the value customers derive from a product. [^1] That phrasing transfers directly to your AI program. Swap "customers" for "the organization" and the definition stands: the north-star metric is the single number that best captures the value your people get out of AI.

Three qualities separate a real north-star from a random KPI, and it is worth holding your candidate up against each one. [^1]

It represents value, not activity. The metric has to capture an outcome the organization can point to, not a count of something being done.

It is within your influence but not directly within your control. This is the subtle one. A good north-star is the result of many smaller things working together, so your teams can push the inputs that move it, but nobody can skip straight to the number by gaming a single action. Product people put it as: if you can move your north-star directly, it is probably not a good north-star, because it should sit one level out of reach and pull the whole organization toward it. [^1]

And it is a leading indicator, not a lagging one. A lagging indicator tells you what already happened, like monthly revenue at the end of the month. A leading indicator tells you where value is heading before the accounting catches up. Your north-star should point forward, which is why reporting and usage dashboards, however useful they are, rarely belong in the north-star seat. [^1]

This is where the number differs from your ordinary KPI list. A KPI tells you how some part of the business performed. The north-star sits above those, capturing the value that your many KPIs are all supposed to be adding up to, and pointing at where that value is heading. You still keep the KPI dashboard, but you stop using its forty graphs to judge the whole program. You judge the program by whether the north-star is moving. [^1]

## Two traps: vanity and the law of gaming

Most teams pick a bad north-star as soon as they start, and the bad choices cluster into two familiar traps.

The first trap is the **vanity metric**: a number that looks good on a slide and tells you almost nothing about whether the organization is better off. [^1] The classic AI example is "the share of people who used AI this month." It feels like proof of progress. It does not tell you whether anyone's work got faster, whether anything got more accurate, or whether any problem the business cares about got smaller. A metric that describes activity without describing value is vanity, and a vanity metric flatters the program while it quietly fails. [^1] Common vanity candidates you should reject for the north-star seat: the count of prompts people run, the raw count of "active users," the number of tools approved, and the plain percentage of the workforce touching AI. Each of these is worth tracking somewhere; none of them is the value the program exists to produce. [^1]

The second trap is deeper and deserves a name. It is Goodhart's law, stated in its modern form: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. [^3] The pattern is everywhere. The moment a number becomes the official goal, people reshape their behavior to move that number, and the number stops telling you about the thing it was supposed to stand for. Pick "share of employees using AI" as your official target and you will get exactly that: use that is wide, shallow, and arranged to tick the box, not use that produces value. The measure was pointing at adoption, the target rewarded the appearance of it, and those are not the same thing. [^3]

These two traps combine to one warning: a north-star must be about value, and it must not be gameable without also producing real value. If a team can meet the number by doing less real work, or by doing showy work that changes nothing, the number is wrong. This is where the guardrail metric earns its place.

## Pair the north-star with a guardrail

A **guardrail metric** is a second number you watch so the north-star cannot be met by cutting quality. You do not set a formal target on the guardrail the way you set one on the north-star. You set a threshold, and you pay attention when the guardrail crosses it.

The need for a guardrail is the natural consequence of Goodhart's law. [^3] The north-star says "produce value." Any number that says "produce value" can be chased the cheap way: claim the value, inflate the rounding, stop checking, grade your own homework. The guardrail exists to catch the cheap way. If the north-star is the number of workflows with a documented improvement, the natural guardrail is the error rate, the share of AI-assisted work that a human reviewer has to fix or discard. When documented improvements climb but the correction rate climbs with them, you are not getting faster, you are getting sloppier, and the guardrail tells you before the quality damage reaches the customer.

The pairing works in one direction. You set the north-star as the thing you want to go up. You set the guardrail as the thing you refuse to let get worse while you chase it. Together they say what every sound goal actually wants to say: produce value, and do not wreck quality to do it.

## Set realistic targets from the baseline

Now that you have a north-star and a guardrail, you turn them into targets, and this is where the baseline from Lesson 4 does its real work.

Decades of research on goal setting, summarized by its founders Edwin Locke and Gary Latham, reach a consistent conclusion: specific, difficult goals produce higher performance than vague or "do your best" instructions. [^2] A target that names a number and a date outperforms a directive to try harder. That is the case for being specific.

But the same body of work, and plain logic, says the target must be grounded. A specific target you cannot possibly hit stops being a motivator and starts being a signal that leadership does not understand the work. A specific target everyone has already exceeded is fake optimism dressed as rigor. Realism is not the enemy of ambition. It is what keeps ambition honest.

Here is the method, and it is short. First, take the baseline value for the metric from Lesson 4. Second, name the current behavior that already contributes to the goal, because the audit will almost certainly show it exists, informal and unmeasured. Third, set a target that assumes you formalize and improve a plausible share of that existing activity rather than inventing adoption from scratch. Fourth, add a date you can actually review by. If your team has the capacity to convert two workflows a month from informal to measured, a target of eight workflows for the quarter is realistic; a target of all thirty is a fantasy, and a target of one is already in the bank.

Realistic also means the target acknowledges the shape of your people's work. Lesson 2 taught you that value concentrates in a manageable set of high-frequency, high-value workflows. You do not need to convert the world. You need to convert that set, which is finite and knowable. That is what makes the target both ambitious and possible: it is bounded by real work, not by a marketing slogan.

## A worked example: Lucia sets her course

Lucia owns AI adoption at the mid-size freight-brokering firm from the earlier lessons, ninety people, one sanctioned accounts-payable invoicing pilot with a real time-saved number behind it, and a three-week baseline that showed far more unsanctioned use than anyone believed, including candidate resumes and customer lists flowing through consumer tools. Her four-line baseline is done. Now she sets her course.

She does not fall for the obvious candidates. "Share of employees using AI this month" is a vanity metric, wide and shallow, and it is the target her head of operations, Amara, first suggests. It ticks the box and proves nothing about value. [^1] She also refuses "count of AI prompts," because prompting is activity, not outcome, and it is trivially gameable, which is exactly how measure-becomes-target failures start. [^3]

She lands on a north-star that captures value and sits one level out of reach: the number of core workflows per month where the team records a time-saved or quality improvement that survives a quick human review. [^1] It is an outcome, not an activity, because it counts improvements, not sessions. It is a leading indicator, because improvements this month point at where the firm's capacity is heading. And nobody can move it directly, because a workflow only counts when work actually improved and a person confirmed it, so the only way to move the number is to produce real improvement.

She pairs it with a guardrail: the share of AI-assisted work that a reviewer has to correct or discard. The north-star pushes value up; the guardrail stops that push from turning into sloppiness. When documented improvements climb but the correction rate climbs with them, Lucia has her early warning. [^3]

Now the targets, grounded in the baseline. The firm has roughly thirty core workflows. Exactly one, the invoicing pilot, is formalized and measured. The audit showed most staff already use AI in value-ready workflows without recording it, so her goal is not to invent adoption but to formalize a plausible slice of what already happens. [^1] She sets a target Lucia thinks is ambitious and her team can reach: twelve core workflows with a documented, reviewed improvement by the end of the next quarter. That is three a month, a rate that matches the audit's finding that the informal use exists for her team to capture, and it leaves the remaining eighteen for the following quarters. The guardrail threshold: if the reviewer-correction rate moves past one in five AI-assisted tasks, she pauses the formalization drive and fixes quality before she adds workflow thirteen.

The point is not the specific numbers. Ninety people, thirty workflows, twelve by quarter's end, none of that transfers to your organization. The point is the shape of the decision. Lucia picked one number about value, one counter about quality, and a target that was specific, grounded in a measured baseline, and achievable with real effort. Nothing in it was a slogan. Everything in it was a decision she could defend.

## Try it

Take the four-line baseline you produced at the end of Lesson 4 and turn it into your own course. This takes an afternoon.

First, write the value your program exists to produce, in one sentence. Not the tool list, not the usage rate. The outcome: fewer hours on some task, fewer errors in some process, faster answers to some question. That sentence is the raw material for your north-star.

Second, draft a north-star metric that captures that value. Hold it against the three tests from this lesson. Does it represent value, not activity? Is it within your influence but not directly within your control, one level out of reach? Is it a leading indicator, pointing where value is heading rather than reporting what already happened? [^1] Reject vanity candidates and anything a team could hit by doing less real work. [^1] [^3]

Third, name your guardrail, the one quality number you refuse to let get worse while you chase the north-star.

Fourth, set a target from the baseline. Take the baseline value for your north-star metric, name the existing informal activity you can formalize, and set a specific number and date that you believe and your team can reach. Write it as "metric from baseline X to target Y by date Z," and add your guardrail threshold underneath.

Then ask yourself the honesty questions. Is the number about value, or would a skeptic call it vanity? Could someone meet it by hurting quality, and would the guardrail catch them? Is the target grounded in a measured baseline, or is it a guess dressed as confidence? If you can answer all three, you have the steering system the rest of this course builds on.

## What's next

You now have what Lesson 4's baseline was missing: direction. A north-star tells you where the program is headed, a guardrail keeps it honest, and a realistic target tells you whether you are moving. That is the measurement half of leading AI adoption, and it is the point where most programs stop.

A number cannot carry a workforce, and this is where the next lesson picks up. Lesson 6 moves from measuring to momentum, covering change management past the 30-day on-ramp. Set a goal and people will nod; build the behavior that actually reaches it and you are doing the work that matters. Bridging that gap, sustaining adoption after the novelty week fades, is the change-management job the course turns to next. Your north-star from this lesson is exactly what you will use to judge whether that momentum is real, so keep your metric and your target close.
