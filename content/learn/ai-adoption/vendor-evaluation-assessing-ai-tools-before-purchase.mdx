# Lesson 17: Vendor Evaluation: Assessing AI Tools Before Purchase

A vendor shows a demo. The tool does something impressive on a curated example, the room nods, someone asks about price, and three weeks later a course of action has started that nobody actually evaluated. This is how most organizations buy AI, and it is the exact moment your governance either earns its keep or quietly signs away the month it spent building an AI policy.

The chain of decisions that gets AI into a company runs through procurement. If the people buying are the only ones deciding, the purchase reflects their demos and their comfort, not the risk your policy just spent a lesson defining. Vendor evaluation is the bridge: the structured process of deciding, before money moves, whether a tool is worth buying and on what terms. The earlier lessons left you with a supervision framework and data-safety rules. This lesson is where those rules meet the market, because every tool you evaluate will arrive claiming it is the exception.

## The words you need

Define the terms before the demo fatigue sets in.

**Vendor evaluation** is the systematic comparison of candidate AI products against a fixed set of criteria that reflect your organization's requirements and risk posture, carried out before a purchasing decision. The word *systematic* matters. A process with a written rubric, a scored result, and a documented decision beats a process that runs on whoever spoke last in the room. Evaluation is not comparison shopping (which product is flashier). It is a risk decision (which product can we use safely, affordably, and with a way out).

**Evaluation rubric** (or scorecard) is the fixed set of weighted criteria you score every candidate against, before you look at any candidate. The weight reflects how much each factor matters to you specifically. Setting the rubric first stops you from moving the goalposts to fit whichever vendor you already liked.

**Proof of concept** (PoC) is a small, time-boxed trial where a real candidate meets your real data and your real people doing the real task, measured against a pre-agreed success metric. It is the difference between a demo (the vendor chooses the example) and evidence (you choose the example). A PoC is not a paid pilot with rolled-up costs; it is the cheapest test that tells you whether the tool works on your actual problem.

**Total cost of ownership** (TCO) is the full cost of using a tool over its life, not the sticker price. It includes license fees, setup and integration, data preparation, training, ongoing support, the time your own staff spend managing it, and the cost of leaving if it stops working out. An AI tool that looks cheap per seat can be expensive once data preparation and exit costs are counted. [^5]

**Exit path** (or vendor lock-in) is the measure of how hard it would be to move the same capability to a different provider. Lock-in is best understood not as a single lever but as the total cost, in money, time, disruption, and risk, of switching. If leaving is so expensive that no one makes the case, you are locked in no matter what the contract's words say. [^4]

**Deployer obligations** are the duties that attach to the organization using an AI system, not the one that made it. Under the EU AI Act, the party that deploys a high-risk system carries real duties of its own: ensuring the system is used per the provider's instructions, assigning human oversight to people with the right competence, training, and authority, ensuring input data is relevant and representative, and monitoring the system's operation. [^3] For you this is the uncomfortable punchline of the lesson: after the signature, the obligation to oversee the tool is yours, and the vendor's marketing materials do not share it.

## Why evaluation is a risk decision, not a shopping trip

Here is the trap. When you evaluate a tool against another tool, you are comparing products. When you adopt a tool, you are adopting a risk profile, a data-flow, a support relationship, and a bundle of obligations that outlive the contract. Those are not the same comparison, and the second one is the one that governs.

The Ada Lovelace Institute studied how public bodies buy AI and data-driven systems and reached a blunt conclusion: procurement is a crucial mechanism for scrutinizing technology, but organizations lack a clear, comprehensive account of how to do it, which leaves them to interpret transparency and fairness on their own. [^2] The finding transfers directly to a private company. Nobody hands you a finished checklist for buying AI responsibly. You build it, and this lesson gives you the shape of it.

So the evaluation criteria are not "does it impress the CFO" and "what is the monthly fee." They are the questions your policy already said matter: where does data go, can we leave, what lands on us, and can the thing be governed at all.

## The evaluation rubric, built before you look

Set the rubric first. A useful vendor-evaluation rubric has a small number of weighted criteria, each tied to a requirement or risk your organization already owns. Six or seven criteria is plenty; more than ten becomes ceremony. A defensible starting set:

**Capability.** Does the tool actually do the task on the shape of data you have? Score this by live PoC evidence, not by demo claims. A tool that nails marketing examples and fumbles your invoices does not have the capability you are buying.

**Data handling.** Where does the data go, who can see it, is it used to train the model, and does it leave your organization or your region? This is the direct test of the data-safety rules from earlier in the course. A vendor that cannot write a specific answer to "who can see our data and for what" is not slow; it is disqualifying.

**Security and compliance.** What standards does the vendor meet, and can it show a third-party assessment? Your policy's security section was a promise; this criterion checks whether the vendor can honor it. Ask for the current assessment document, not a marketing page.

**Total cost of ownership.** Not the license. Include integration, data preparation, training, running support, your staff time, and the exit cost. The Pertama guide to AI TCO is blunt that data preparation and quality work often cost more than the software itself, and that exit costs are routinely forgotten. [^5]

**Exit path.** Can you export your data, in a usable form, and can you take it somewhere else? The practical test is to actually ask the vendor, during evaluation, to export a sample of your data and check whether it is usable rather than merely downloadable. This is a prenup conversation, not romantic at all, and very useful. [^4]

**Oversight and accountability.** What does using this tool require from your people, and what does the vendor commit to in writing? For high-consequence systems this maps to deployer obligations from the EU AI Act: human oversight with real authority, monitoring, and log keeping are yours to staff. [^3] If the tool makes important decisions and the contract gives you no monitoring rights or incident contact, you are shouldering the downside alone.

**Vendor stability and roadmap.** Is the company likely to exist, and to keep supporting this product, as long as you need it? A tool from a vendor that churns products is a lock-in risk wearing a friendly face.

Weight each criterion before you look at any candidate. The rubric is not a democracy where every factor is equal; it is your risk posture made explicit. If your organization is in a regulated sector, security, data handling, and oversight should weigh far more than capability, because a breach or a compliance failure costs more than a feature gap.

## Worked example: two candidates, one rubric

Let me run the process on a concrete case so you can see the scoring, and check the numbers as you go. A mid-size services firm wants an AI assistant to help its support team draft responses to client tickets. The team handles sensitive client data. The firm's policy flags data handling and oversight as its top risks, so those two criteria get the heaviest weights.

The firm sets a rubric before seeing either vendor, with weights out of 20:

- Data handling, weight 5
- Security and compliance, weight 5
- Oversight and accountability, weight 4
- Total cost of ownership, weight 3
- Exit path, weight 2
- Capability, weight 1

Each criterion is scored 1 to 5, and the weighted score is the weight times the score, out of a possible 100.

**Vendor A** is the one everyone in the room liked after the demo. Scoring against the pre-set rubric and a real PoC on the firm's own tickets:
- Data handling: 2. The vendor's answer to "who can see our data" was a reassurance and a links page, not a specific statement, and the PoC showed data egressing to a processing region outside the firm's approval boundary. Score 2.
- Security: 4. A current third-party assessment exists and the vendor shares a data-responsibility matrix. Score 4.
- Oversight: 2. No monitoring rights offered; the contract's incident clause is a single sentence and no named contact. Score 2.
- TCO: 3. Cheap license, but integration is custom and data preparation is billed separately and estimated high. Score 3.
- Exit path: 1. Data export is via a proprietary format with no migration help. The firm asked for a sample export and it came back unusable without heavy cleanup. Score 1.
- Capability: 5. On the real PoC it was excellent, the best of the two. Score 5.

Weighted: (5x2) + (5x4) + (4x2) + (3x3) + (2x1) + (1x5) = 10 + 20 + 8 + 9 + 2 + 5 = 54.

**Vendor B** was less impressive in the demo, which is why nobody initially wanted it.
- Data handling: 5. A precise statement of where data lives, who can see it, and a training-data option that keeps the firm's data out of model training. Score 5.
- Security: 4. A current assessment and a clear support and patching commitment. Score 4.
- Oversight: 4. Monitoring rights, a named support channel, and a process to report an issue for high-consequence outputs. Score 4.
- TCO: 4. Higher license, but standard integration, lower data-prep, and a documented support cost. Score 4.
- Exit path: 4. Clear data export in a standard format, and the firm's sample export came back usable. Score 4.
- Capability: 3. Good but not the best on the PoC; it needed more setup to reach parity. Score 3.

Weighted: (5x5) + (5x4) + (4x4) + (3x4) + (2x4) + (1x3) = 25 + 20 + 16 + 12 + 8 + 3 = 84.

![Two candidates scored against the same weighted rubric: Vendor A totals 54 and Vendor B totals 84, because the firm's risk posture weights data handling and oversight far above capability](</diagrams/vendor-evaluation-assessing-ai-tools-before-purchase/2-vendor-a-vs-b.png>)

Vendor B wins on weighted score despite losing the demo comparison. That is the whole point. The demo picked A; the rubric picked B because the firm's stated risk posture is data handling and oversight, not flash, and B is the one the firm can actually govern. Vendor A's capability advantage (5 versus 3) could not outweigh a data-handling failure and a non-existent exit path, because those two factors carried most of the weight. If the firm had weighted capability first, A would have won, and the firm would have adopted the tool it cannot control.

The numbers here are the arithmetic of a 1-to-5 score times a weight out of 20, so you can multiply any alternative scoring and get a different total, but the ordering holds because the weights encode the firm's risks, not the vendors' marketing.

## The gate before you buy: proof of concept, exit tested, obligations named

The weighted score is not the whole decision. Three practical gates should clear before any purchase, and all three are measurable.

![Three gates before you buy: run a proof of concept on real data, test the exit during evaluation, and name the deployer obligations before you sign](</diagrams/vendor-evaluation-assessing-ai-tools-before-purchase/3-three-gates.png>)

First, run a proof of concept on real data with a pre-agreed success metric, and record the result. Not a demo. Drop the vendor's curated example, run your actual workflow, and check whether the tool reaches the bar you set in the PoC scope. If it does not reach the bar on its own data, it will not reach it on the bigger, messier version later.

Second, test the exit during evaluation, while you still have leverage. Ask for a sample data export and confirm it is usable. A vendor that cannot hand back your data in useful form is building a lock-in you will pay for at renewal, when the dependency becomes their leverage. [^4]

Third, name the deployer obligations before you sign, because they will not shrink afterward. If the tool is high-consequence, you are taking on human oversight, monitoring, and log-keeping duties that are yours to staff. [^3] The contract should name the vendor's obligations as precisely as your own: monitoring rights, an incident process, a named contact, and a standard data format on the way out.

## Try it: score a tool under consideration in your organization

Pick one AI tool your organization is actively considering, or one you already bought and now wish you had evaluated. Build a six-criterion rubric from the list above, assign weights that reflect your organization's actual risk posture (if you do not know it, use the policy the earlier lessons had you draft), and score the candidate against your real requirements, not its demo.

For at least the data-handling, security, and exit-path criteria, do not score from what the vendor says on its site. Get a specific answer in writing from an actual sales or security contact: where does our data go, can we leave with a usable export, and what is our monitoring access? Flag every criterion where the vendor could not or would not give a concrete answer.

Then answer honestly: does the score match the enthusiasm in the room, or did the demo outrun the evidence? The gap between the two is what vendor evaluation exists to expose. If the tool you already bought does not survive the rubric, that is not a reason to rage or to defend the purchase; it is the prompt for the next lesson, because the contract you are already inside is where the leverage, or the lack of it, actually lives.

Write your rubric and your scores down. You will want them, because Lesson 18 takes you one step further down the same road.

## What's next

You have scored a candidate against a rubric that reflects your risk posture, and you have seen the uncomfortable split between how persuasive a demo is and how governable a product is when the data, the exit, and the oversight are actually tested.

The unavoidable next step is signing the thing. Lesson 18 is Licensing and procurement: what you are actually buying. Every vendor you evaluated has a license buried under the demo, and the license is where capabilities become costs, where your obligations get locked into legal language, and where the exit path you tested in this lesson becomes either a real clause or a dead promise. Next lesson walks you through the parts of an AI procurement contract and license that a non-technical owner actually has to read, so the tool you picked with a clear head is the tool you get to use, keep, and leave when you need to.

For now, hold the core idea: evaluate the tool you can govern, not the tool that impresses you, and test the parts that will outlast the contract while you still have leverage to ask for them.
