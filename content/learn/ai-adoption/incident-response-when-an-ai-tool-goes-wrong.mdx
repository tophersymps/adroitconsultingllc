In the last lesson you learned to see the privacy rules that anchor your AI use. This lesson is about the moment those rules stop being abstract: the day a tool actually fails. It happens to every organization that runs AI, and it is not a sign your program is broken. It is a sign your program is real. What separates a defensible program from a chaotic one is not avoiding incidents. It is having a plan for them before they arrive.

The uncomfortable truth is that most organizations do not have that plan. They have a security incident response plan for their network, and they assume it covers AI. It does not, not fully, because AI fails in ways traditional software does not. A model can produce a confident wrong answer that a human then acts on. It can leak data through a prompt someone typed. It can make a decision about a person that harms them. None of those look like a server going down, and a plan built for servers will miss them. This lesson gives you the vocabulary and the sequence to respond to the failures that are actually yours to own.

## The words you need

**AI incident** is the term you need to hold first, because it is broader than you expect. NIST, the US standards body that wrote the AI Risk Management Framework, defines an AI incident as an event, circumstance, or series of events where the development, use, or malfunction of one or more AI systems directly or indirectly contributes to a harm: injury or harm to a person's health, including psychological harm; disruption of critical infrastructure; violation of human rights or a breach of law meant to protect fundamental, labor, or intellectual property rights; or harm to property, communities, or the environment.[^1] Read that definition closely and you will notice two things. First, the harm can be indirect. The AI does not have to be the sole cause, or even the main cause. It just has to contribute. Second, the harm can be psychological or reputational, not just physical or financial. A model that tells a customer something false and damaging, or that screens someone out of a job unfairly, is an incident even though no server crashed.

**Near miss** is the term for the event that almost caused harm but did not. The model produced the wrong answer, but a human caught it before it went out. The data was almost pasted into an unapproved tool, but someone stopped it. Near misses matter more than incidents for one reason: they are free lessons. They cost you nothing to learn from, and they tell you where your next real incident is likely to come from. A program that only reacts to actual harm is always one step behind. A program that logs and studies its near misses is learning while it is still cheap to learn.

**Containment** is the act of stopping the harm from spreading. In traditional IT this means isolating a compromised server. In AI it means the equivalent: pausing the tool, recalling its outputs, reversing a decision it made, or cutting off the data path that is leaking. The point of containment is not to fix the root cause. It is to stop the bleeding while you figure out what happened.

**Root cause** is the underlying reason the incident happened, as opposed to the surface symptom. The surface symptom might be "the model gave a wrong answer." The root cause might be that the model was trained on outdated data, or that someone changed the prompt, or that the tool was being used for a task it was never designed for. You cannot prevent a repeat until you know the root cause, and the root cause is often not where the symptom appeared.

**Post-incident review** is the structured look back after the incident is contained, where you write down what happened, why, what worked, what did not, and what you will change. It is the step that turns an incident from a cost into an investment. Skip it and you will pay for the same failure twice.

## Why AI incidents are different from software failures

Before you can respond well, you need to understand why AI incidents do not fit the playbook you already have. NIST's AI RMF is explicit that AI risks differ from traditional software risks in ways that matter for response.[^2] Three differences carry most of the weight.

The first is that AI systems are socio-technical. The harm does not come from the model alone. It comes from the interaction between the model, the people using it, the data it was trained on, and the context it is deployed in.[^2] A model that is perfectly safe in one department can cause harm in another, because the people and the data are different. That means your incident response cannot be owned by a single technical team. It has to involve the people who understand the use case, the data, and the people affected.

The second is that AI failures are often silent and gradual. A server outage is loud: the site is down, everyone notices. A model that has been drifting for weeks, slowly getting worse at its job, produces no alarm. It just quietly makes more mistakes, and each mistake is small enough to ignore until one of them is not. NIST's monitoring guidance warns that deployed AI systems are hard to monitor, and that failures can emerge from changes in the data, the model, or the context that are hard to detect.[^3] Your response plan has to include detection, not just reaction, because by the time someone notices a gradual failure, the harm may already be done.

The third is that the failure can originate outside your perimeter. In the last lesson you learned about shadow AI, tools your people use that you never approved. When one of those tools fails, the failure lands inside your organization even though the tool was never yours. NIST's guidance on third-party AI is blunt: you need incident response plans for AI systems you did not build, and you need to rehearse them, because a vendor's failure becomes your incident the moment it touches your data or your people.[^4] Your plan has to cover tools you did not choose, not just the ones you did.

## The response sequence: detect, contain, investigate, remediate, report, learn

There is no single official AI incident response playbook, but the sequence that recurs across the serious guidance is consistent. NIST's AI RMF frames it as part of the MANAGE function: risk treatment includes plans to respond to, recover from, and communicate about incidents.[^5] The AI RMF Playbook adds the practical point that establishing and maintaining incident response plans can reduce the likelihood of additive impacts during an AI incident, meaning the plan does not just help you respond, it helps you avoid making things worse.[^6] Here is the sequence a non-technical owner needs to hold, in order.

![The response sequence: six steps in order, detect, contain, investigate, remediate, report, learn. Containment is the first job, not root cause, and the learn step feeds back to change your plan, monitoring, and training.](</diagrams/incident-response-when-an-ai-tool-goes-wrong/3-response-sequence.png>)

**Detect.** The incident has to be noticed before anything else can happen. Detection is not just waiting for someone to complain. It is having signals: a way for users to report that a tool gave a wrong or harmful answer, a review of outputs in high-risk use cases, and a habit of treating near misses as reportable events. NIST's AI RMF names this explicitly: organizational practices should enable AI testing, identification of incidents, and information sharing.[^7] If your only detection mechanism is "someone will tell us," you are detecting late.

**Contain.** The moment you confirm an incident, your first job is to stop the harm from spreading, not to find the root cause. Containment actions vary by the type of failure. If a model is producing harmful outputs, you pause it or swap in a fallback. If a decision harmed someone, you reverse it or stop acting on it. If data leaked, you cut off the path it is leaking through. The AI RMF Playbook is clear that smaller organizations, which may not have full governance programs, can still use incident response plans to address system failures, abuse, or misuse.[^6] Containment is the part you can do even with a small team, and it is the part that matters most in the first hours.

![Containment depends on the failure type: if the tool produces harmful outputs, pause it or swap in a fallback; if a decision harmed someone, reverse it or stop acting on it; if data is leaking, cut off the path.](</diagrams/incident-response-when-an-ai-tool-goes-wrong/4-contain-by-failure-type.png>)

**Investigate.** Once the harm is contained, you find the root cause. This is where you answer the questions that prevent a repeat: what specifically went wrong, when did it start, how many outputs or decisions were affected, and why did your monitoring not catch it earlier. The investigation has to be systematic and documented, because the answer you write down is what you will act on. NIST's AI RMF treats this as part of managing risk: you identify and document the likelihood and magnitude of impacts based on expected use, past uses, and public incident reports.[^8]

**Remediate.** You fix the root cause, not just the symptom. If the model was trained on bad data, you fix the data or the model. If the tool was being used for a task it was not designed for, you change the process or the tool. If the prompt was wrong, you fix the prompt. The AI RMF Playbook frames this as treatment procedures to recognize, counter, mitigate, and manage risks that were not previously identified.[^6] The test of remediation is not whether the immediate symptom is gone. It is whether the same failure can happen again.

**Report.** You tell the people who need to know. This has two parts. Internally, you escalate to the right level and loop in legal for anything with regulatory or litigation implications. Externally, you assess whether you have a legal obligation to notify, and to whom, and within what timeframe. NIST's AI RMF is explicit that incidents and errors must be communicated to relevant AI actors, including affected communities, and that processes for tracking, responding to, and recovering from incidents must be followed and documented.[^9] The GenAI profile adds that you should report incidents in compliance with legal and regulatory requirements, such as breach reporting rules.[^10] The instinct to stay quiet is strong, but the law and the trust of the people you serve both point the other way.

**Learn.** You turn the incident into a change. This is the post-incident review, and it is the step most organizations skip because the crisis is over and everyone wants to move on. That is exactly when the learning is most valuable, because the details are still fresh. NIST's guidance on third-party AI is explicit that you should improve incident response plans based on retrospective learning.[^4] The output of the review is not a report that sits in a drawer. It is a change to your policy, your monitoring, your training, or your tool choices, so that the next incident is less likely and less costly.

## Worked example: a customer-facing assistant goes wrong

Let me run this sequence on a concrete case so you can see it land. This is an illustration, not a real company, but the shape is one you will recognize.

A mid-size services firm, about 200 people, deploys an AI assistant that drafts responses to customer support emails. The assistant is trained on the firm's past support conversations and is supposed to draft a suggested reply that a human reviews before sending. It has been running for three months, and the firm believes it is working well.

The incident starts quietly. A customer writes in with a billing question, and the assistant drafts a reply that confidently states the customer's account was overcharged and offers a refund amount. The human reviewer, busy and trusting the tool, sends it without checking the numbers. The refund amount is wrong, and worse, the reply implies the firm admits to an overcharge that did not happen. The customer forwards the reply to a consumer protection agency, and the firm's legal team finds out from the agency, not from its own staff.

Here is how the sequence plays out.

**Detect.** The firm did not detect it. The customer complained to the agency, and the agency told the firm. That is the worst detection outcome, because it means the firm lost control of the narrative and the timeline. The lesson is not that the assistant is bad. It is that the firm had no signal for "the assistant produced a wrong answer that a human did not catch." A simple review of a sample of sent replies, or a rule that billing-related replies get a second check, would have caught it.

**Contain.** Once legal learns of the complaint, the firm pauses the assistant's billing-related drafting immediately. It stops the tool from producing any new billing replies while it figures out what happened. This is the right containment move, and it is the part the firm can do fast even though it was late to detect.

**Investigate.** The firm pulls the assistant's logs and finds that the wrong answer came from a specific pattern: the assistant was trained on past conversations that included a genuine overcharge incident from two years ago, and it generalized that into a habit of flagging overcharges that were not there. The root cause is not a random glitch. It is that the training data contained a rare but memorable example, and the model learned to over-apply it. The firm also finds that the human review step was not actually reviewing, because reviewers were under pressure and trusting the tool.

**Remediate.** The firm fixes two things. It retrains or reconfigures the assistant so it does not draft billing replies from that pattern, and it changes the process so billing-related replies require a second human review regardless of what the assistant drafts. The remediation is not just "the model is fixed." It is "the process now has a check that catches this class of error."

**Report.** The firm responds to the customer directly, corrects the record, and documents the incident internally. It assesses whether it has a legal obligation to notify the agency or any regulator, and it works with legal on that. It also tells its support team what happened, because the team needs to know the assistant can be wrong and that the review step matters.

**Learn.** The firm holds a post-incident review. It writes down the timeline, the root cause, what worked, and what did not. It changes its monitoring so that a sample of assistant-drafted replies is reviewed every week, and it adds a rule that any reply touching money gets a second check. It updates its AI policy to name the assistant and its review requirement. The incident cost the firm a customer and some reputation, but the review turns it into a change that makes the next incident less likely.

The through-line is that the firm's failure was not the assistant. It was the absence of detection and the absence of a real review step. The assistant was always going to be wrong sometimes. The plan was what was missing.

## Try it: build your incident response plan before you need it

The best time to build an incident response plan is now, while nothing is on fire. This exercise produces the actual plan, not a meeting about one.

Start by naming your highest-risk AI use cases. Take the inventory you built in the shadow IT lesson and pick the two or three tools that handle the most sensitive data or make the most consequential decisions. For each one, write down the answer to one question: if this tool failed tomorrow, what would the harm be? A wrong answer a customer acts on? A decision that harms an employee or applicant? Data leaking to a model provider? Name the specific harm, because the harm determines the response.

Then write the response sequence for each use case, one line per step. Who detects the failure, and what signal tells them? Who has the authority to contain it, meaning pause the tool or reverse a decision, and can they do it without waiting for approval? Who investigates the root cause? Who fixes it, and what would the fix look like? Who has to be told, internally and externally, and does any of it trigger a legal notification? And who runs the post-incident review, and what change would it be allowed to make?

Finally, test the plan against a scenario. Take the worked example above, or invent one for your own highest-risk tool, and walk your plan through it. Find the step where the plan goes silent, where nobody is named, or where the answer is "we would figure it out then." That gap is your real deliverable. Fill it now, while you can think clearly, and you will not be filling it at 2am during an actual incident.

Write the plan down and keep it where the people who would need it can find it. It is the single highest-leverage artifact you can produce as the owner of your AI program, because it is the one you will reach for at the worst moment.

## What's next

You now know how to respond when a tool fails: detect it, contain the harm, find the root cause, fix it, tell the people who need to know, and learn from it. That plan assumes you can act on the tool, which raises a question you have not yet answered: what happens when the thing that fails is not yours to fix?

Lesson 23 is Model and provider risk: dependency and lock-in. It picks up exactly where this lesson stops. Your incident response plan covers the tools you run, but most of the AI in your organization runs on models and services provided by someone else, and that dependency is itself a risk. The next lesson teaches you to see the risks of relying on a single model or provider, what lock-in actually costs you, and how to build resilience into your AI program so that a vendor's change, outage, or price increase does not become your incident. It is the natural next step, because you cannot fully respond to a failure until you understand how dependent you are on the thing that failed.

For now, hold the core idea: incidents are not a sign your program is broken, they are a sign it is real, and the difference between a bad outcome and a disaster is whether you had a plan before the moment arrived. Build the plan now, while you can think clearly, and you will be ready when the tool goes wrong.
