# Lesson 37: Running a periodic AI health review

Lesson 36 gave you a process for the outside world. It reads what your vendors are shipping, retiring, repricing, and rewriting in their terms pages, and it turns that reading into dated entries in a watch log. That process looks outward, and it works.

It will not tell you that a licence you approved for a pilot in February is still switched on for eleven people who left that pilot in April. It will not tell you that the team which agreed to stop pasting client contracts into a public chatbot has quietly gone back to doing it. Nothing in a vendor's release notes covers the distance between what your policy says and what your people actually do.

That distance is what this lesson closes.

Here is the loose end lesson 36 left behind. A watch log ends a quarter with deferrals in it, and a deferral is a claim that a decision will arrive on a date. A claim with no meeting attached is not a plan, it is a hope. The health review is the meeting.

Owners skip it because it sounds like overhead. It is the opposite. Every deferred decision, every pilot that stalled and stayed on the roadmap anyway, every access grant nobody remembers making, comes back later as an emergency with a deadline attached. A health review is how you schedule the emergency out of existence, while it is still small enough to be boring.

This lesson builds the review itself: the five instruments and the single data pack they feed, a ninety-minute agenda you can run with no preparation beyond that pack, the difference between a health review and a status report, and a worked example of one quarter at the insurer we have been following since lesson 35.

## Define the terms

**Health review.** A recurring meeting with a fixed agenda and a fixed data pack that holds your AI program against its own commitments. It is not a project review and it is not a steering committee. Its only real output is decisions, each with a name and a date attached.

**Scorecard.** The one page of numbers the review reads every cycle, using the same definitions every time. A metric whose definition drifts quarter to quarter cannot show you a trend. It can only show you a mood.

**Cadence.** How often the review happens and who is responsible for convening it. Quarterly is the usual answer. The specific interval matters much less than the fact that it does not move when a quarter goes badly.

**Drift.** The slow divergence between what a system or a workflow was approved to do and what it is doing now. Drift is rarely the model changing underneath you. It is almost always people changing, or volume changing, or an exception that outlived the reason it was granted.

**Access recertification.** The periodic act of asking a named human to confirm that a named list of people should still reach a tool or a data set, and revoking the ones nobody will claim. It is the single cheapest control in this lesson and the one most often skipped.

**Exception register.** The list of approved departures from your own policy. This team uses an unapproved summarizer. This workflow retains more data than the standard allows. This agent runs without a human in the loop. Every entry carries a reason, an owner, and an expiry date, or it is not an exception, it is just an unmanaged risk.

## Five instruments in one data pack

A health review is only as good as what it reads. Five instruments cover the whole program, and all five have to be assembled before anyone walks into the room. If the pack is built during the meeting, the meeting is a discussion, not a review.

| Instrument | The question it answers | Where the number comes from | The failure it catches |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Adoption and usage | Who is using this, and is use growing or fading? | The vendor usage export, your sign-in logs, the team's own task counts | A pilot that stalled two quarters ago and is still being reported as active |
| Risk and incidents | What went wrong, what nearly went wrong, and what is still open? | One shared register, not somebody's inbox | The fourth report of the same problem, which means it is not an incident, it is a defect |
| Access and licences | Who can reach this, and who is paying for seats nobody opens? | Group membership, the vendor's seat report, the invoice | Departed staff with live access, and licences bought for a pilot that ended |
| Spend and unit cost | What does one unit of work cost now? | Invoices, the vendor's pricing page, your own task counts | Cost creep, and a business case that quietly crossed a threshold |
| The deferral ledger | Which decisions came due since we last sat down? | The watch log plus the last review's minutes | Deferrals that became permanent because nobody was counting |

The fifth row is the one that distinguishes a health review from a dashboard. A dashboard tells you the state of things. The deferral ledger tells you which of your own promises has come due, which is the only part of the picture that requires a decision rather than a reaction.

The access row earns its place twice over. Excessive access rights are the mechanism behind two separate failures at once: a compromise, and an audit finding that says you are not in control of who reaches what. Vendors who ship the control at all ship it as a recurring review whose frequency you choose, which means monthly, quarterly, or annually is a decision you are already making whether you know it or not.[^4]

The risk row does something similar for incidents. The generative AI profile that accompanies the framework treats incident disclosure as one of four primary considerations, and it defines risk as the composite of how likely an event is and how bad it would be. That definition is the reason the register holds near misses next to incidents. A near miss has the same probability story and a smaller magnitude, and it is the cheapest evidence you will ever get about a failure you have not had yet.[^5]

Two rules make the pack work. First, one named owner per row, so a missing number has an address rather than an apology. Second, the same definitions every cycle, written down. If weekly active use meant "signed in at least once" in January and "completed three tasks" in July, you do not have a trend, you have two unrelated numbers and a story that makes them line up.

This is not a NIST invention or an ISO invention. It is the shape the standards describe when they get practical. The AI Risk Management Framework is built on four functions, govern, map, measure and manage, and it places governance as a cross-cutting function that has to be present in the other three rather than sitting off to the side.[^1] The framework's own playbook makes the cadence explicit: ongoing monitoring and periodic review of the risk management process are planned, roles and responsibilities are clearly defined, and the organization determines the frequency of that periodic review.[^2] The standards tell you the outcome. You still have to pick the date, put a name against it, and actually meet.

## What a health review is not

Three things get confused with this meeting, and the confusion is expensive.

It is not a steering committee. A steering committee decides whether to fund something. A health review decides whether the thing you already funded is still healthy, still used, still compliant, and still worth its cost. Same people, different question, different agenda. Merging them is how funding decisions crowd out the unglamorous work of looking after what you already own.

It is not a status report. A status report tells people what you did last month. There is a place for that, and it is called a note. A health review reads evidence and produces decisions. If your meeting can be replaced by an email, it is already an email.

It is not an audit, and you should resist the urge to run it like one. An audit arrives with an external standard, a sample, and findings that land in a report. A health review is your own instrument, run by you, with the authority you already have. The management system discipline behind this is standard and unremarkable: establish, implement, maintain, and continually improve, working through a plan, do, check, act loop.[^3] Check is the function most organizations quietly drop, which is why the loop stops improving and starts drifting.

## The ninety-minute agenda

Ninety minutes, eight blocks, time boxes written into the invite. The time boxes exist so that one loud item cannot eat the whole meeting, which is what happens every time a review has no structure.

1. **Data pack, circulated 48 hours ahead.** Zero minutes in the room. If it is not sent early, the review starts by reading.
2. **Scorecard walk, 15 minutes.** Read the five rows out loud. Agree on what moved. Resist explaining anything in this block, because explanation is how a 15-minute block becomes 40.
3. **Deferral ledger, 15 minutes.** Every item whose revisit date has arrived gets a decision, even if the decision is to defer again with a new date and a stated reason. A deferral with no new date is a deletion.
4. **Access and licence exceptions, 10 minutes.** Revoke, or record an exception with an expiry. Those are the only two outcomes.
5. **Risk and open incidents, 20 minutes.** Open items only. Anything new gets an owner and a date before the meeting ends, or it does not count as discussed.
6. **Unit cost and spend, 10 minutes.** One number per workflow. No modeling in the room.
7. **The one question, 10 minutes.** What did we learn this quarter that we did not know last quarter? If the honest answer is nothing, say that out loud, because a review that teaches nothing has turned into theater and theater is more expensive than silence.
8. **Decisions read back, 10 minutes.** Every decision, its owner, its date, in one pass. Decisions that are not read back are decisions that will be remembered differently by everyone in the room.

Notice what is missing: no demos, no roadmap replanning, no vendor pitch. Those belong somewhere, just not here. The fastest way to kill a health review is to let it become the meeting where exciting things happen.

## Worked example: Owen's third-quarter health review

Owen runs AI for the 1,900-person commercial insurer from lessons 35 and 36. The figures here are illustrative and checkable, and the method is the real thing. Claims document intake is live with 60 adjusters. Underwriting submission triage is live with 60 underwriters after the extension approved last quarter. The watch log closed the previous quarter with one migration scheduled, one extension approved, and two deferrals carrying dates.

The pack for this quarter reads as follows.

Adoption. Weekly active use in claims sits at 71 percent against a 60 percent floor. Sampled extraction accuracy is 87 percent against an 85 percent bar. Both are healthy. The second one is thin enough that the review asks for a sampling plan rather than a celebration, because 87 against 85 is two points of cushion and the last quarter moved four.

Deferral ledger. Three items came due. The agentic adjudication revisit holds at won't do, for the same three reasons recorded a year ago: the oversight model is not mature, the permission model cannot support delegated authority, and the failure cost is a wrongly denied claim. The underwriting triage extension is approved and live. The training refresh for new adjusters is two weeks overdue, which nobody had noticed because the owner changed teams in June.

Access and licences. Fourteen people hold an administrator role on the assistant platform. Managers can name eleven of them; three are unnamed, which in practice means unowned. Two pilot licences are still billing 38 seats, of which 9 were active in the last 30 days. The review revokes the three unnamed admin roles and the 29 dormant seats, and does it in the room rather than sending a request.

Risk and incidents. Two near misses, both the same shape. Adjusters pasted policy schedules into a public chatbot because the approved tool did not have the document they needed loaded. The review does not treat this as a training failure, because it is not one. It is a content gap with a training failure on top, and the fix is to load the schedules, not to send a reminder email.

Spend and unit cost. Cost per processed document is down 12 percent on the quarter with no change in accuracy. That number matters because it reopens a use case the annual plan rejected: policy endorsement summarization, which failed on unit cost in January and now clears it. Logged with a decision date at the next quarterly gate, not approved on the spot.

Four decisions come out of the sitting. Load the policy schedules and re-run the sampling. Revoke three admin roles and 29 seats. Reassign the training refresh to a named owner with a four-week date. Log endorsement summarization for a costed decision at the next gate. One new exception is registered with an expiry: a team may use an unapproved summarizer on internal-only documents for 60 days while the approved tool's document support catches up.

Total meeting time, ninety minutes. One thing got worse, the training refresh, and it got worse because the pack surfaced it rather than because the meeting caused it.

Put the counter-example next to it. A program runs a monthly "AI update" with no data pack, no fixed rows, and no ledger. Attendance is good. Everyone reports what they are excited about. Nine months in, nobody can say what the program costs, who has administrator access, which of the three pilots is still running, or what was promised to whom. The failure was never the meeting. It was the missing pack.

## Common failure modes

**Reading the number but not the definition.** The scorecard says usage is up. Up against what, measured how? If the definition is not in the pack, the number is decoration.

**Letting the review become a demo session.** A vendor or a champion wants to show something new. It is genuinely interesting and it consumes the deferral ledger's only slot. Book a separate thread.

**Keeping the pack in the owner's head.** The review works because the numbers exist in a file that survives the owner's holiday, their promotion, or their resignation.

**A review with no authority.** If the group in the room cannot revoke access, stop a pilot, or move a date, the meeting produces observations, not decisions. Ask for the authority before you ask for the meeting.

**Skipping the quarter after a bad quarter.** This is the most common failure and the most damaging. A bad quarter is the precise moment the review earns its cost.

## Try it: run your first health review in two weeks

Work through these in order. Most owners finish the setup inside a working day.

Pick the date and put it on a recurring calendar invite before anything else, because a review without a recurring slot is a one-off meeting. Ninety minutes, quarterly, same time.

Name the pack. One owner per row: adoption, risk and incidents, access and licences, spend and unit cost, deferral ledger. Write the owners next to the rows in the invite.

Write down the definition of every scorecard number you intend to read, in one sentence each, and keep them in the pack file. Do this even if some definitions are approximations. An approximate definition that stays constant beats a precise one that changes.

Pull the access list today, before the first meeting. Sort it by who has an administrator role. Try to name the manager for each one. Whatever you cannot name is your first finding, and it is also your fastest win.

Open the deferral ledger with two columns, what and when. Copy into it every deferred decision you can find from the last two quarters of notes. Most owners are surprised by the length of that list, and the list is the reason the review exists.

Run the meeting with the eight blocks and the time boxes from this lesson. Read the decisions back at the end. Send the read-back within a day, and hold the next date in the same message.

## What's next

You now have the two halves of the operating rhythm. The watch process reads the outside world and turns market news into dated decisions. The health review reads your own program and turns deferrals into dated decisions. Between them, almost nothing can drift for long without somebody seeing it on a page.

What is missing is the thing both of those processes are pointed at: what it actually looks like when this works. Lesson 38 is a case study of an organization that scaled AI well, walked through honestly, including the parts it got wrong. You will see the same instruments you have just built running inside a business that grew adoption past the pilot stage, the four moves that separated it from the programs that plateau at 20 percent usage, and the one place where a well-run program still nearly lost its footing.
