Most annual AI roadmaps are written in the wrong order. They start with what the tools can do this year, subtract what the budget can afford, and call the remainder a plan. The document that comes out is confident, organized, and wrong in a specific way: it describes a year in which nothing else in the company is happening, nobody needs training, and every dependency arrives on time.

The order that holds up is the reverse. Start with what the organization can absorb over twelve months, then spend that capacity on the few things worth doing. That sounds like a constraint on ambition. It is actually the only way ambition survives contact with a calendar.

This lesson is about building that document. By the end you should have a roadmap you can defend line by line, with named owners, dated decisions, an explicit list of what you are not doing, and a rough number for how much change each team can take before it stops cooperating.

## The evidence says the constraint is capability

The gap between ambition and execution is measured every year, and the measurements agree with each other.

McKinsey's November 2025 global survey found 88 percent of respondents reporting that their organizations use AI in at least one business function, up from 78 percent a year earlier. Nearly two-thirds said their organizations had not yet begun scaling AI across the enterprise, and roughly one-third said they had. Thirty-nine percent attributed any EBIT impact to AI at the enterprise level, and most of those said the impact was under 5 percent of EBIT. The group McKinsey calls high performers, meaning at least 5 percent EBIT impact plus significant reported value, was about 6 percent of respondents.[^1]

Read that shape carefully. Almost everyone is using the tools, a minority is scaling, and a smaller minority can point at money. The missing middle is not enthusiasm. It is capacity: skills, process redesign, clean data, working systems, and governance that can keep up.

Deloitte's 2026 State of AI in the Enterprise report, based on 3,235 senior leaders surveyed between August and September 2025, adds the internal view. Forty-two percent now describe their AI strategy as highly prepared, more than last year, but they feel less prepared on infrastructure, data, risk, and talent. Insufficient worker skills was named the biggest barrier to integrating AI into existing workflows, and the leading talent response was educating the broader workforce to raise AI fluency, cited by 53 percent. Only about one in five companies reported a mature governance model for autonomous agents.[^2]

That is a system telling you where it is thin. Strategy documents are cheap. The four things those leaders feel least ready for, which are infrastructure, data, risk, and talent, are where the year actually gets spent.

Gartner's numbers describe what happens when a roadmap ignores that. Its July 2024 forecast had at least 30 percent of generative AI projects abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025, naming poor data quality, inadequate risk controls, escalating costs, and unclear business value as the causes, and putting deployment approaches at five to twenty million dollars each.[^3] Two years later its infrastructure and operations survey of 782 leaders found 28 percent of AI use cases fully succeeding and meeting ROI expectations while 20 percent failed outright, with 38 percent of the leaders who hit setbacks blaming persistent skill gaps and another 38 percent blaming poor data quality or limited availability.[^4]

None of those causes is a model problem. Every one of them is a capability you either built or did not.

## Six terms to settle before you write anything

**Strategy.** What you are trying to win over about three years, in one page. It should not change annually. If your strategy is rewritten every January, you have a budget request with a mission statement attached.

**Roadmap.** A dated sequence of work, each item carrying an owner, a capability cost, and a decision date. A roadmap commits sequence and decisions, not scope on a fixed date.

**Plan.** The resource commitment for a defined scope. Plans sit inside roadmaps. Programs that confuse the two end up promising fixed scope on fixed dates and calling the result agility.

**Capability.** Your organization's ability to do a thing without heroics. For AI work five kinds matter: skills, workflow design, data, systems, and governance. A use case consumes all five in some proportion, and the proportion is what makes it cheap or expensive.

**Absorptive capacity.** A term from Cohen and Levinthal's 1990 paper: the ability of a firm to recognize the value of new external information, assimilate it, and apply it to commercial ends. Their argument is that this ability is largely a function of prior related knowledge and is path dependent, so a company that does not invest early in an area can find later development foreclosed.[^5] In plain terms: the skills you built last year decide what you can learn this year. That is why a training line is not overhead.

**Change capacity and change disruption.** Prosci's change saturation model puts these on opposite sides of a simple balance. Change capacity is how much a group can absorb, shaped by culture, history, and people's ability to adapt. Change disruption is the collective impact of everything hitting them at once. When disruption exceeds capacity you get saturation, and its symptoms show up as poor adoption, fatigue, and turnover rather than as a project failure. In Prosci's most recent Best Practices in Change Management study, 73 percent of respondents reported being near, at, or past the point of saturation.[^6]

Add one more idea, because it changes how you write the document.

**Commitment versus option.** A commitment spends the full budget on a defined outcome. An option buys information with a small capped spend and a decision date. A roadmap made only of commitments is a bet you cannot exit. A roadmap made only of options is a research program with a logo.

## Four lanes, and one of them is empty on purpose

A roadmap that only lists work is not a roadmap. It is a queue, and queues get filled by whoever shouts loudest. Give the year four lanes.

The first lane is **run**: keeping what already works alive. Support, maintenance, renewals, the training that stops decaying, the review of connections and access that keeps governance real. Owners of AI programs routinely underfund this lane and then discover in month seven that the thing everyone praised in month two has quietly stopped being maintained.

The second lane is **build**: capability the whole program depends on, and that no single use case pays for on its own. A data dictionary for the fields that matter. A permissions model. A skills curriculum. A governance update for agents. This lane is the least exciting and the one that decides whether next year is possible.

The third lane is **prove**: experiments with a capped spend, a small population, and a written decision date. Every item here has kill criteria stated at approval, not after the demo.

The fourth lane is **won't do**. An explicit list of things you are declining this year, with a revisit date and a one-line reason. This lane is the most useful page in the document, because it is the only defense against the request that arrives in April with an executive's name on it.

A starting allocation, not a law: half of the year's capacity on committed work expected to deliver, a quarter on run, and a quarter on capability build and options. The money need not follow the same split, and in the worked example below it does not. Programs skewed heavily to prove look busy and never compound. Programs with nothing in prove stop learning and get surprised.

## Score the five capabilities, then let the score choose the roadmap

You cannot plan against a capability you have not described. Score each of the five from 0 to 3 for the area you are planning for, using evidence rather than impressions.

For skills, 0 means nobody has used the tool, 1 means a handful of enthusiasts, 2 means most of the team uses it weekly with wide skill variance, 3 means the team can design a workflow around it and teach a new joiner. For data, 0 means the fields you need are not captured, 1 means they exist in three systems that disagree, 2 means a named system of record per field, 3 means those fields are documented and monitored. For systems, 0 is no connection, 1 is manual export and paste, 2 is a read connection with mirrored permissions, 3 is read and controlled write. For workflow design, 0 is nobody owns the process, 1 is a documented current state nobody follows, 2 is an owner who can redesign it, 3 is a team that has already redesigned one workflow around AI. For governance, 0 is no policy, 1 is a policy nobody reads, 2 is policy plus an owner plus a review cadence, 3 is that plus agent oversight and a tested incident path.

Now the rule that makes the scores useful: every roadmap item names the capability it consumes and who supplies it. If an item needs skills at 2 and you are at 1, the item has a prerequisite, and the prerequisite goes in the build lane with a date. If the prerequisite has no owner, the item is a wish and it belongs in won't do.

This single discipline kills most bad roadmaps in an afternoon. It also explains a pattern you have probably seen: two companies buy the same tool, one gets value in a quarter and the other gets a pilot nobody can reproduce. The tool was never the variable.

## Turn change capacity into a number you can budget

The capability score tells you what is missing. Change capacity tells you how much fixing the team can take at once.

Do this per team, not per company, because saturations are local. Pick the unit of work whose daily routine changes and estimate the disruption on a 1 to 3 scale: 1 for a tool change inside an existing process, 2 for a process change people must learn, 3 for a role change where some decisions move. Then set a quarterly ceiling per team, and six units is a workable starting ceiling for most operational teams. Count the changes already committed by other functions, including the ERP migration and the new ticketing system, because those consume the same capacity your AI program needs.

Two rules follow, and both will feel restrictive in March and obvious in November.

First, no team takes more than two material changes in a quarter. Second, the AI roadmap is planned against remaining capacity, not total capacity. If claims operations is spending the first half of the year on a policy admin migration, claims gets zero new AI changes until the third quarter regardless of how good the business case is.

The reason to formalize this instead of leaving it to judgment is that the request always sounds reasonable in isolation. The claims director asks for one automation. The finance director asks for one report. The service director asks for one assistant. Each is a unit of disruption, and the team receiving all three experiences a program, not a tool. Prosci's framing is the one to borrow when you explain the refusal: saturation is disruption exceeding capacity, and the fix is sequencing, not more communication.[^6]

## Sequence by dependency, not by enthusiasm

Ordering a roadmap is where most of the craft sits, because the same set of items can be a good year or a bad one depending on sequence. Four dependency types cover nearly everything.

Skills before scale. A tool rolled to three hundred people who have had ninety minutes of training produces three hundred shallow uses and a support queue. A tool rolled to forty trained people, then to the rest, produces examples the rest can copy.

Data before integration. Connecting a system whose fields disagree with another system does not create information, it creates a faster argument. Name the system of record per field first, even when that work is dull and invisible.

Read before write. Every step from suggesting to acting raises the cost of being wrong. Read access with human approval is a different risk class from an agent that updates a customer record, and the roadmap should show that gap in the schedule.

Governance before autonomy. Gartner's infrastructure survey found success correlated with embedding AI in systems people already use and with full executive support, and its failure cases clustered in overly ambitious or poorly scoped initiatives that expected too much, too fast.[^4] Deloitte's finding that only about one in five companies has mature agent governance points the same way.[^2] If oversight is not ready, autonomy is not a roadmap item, it is a future incident with a date on it.

One more sequencing rule earns its own paragraph. Watch for the project that is actually a prerequisite wearing a project's clothes. An assistant that answers questions about contract terms is a project. A documented, cleaned, permissioned contract repository is a prerequisite, and it will take longer than the assistant. Programs that fund the assistant and hope the repository appears get neither.

## Write kill criteria before you write the business case

Every item in the prove lane needs three things written at approval: the metric, the threshold, and the date. Together they are the kill criteria, and their purpose is to make stopping a decision rather than an argument.

A workable shape is one sentence each. We will know this worked if weekly active use among the pilot group passes 60 percent by week eight. We will stop if sampled extraction accuracy falls below 85 percent on one hundred documents, or if the human edit rate stays above 40 percent after four weeks, because past that point the tool is generating work rather than removing it. The decision is made on this date by this person.

That paragraph does something subtle. It moves the emotional weight of the decision from the person who championed the idea to a number agreed in advance, which is why it survives contact with a sponsor who is fond of the project.

Commitments need the same treatment in a different form: a scope statement, a named owner, a review point at which scope can be cut without restarting, and a stated maximum loss. The maximum loss sentence is the one executives remember. It reads: if this fails, we have spent this much and we will know by this date.

## The page that goes to leadership

You will not get a forty-slide deck read. Build one page with seven blocks, in this order.

The north star, one sentence, with its current baseline number. The three or four committed bets, each with owner, budget, and the metric that proves it. The build plan for capabilities, with the dependency each one unlocks. The absorption budget by team and quarter, which is where the refusals get their justification. The options being bought this year, each with a spend cap and a decision date. The won't-do list with revisit dates. And the decision calendar: every date in the year at which a real choice gets made, named.

The decision calendar is the part that turns a document into a management system. A roadmap without decision dates is a forecast. A roadmap with them is a schedule for governing, and it tells your leadership exactly when they will be asked to choose rather than being told after the fact what already happened.

## Worked example: Owen builds a year for an insurer

Owen runs AI for a 1,900-person commercial insurer. Claims operations has 340 adjusters, underwriting has 120, and broker service has 60. Leadership has approved a program budget and wants an annual roadmap in three weeks. The figures below are illustrative, built so you can check the arithmetic, but every step in the method is the real thing.

He scores claims operations first, because it has the clearest business case. Skills 1: a fifth of adjusters use a general assistant weekly, mostly for email. Data 2: the claim fields exist with a named system of record, though the notes field is a swamp. Systems 1: no connection to the claims platform, exports are manual. Workflow design 1: the intake process is documented and everyone works around it. Governance 2: a policy exists, an owner exists, agent oversight does not.

Then he checks the change calendar, and it reshapes the year. Claims operations is mid-migration to a new policy administration platform through the second quarter. The migration itself is a 3 on the disruption scale, and the retraining that travels with it is another 3, so the team sits at its six-unit ceiling in both quarters. Underwriting carries one committed change and has room for one more. Broker service has room for two.

So claims gets no new AI work until the third quarter, even though its business case is the strongest. That single constraint decides the sequence.

The build lane runs in the first quarter with three items and no new tools. A training program for 180 claims staff, built around three real workflows rather than features, 180,000 dollars including backfill for class time. A data dictionary for the eleven claim fields the use case will read, owned by the data lead, 40,000 dollars of internal time. A governance update covering agent behavior, with a kill switch, an incident path, and a review cadence, owned by risk, 35,000 dollars.

Underwriting takes the first prove slot in the second quarter: submission triage for 25 underwriters, a small item with a decision date in September and a spend cap of 25,000 dollars.

Claims takes the second prove slot in the third quarter: document intake, meaning retrieval over claim documents with a drafted summary, read-only, mirrored permissions, capped at 60 adjusters for eleven weeks. Kill criteria are written before the first vendor meeting. Weekly active use above 60 percent by week eight. Sampled extraction accuracy above 85 percent on one hundred documents. Human edit rate below 40 percent after four weeks, because past that point the tool is generating work instead of removing it. And one named question the system must refuse to answer for a user who lacks access.

The pilot measures 9.4 minutes per document before and 7.8 after, on 22 documents per adjuster per day, a saving of about 35 minutes per person per day. Owen annualizes the run rate at full scale: 340 adjusters times 35 minutes is roughly 198 hours a day, at a loaded 38 dollars an hour across 240 working days, about 1.81 million dollars a year. Then he plans against 40 percent of it, because time saved is not the same as capacity freed. People absorb part of the gain into quality and part into slack, and a business case that ignores this gets discredited the first time headcount does not fall. At 40 percent realization the run rate is about 723,000 dollars a year.

The honest part of the plan is the calendar. Scale-up lands in the fourth quarter at the earliest, so year one captures roughly a quarter of that value, about 180,000 dollars against a 610,000 dollar program cost. Owen presents a two-year case with the full run rate from year two. That is a harder conversation than a same-year payback, and it is the one that survives a second review.

The whole first-year budget comes to 610,000 dollars: 140,000 for platform and integration, 190,000 for internal engineering and program staff time, 180,000 for training, and 100,000 held as contingency for the integration surprises that always arrive. Close to 40 percent of it sits in the build lane rather than the tool line. That ratio is the tell of a capability-shaped roadmap, and it is the part a finance reviewer will question first, so it needs the dependency argument attached.

Broker service is offered a prove slot and turns it down, because its two change slots are going to a CRM upgrade and a new commission process. Owen records the decline with a revisit date in April, which is how it stays a decision instead of becoming a grievance.

Agentic claims adjudication, the idea the chief claims officer keeps raising, goes to won't do with a one-line reason and a revisit date next year: the oversight model is not mature, the permission model does not support delegated authority, and the failure cost is a wrongly denied claim, which is not a risk worth buying this year.

The finished document shows three build items, two options with decision dates, one refusal on record, one team that passed, and a decision calendar with six dates on it. None of it looks ambitious on paper, which is the point.

## Try it: build your own in a week

Day one, write the north star and its baseline. One sentence, one number, taken from your measurement work rather than from a vendor deck.

Day two, score the five capabilities for the one area you care about most. Use evidence: a sample of documents, a permission test, a usage export. Most scores come out lower than the leadership team expects, and that is the finding.

Day three, collect the change calendar from every function, not just IT. List every material change hitting your target teams by quarter, and convert each to disruption units. Subtract from capacity.

Day four, draft the four lanes. Force yourself to fill the won't-do lane with at least three items, each with a revisit date. If you cannot name three things you are declining, you have not made choices.

Day five, write kill criteria for every prove item and a maximum loss sentence for every commitment. Then build the decision calendar. If a month in the year has no decision in it, either the plan is too safe or it is not really sequenced.

Finish with the test that matters. Hand the one page to someone outside the program and ask them what you are not doing this year, and when you will know whether the biggest bet is working. If they can answer both without your help, the document is a roadmap.

## What's next

You now have a year that fits the organization rather than the other way around: capability scored, capacity budgeted, work sequenced, refusals written down, and decisions dated. The weakness built into any annual document is that the ground moves while it sits there. A model improves, a vendor changes its pricing, a competitor ships something that resets expectations, and the assumptions you made in January are wrong by June. Lesson 36 is about staying current as tools and models change: how to watch the market without chasing it, which signals deserve a response, and how to update a roadmap without rewriting it every time a release note lands.
