# Lesson 4: The Adoption Baseline: Audit Where Your Organization Actually Is

Lessons 1 through 3 assumed your organization is using AI. This lesson confronts how much, and how wrong your leaders are likely to be about it.

Here is the starting position. Global survey data says the overwhelming majority of knowledge workers use AI at work. Microsoft and LinkedIn's 2024 Work Trend Index, which surveyed 31,000 people across 31 countries, found that 75 percent of knowledge workers use AI at work. [^1] The same index found 78 percent of those AI users bring their own tools, meaning tools the company did not approve or provide, and that number rises to 80 percent in small and medium companies. [^1] Nearly half, 46 percent, had started using AI in the six months before the survey. [^1]

Now hold that against what leadership usually believes. McKinsey's survey for its 2025 report on AI in the workplace found a sharp split. Surveying 3,613 employees and 238 C-suite leaders in late 2024, it asked both groups how much employees use generative AI for at least 30 percent of their daily work. C-suite leaders estimated only 4 percent of employees do; 13 percent of employees reported that they actually do, roughly three times the leaders' estimate. [^2]

Two separate findings, same conclusion. AI is already inside your organization at a scale leadership does not see, running through tools the company did not approve, and doing the most important work in the building. That is the problem this lesson exists to fix, because you cannot govern, measure, or scale what you cannot see.

## The baseline is where every decision starts

An **adoption baseline** is a snapshot of where your organization actually stands on AI at a point in time: which tools are in use, where they are used, what data flows through them, and how the people using them perceive the whole thing. It is the "before" measurement that every later decision about goals, budget, policy, and value gets measured against. [^1]

The word that matters in that definition is *actually*. The baseline is not the adoption plan, not the sanctioned tool list, not what leaders believe. It is ground truth, what really happens on a normal Tuesday. Lesson 3 taught you to measure a pilot with real numbers instead of enthusiasm. The baseline applies the same honesty at the scale of the whole organization.

An **adoption audit** is the process that produces the baseline. The two words describe the same act, the inspection and its result, and you will hear them used interchangeably. What they share is the refusal to plan from a guess.

The audit is not an optional warm-up. Every downstream decision in this course assumes one. A north-star metric needs a starting value to measure progress against. A budget needs to know what is currently being spent on unsanctioned tools. An AI policy needs to know what it is regulating. An incident response plan needs to know what data already sits in tools outside your control. Lesson 5 will ask you to set realistic goals, and you cannot set realistic goals about a number you never measured. [^2]

## The perception gap is the first thing you will find

Name the single most consistent audit result, and it is the **perception gap**: what leadership believes about AI adoption versus what the audit actually finds. The Microsoft and McKinsey numbers are not isolated. They describe a structural pattern.

Leaders underestimate for three reasons that reinforce each other. First, adoption that happens through employee-owned tools generates no procurement record, no license line on the budget, no ticket to IT, so there is no trace for leadership to see. Second, people hide it. The Microsoft index found 52 percent of people who use AI at work are reluctant to admit using it for their most important tasks, and 53 percent worry that using it on important work makes them look replaceable. [^1] If your workforce fears that admitting to AI use invites scrutiny or suggests they are dispensable, they will not tell you the truth in a survey, and you cannot blame them for a fear the company created.

Third, and most quietly, leaders and employees answer from different frames. McKinsey found employees are three times more likely than leaders realize to believe AI will take on a major share of their work within a year, and those employees, wary of being made redundant, are simply not eager to advertise how much they rely on a tool that might look like it is replacing them. [^2] The people doing the work know adoption is further along than the people reading the reports about the work. The audit exists to close that gap with evidence instead of blame.

## What the audit maps: four tracks

Run the audit as four parallel tracks, each answering a different question. Together they give you a baseline you can govern.

**The tool track: what is actually in use.** List every AI tool showing up across the organization, approved or not. Start from the sanctioned list, then add what the other tracks turn up: the free consumer tools people keep open in another tab, the browser extensions, the "AI assistant" now embedded in software the company already pays for, the phone app people use after hours. Expect to find that a meaningful share of AI use happens through tools nobody approved, which is the BYOAI pattern the global data describes. [^1]

**The workflow track: where it is used.** Map which jobs and which steps use AI. This is the direct application of Lesson 2: adoption is not "we use AI," it is a list of concrete workflows where a specific tool does a specific step. You want to know which functions are saturated and which are untouched, because that tells you where value already lives and where governing risk matters most. [^2]

**The data track: what flows through it.** Record what kinds of information get typed, pasted, or uploaded into these tools: customer records, candidate resumes, financials, employee details, source code, proprietary spreadsheets. This is the risk surface. AI tools are not defenseless, but consumer tools generally are not built to hold your regulated or confidential data to your standard, and you need to know what is already out there before you can write a policy or an incident response around it. This track does not require you to decide anything yet, only to record what you find.

**The perception track: what people believe.** Ask leaders what they think adoption looks like, and ask workers what they actually do. Run both sets of answers side by side. The gap between them, the difference between the 4 percent estimate and the 13 percent reality, is itself a result worth reporting, because it is the reason your audit had to happen. [^2]

Two rules govern all four tracks. Combine what people tell you with what you observe, because self-report alone undercounts, and half your workforce is reluctant to admit the truth. [^1] And interview enough depth, meaning a spread of roles and both leaders and frontline workers, that the picture is real rather than a few enthusiastic voices. A baseline built on two volunteers is not a baseline.

## Why people hide it, and how to listen past that

You will not get an honest baseline if you simply ask people to confess. The most important move in the whole audit is to read the admission problem and design around it.

The Microsoft index is blunt: more than half of AI users are reluctant to admit how much they rely on AI for important tasks, and worry it makes them look replaceable. [^1] When you hold a session and say "who uses AI here," you are asking people to raise their hands against their own job security. Many will stay silent, and the silent ones are often the heaviest users.

So collect the audit through the channels that do not require disclosure in front of a group. Anonymous surveys tend to loosen the admission, though they still carry the usefulness bias, people who think AI makes them look replaceable under-report even anonymously. [^1] Procurement and expense records catch the tools the company actually pays for. What you can observe, license logs, browser and network telemetry where you have the authority to view it, extension registries, what IT has deployed versus what is running, catches the tools nobody asked about. The lesson is not that you should spy on your people, it is that a baseline built only from voluntary self-report is guaranteed to undercount, so you pair disclosure with observation and treat the two sources as a combined picture. [^1]

## A worked example: the freight firm finds its shadow

Lucia owns AI adoption at the mid-size freight-brokering firm from the earlier lessons. Ninety people. One sanctioned pilot, the accounts payable invoicing work from Lesson 3, running inside approved software with a real time-saved number behind it. That pilot is clean. It is also small, and Lucia suspects it is nowhere near the whole story.

Her head of operations, Amara, is certain "maybe a handful" of people use AI. Most of the "younger folks," she guesses. Before Lucia spends a dollar on a goal or a policy, she runs the three-week audit.

The tool track comes back first and reframes everything. Procurement records show the one sanctioned pilot. Observation and an anonymous survey show something else: most of the workforce uses a consumer AI tool several times a week, and a majority of the office staff use AI for some part of their job, a share that is not surprising given the national pattern that 75 percent of knowledge workers use AI at work. [^1] Most use it on personal accounts, no license, no ticket, no record. The actionable number was not the usage count. It was that the share Amara believed, a handful, was off from the measured reality by an order of magnitude, exactly the perception gap the research predicts, the difference between a leader's estimate and a worker's report. [^2]

The workflow track places it. Marketing uses AI for first drafts of outreach copy. Sales uses it to draft and tighten follow-up emails to prospects. Operations uses it to summarize long carrier contracts. Recruiting uses it to write interview notes and draft job descriptions. None of these were requested, approved, or measured. Several sit inside the value-ready shape from Lesson 2, which is exactly why people adopted them.

The data track is where the audit earns its keep. The recruiting coordinator has been pasting candidate resumes into a free public tool to pull out experience summaries. Customer lists with contact details have gone through an online AI tool to spot-clean duplicates, and those lists carry the firm's sales data outside its systems. Nothing about this was malicious, and none of it was visible on any approved-tool report. It is now, which is the entire point.

The perception track closes the loop. When Lucia presents the baseline, she does not open with a list of who is misbehaving. She opens with two numbers side by side: Amara's estimate of a handful versus the measured reality, plus the national finding that three-quarters of knowledge workers already use AI at work. [^1] The gap itself makes the case that the company has been running this program blind, and the audit is the first honest picture it has had. Nothing in the baseline is an accusation. It is the ground truth that a goal, a policy, a budget, and a governance plan can finally stand on.

![A three-week audit at a 90-person freight firm reveals broad unsanctioned AI use in the workforce against one clean sanctioned pilot, including candidate resumes and customer contact lists flowing through consumer tools](</diagrams/the-adoption-baseline-audit-where-your-org-actually-is/3-sanctioned-vs-shadow.png>)

This is the correct reading of the worked example. The audit's value was not the inventory as an end. It was converting "we barely use AI" into "here is exactly what is running, where, on what data, and how far leadership's belief is from the reality," which is the only material you can build a governed program from.

## What the baseline is for

A baseline is a starting line, not a report that gets filed away. Three uses define it.

It sets the reference for measurement. Lesson 5 will ask you to set a north-star metric and realistic goals. That metric is meaningless without a starting value, and the baseline provides it, the usage rate, the mix of sanctioned to unsanctioned tools, the workflows covered, the data-exposure surface, all measured before you change anything. [^1]

It defines the surface you must govern. Shadow use is not a scandal, it is the discovery that unmanaged AI already touches your customer data, your candidates, and your proprietary information. You cannot write a policy, a vendor standard, a data-handling rule, or an incident response plan for tools you did not know existed. The baseline hands you that list.

And it converts priority from opinion into evidence. When a budget request comes in, you do not argue about whether AI is "really happening." You point at the measured baseline and the workflows it turned up, which is Lesson 2's judgment applied to the whole organization at once. The person who runs governance from a measured baseline can show the reasoning. The person who runs it from a guess can only shrug.

## Try it

This exercise sets up the rest of the course, and it takes about a week with interviews scheduled across it.

Write down your leadership hypothesis first, before you collect anything. Ask the senior people who own functions in your organization what they believe: how many people use AI, which tools, for what. Capture those estimates in one column. You will compare them to what you actually find, and the gap is a result.

Then run a light version of the four tracks. For the tool track, list every AI tool you can find evidence of, from what IT has approved, from procurement records, from your own knowledge, and from asking a handful of people in each function what they use day to day. For the workflow track, name which jobs and which steps those tools perform. For the data track, note what kinds of data go in, customer records, candidates, financials, confidential files, with a one-line note on anything sensitive. For the perception track, run the anonymous survey about usage and reluctance, and compare its answers to the leadership estimates.

Two honesty rules. Record what you find without judging anyone; the goal is ground truth, not a list of offenders. And pair disclosure with observation, because a baseline built only from voluntary answers undercounts, and more than half our workforce is reluctant to admit how much they rely on AI. [^1]

Close by writing the baseline down in four lines, one per track, each with a number if you have one. That single page is the reference you will carry through the rest of the course, into the next lesson where you turn it into goals.

## What's next

You now have an honest picture of where your organization actually stands on AI: the tools in use, the workflows that use them, the data flowing through them, and the gap between what your leaders believed and what the audit found. That baseline is the raw material every future decision gets measured against.

The danger of a baseline is that it sits there, a valuable fact with no direction. Lesson 5 removes that risk. It takes the audit you just produced and turns it into something you can steer the whole program by: setting realistic goals and a north-star metric. You will learn how to choose one metric that captures whether AI is actually moving the organization, how to set targets that are ambitious enough to matter but honest enough to hit, and how to keep the program aligned to that single number instead of drowning in dashboards. Bring your four-line baseline, because it is exactly the starting value a north-star metric needs.

For now, hold the two ideas this lesson gave you. Adoption runs ahead of leadership's belief, through tools nobody approved, on the most important work in the building, so the baseline exists to find the truth your reports were not showing. And ground truth, painful as it can be to discover, is the only foundation a governed, measurable, scalable program can be built on.
