Every earlier lesson in this course treated AI as a thing to govern: who uses it, what goes in it, how it is supervised, how it is audited. This lesson moves the question to money. The uncomfortable fact is that a company can run a perfectly governed AI program and still blow its budget, because the budget is where most organizations discover they were not really in control.

Think about what happened before AI. Software budgeting was mostly an exercise in seats. A vendor charged a flat monthly fee per named user, you predicted headcount a year out, and your IT budget was roughly headcount times price. The line item was stable, boring, and easy to defend. AI does not behave that way. A large share of AI spend is usage-driven. It scales with adoption, and it spikes when a workflow works, because the more useful an automation is, the more people hand it work.

This lesson gives you a budgeting method that fits that shape. You will split the spend into three buckets, size each one to reality, attribute cost to the team that owns the value, and put controls on the part that can move on its own. By the end you should be able to build a defensible AI budget and explain where every dollar goes.

## Why the old budgeting habit fails you

Start with the scale. AI is becoming a material line item fast. BCG's Applied AI 2026 survey found respondents reporting that AI's share of their IT budget more than tripled, from 5% to 17%, and companies now spend roughly 3.3% of revenue on enterprise IT plus another 2.8% of revenue on AI initiatives funded outside the Tech/IT budget.[^1] So AI money is not only growing, it is increasingly sitting outside the budget you would naturally reach for.

The growth is real, but so is the pressure to control it. In McKinsey's 2026 state-of-AI survey, about one in five organizations reported that AI-related operating costs, including token costs, were constraining their AI use.[^2] Zip's analysis of approved purchase requests across a trillion dollars of enterprise spend shows the same tension from the buying side: AI vendors' share of software spend rose from 1.4% to 8.1% in a year, while 21% of purchase requests involving AI vendors were rejected or canceled, compared with 12% for other vendors.[^3] Companies are spending more on AI and getting pickier about what they approve. That is the environment your budget has to survive.

The reason traditional habits break is structural, not organizational. AI costs are usage-driven, not seat-driven, and they are owned by no single function, which makes them structurally hard to forecast.[^4] A support automation that returns no value costs nothing, until it works, and then it costs more every month because everyone routes work to it. That is a feature of the technology, not a bug in your planning. The budget simply has to be built around it.

## Define the terms before anything else

Five terms carry this lesson, and teams that skip defining them end up arguing about "the AI budget" while meaning different things.

**Per-seat pricing.** A flat monthly fee per named user. This is the model traditional SaaS used and it is still common for AI assistants. Its cost is a function of headcount, which means it is predictable and easy to approve, but it bills every licensed user whether or not they sign in.[^5]

**Usage-based, or token, pricing.** A charge for what is actually consumed, usually measured in tokens, the units of text a model processes. Its cost is a function of work, not headcount, so it scales with real adoption and carries the least predictable bill unless you add caps and monitoring.[^4][^5]

**Attribution.** The practice of tagging each AI request so it can be traced back to a team, a workflow, a model, and an environment. Without attribution you cannot say which team is driving spend, which means no one owns it and no one has a reason to reduce it.[^6]

**Showback and chargeback.** Two ways to turn attribution into ownership. Showback gives a team visibility into what it spends without moving money between budgets; it builds awareness through transparency. Chargeback transfers the actual cost to the consuming business unit's budget, creating direct financial accountability.[^6]

**Cost governance.** The structured practice of tracking, attributing, controlling, and optimizing what AI systems consume. It differs from traditional IT cost management because AI spend is variable and usage-driven, so it needs real-time controls rather than static allocations.[^6]

## Match the pricing shape to the workload

Most of what you will buy fits into a few pricing models, and each one has a different predictability and a different failure mode. Zylo, which tracks enterprise software spend, groups them into per-seat subscriptions, usage-based and token pricing, tiered and hybrid models, and outcome or agent-based pricing.[^4] You do not need to pick one. You need to know which shape each purchase has, because that decides whether it is forecastable and where the controls go.

Per-seat pricing is the easiest to forecast and the easiest to overbuy. Because the price is flat, it is simple to keep adding seats faster than adoption justifies. The telltale waste is in the utilization. ibl.ai's comparison of the two models notes that in practice a small slice of licensed users, often 10-20%, generates the large majority of AI activity, while a per-seat contract charges full price for everyone else.[^5] Below a few hundred users the two models are close enough that either works. Past roughly a thousand users the gap stops being a line item and becomes the budget.[^5] So the fix for per-seat is simple even if it sounds obvious: license the people who will actually use the tool, not the whole floor.

Usage-based pricing is the opposite shape. It has the lowest entry cost because you pay only for what you consume, but the bill is the least predictable, because spend rises with activity and rarely comes with built-in caps. That makes consumption-based pricing the hardest structure to budget for once adoption scales.[^4] This is the bucket that needs the controls. A usage-based workload without rate limits and monitoring is a bill you will meet in the mail.

The general rule matches most budgeting instincts: price the stable parts for predictability, and put controls wherever the bill can move on its own. If a team's usage is steady and broad, per-seat gives you a clean number. If usage is variable or experimental, usage-based is cheaper to enter but only works if you pair it with caps, alerts, and regular reviews.[^4]

## Build the budget in three buckets

Here is the method. Go back to the baseline audit you ran earlier in this course, the one that told you who in the organization actually uses AI. Budget against that reality, not against headcount, and split the money into three buckets.

**Bucket one, the central foundation, is fixed.** Platform integration, security controls, governance tooling, data safety, training, and the people who run the program. These are the costs of owning AI responsibly, and they do not move with usage. Fund them centrally and treat them as infrastructure. This bucket is the smallest share of the program for most companies, but it is the one you cannot cut without sacrificing the governance this course has spent twenty-six lessons building.

**Bucket two, seats, is sized to active users, not the whole company.** Take the number of employees who actually use the assistant and license those seats. Do not license the whole department because the headcount number looks round. A budget that funds four hundred seats for a company where three hundred people are active is a budget that quietly pays for a hundred unused licenses, which at twenty-five dollars a seat is roughly thirty thousand dollars a year. That money is not producing value; it is producing contract value.

**Bucket three, usage, is capped and attributed.** This is the volatile part: tokens, API calls, automation agents. Budget it as a range, not a point, with a hard ceiling, and assign every dollar to the team that owns the workflow producing it. This is where showback and chargeback do their work, and where the governance layer matters most.

![The three-bucket AI budget: a fixed central foundation, seats sized to active users, and a capped, attributed usage pool, each with its own control](</diagrams/budgeting-ai-spend-across-teams/1-three-bucket-budget.png>)

## Use showback first, chargeback once the data is trustworthy

Attribution is what makes this whole structure hold together, but you cannot charge back money you cannot tag. The MLflow guide to AI cost governance makes the sequencing concrete: roughly 90% tagging coverage is required before a chargeback model can be applied fairly. Below that, untagged costs create disputes and erode trust. Most organizations start with showback, which gives teams visibility without moving money, and graduate to chargeback once tagging is solid enough to be fair.[^6]

The practical model distributes responsibility on purpose. A central team sets the tagging taxonomy and the rules, while individual teams own their budgets and are accountable for their consumption. MLflow calls this split the answer to two failure modes: a central team that cannot enforce accountability at scale, and distributed teams that optimize locally without the full picture.[^6] So someone central owns the system that tags and reports, and each department head owns the number that lands on their standing agenda.

Showback creates awareness without penalty, which is the right first step when adoption is still fragile. Chargeback creates accountability, which is the right step once data is reliable, because it makes the department that benefits from the automation also pay for it, which aligns incentives with efficiency.

## Put controls on the part that can move

Visibility is not control, and dashboards tell you what happened, they do not stop the overrun. The AI cost governance layer turns policy into runtime enforcement. MLflow lists the operational controls that make it real: budget caps set at the team, project, or environment level; rate limits that throttle calls before a ceiling is hit; anomaly detection that flags unusual patterns in real time; kill switches that shut down a runaway workload; and approval gates that require sign-off before a high-cost model reaches production.[^6] This is the difference between governance and after-the-fact reporting: reporting tells you what happened, governance stops it while it is still happening.

Two small practices make the layer far more useful. Set your anomaly and alert thresholds at around 80% of the cap, not 100%, so you have time to investigate before you hit the limit.[^6] And split the roles: finance owns budgeting, forecasting, and approval workflows; IT owns the technical controls that enforce them at runtime; procurement handles vendor terms and contract discipline. When those three act in silos, costs slip between them.[^6]

![Governance stops the overrun while it is happening: budget caps, rate limits, anomaly detection, kill switches, and approval gates, with alerts set at 80 percent of the cap and finance, IT, and procurement each owning a piece](</diagrams/budgeting-ai-spend-across-teams/4-governance-controls.png>)

The discipline of FinOps, adapted for AI, is the umbrella term for this work: forecasting token demand, enforcing value thresholds, and approving only projects that meet them.[^4] You do not need the jargon. You need the habit. Budget, cap, measure, review.

## Unit economics is the lens that makes the budget defensible

Here is the trap in gross numbers: a team sees a six-figure AI line and panics, or a sponsor sees the same number and shrugs because adoption is up. Neither reads the number correctly. The number that matters is unit economics, the cost per completed task (or per customer resolved, per draft produced, per report generated), not cost per API call. A budget that grows because it now resolves three times as many support tickets for a per-task cost that is flat or falling is a healthy budget, even when the headline number rises.

So define one unit metric per major workflow and track it monthly. Is the support automation's cost per resolved ticket steady while volume climbs? Is the marketing drafting pipeline cheaper per asset than the old agency route? That trend, not the absolute spend, is what tells you the money is working. It is also the bridge to the next lesson, because a cost-per-unit figure is the numerator that makes an ROI story possible.

## Worked example: Dhara budgets the AI program

Dhara owns AI adoption at a company of about eight hundred and twenty people. Her earlier baseline audit found three hundred employees actually using AI day to day, concentrated in support and engineering, with lighter adoption in sales and marketing. She is now asked to produce the coming year's AI budget and to defend it to the CFO.

She starts by defusing the instinct to buy a seat for everyone. If she licensed all eight hundred and twenty employees at twenty-five dollars a month, that is over twenty thousand dollars a month, roughly two hundred and forty-six thousand dollars a year, and most of those seats would belong to people who touch the tool a couple of times a week. ibl.ai's pattern holds: a minority of licensed users generate most of the activity, so a headcount-sized seat plan overpays for the quiet majority.[^5] Dhara sizes seats to her three hundred active users instead.

She builds the budget in three buckets.

The central foundation is fixed: integration, security controls, governance tooling, training, and a part-time program owner. This runs about three thousand dollars a month, thirty-six thousand a year. It is the smallest bucket and the one she will not touch.

The seat bucket is active users only. Three hundred seats at twenty-five dollars a month is seven thousand five hundred dollars a month, ninety thousand a year.

The usage bucket is the interesting one. Support runs an automation that resolves about sixty thousand assistance tasks a month, and engineering's coding agents carry large context windows. Dhara models token spend using mid-range frontier API pricing, roughly three dollars per million input tokens and fifteen dollars per million output tokens, which she labels clearly as an assumption she will revisit. That model comes to about eight thousand four hundred dollars a month in usage. She budgets thirty percent headroom and sets a hard ceiling of eleven thousand dollars a month, so the usage bucket is capped at about one hundred and thirty-two thousand a year.

Her full budget ceiling is roughly two hundred and fifty-eight thousand dollars a year. Then comes the attribution, and this is where the CFO's question gets answered.

Looking at the monthly numbers behind that total, the picture is concentrated. Engineering's coding workloads come to about eight thousand seven hundred a month, and support is around three and a half thousand. Sales, marketing, and the rest of the org are each under two thousand. Between them, support and engineering account for about ninety-five percent of the usage spend. That concentration is the story: two owned workflows are carrying the program, and the other teams are cheap. If someone proposes cutting the AI budget by twenty percent, Dhara can now point at exactly which two workflows that would gut.

She also reads the unit economics. Support's automation is about six cents per task including its share of the seat cost. As volume grows, she will watch that number: if it stays flat or falls while task count climbs, the automation is getting more efficient, not just more expensive. If it rises, she has a signal to investigate before the CFO has to.

Finally she wires the governance. The usage bucket is tagged to teams and capped, with alerts set at eighty percent of each team's ceiling, so engineering gets a flag before it blows through its cap rather than after. Showback reports go to the department heads monthly. She holds off on full chargeback until tagging coverage clears the bar where it would be fair. Finance owns the forecast, IT owns the runtime controls, and the monthly review reads one page of unit economics alongside the spend.

That is the whole method in action. Fixed foundation, seats sized to real users, and a capped, attributed pool for the work that grows, read through unit economics on a monthly cadence.

## Try it: draft your organization's AI budget

For the next budgeting cycle, build a three-bucket draft for your own organization. Use the baseline you already have.

First, list every current AI expense you can find, including the ones bought on cards outside procurement, because that shadow spend lands in your budget whether or not you account for it.[^3][^4] Sort each line by its pricing shape: fixed per-seat, usage-based, tiered or hybrid, or outcome-based. Second, size bucket two to the active users from your baseline audit, not to total headcount, and cut the seats that belong to the quiet majority. Third, for bucket three, model your variable usage as a range with assumptions noted, add headroom, set a hard ceiling, and tag every dollar to the team that owns the workflow. Fourth, assign budget owners and split finance, IT, and procurement responsibilities. Fifth, choose one unit economics metric per major workflow and commit to read it on the same monthly cadence as the spend.

When you finish, it should survive one test: point at your largest AI line item and say which team owns it, what task it completes, its cost per task, and the ceiling that stops it from running away. If you can answer all four, you have a budget, not a figure. If you cannot, that is the gap to close.

## What's next

This lesson gave you a budget that can survive AI's cost shape: a fixed foundation, seats sized to real users, and a capped, attributed pool for the usage that grows, watched through unit economics on a monthly cadence. But a budget only holds the spending in check; it does not by itself tell you whether the money is worth it. A cost-per-task figure is a measure, and a measure without a verdict is just a number. Lesson 28 is the piece that turns the measure into a decision: proving the program's value to the business, building the ROI case that turns a defensible budget into an investment leadership re-ups with confidence.
