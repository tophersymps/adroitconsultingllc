Lesson 12 ended with you rebuilding trust with the user who got burned. That recovery worked because you found out what happened. But here is the uncomfortable question this lesson is built on: how did you find out, and would you have found out at all if the person had not told you? Most adoption owners run their programs on what people say they will do, or say they are doing, and that is not the same thing as what they actually do. It is a feedback problem, and it is usually invisible until it is too late.

A feedback loop is the system that feeds what people actually do back into how you run the program, instead of relying on what they say. The people in your organization are leaving a stream of honest signals every day: which tools they use, which they abandon, where they correct output, where they quietly route around the approved tool. Those signals are more truthful than most of what anyone tells you in a survey. This lesson gives you the discipline of reading them and the habit of closing the loop, so the adoption program gets better from real evidence rather than slowly going stale on assumption.

## The words we are using

A **feedback loop** is a process in which you collect information about what is actually happening, read it against a decision, act on what you learn, and then feed the result back so the next round is better informed. The word loop matters as much as the word feedback: a one-time survey is not a loop. A loop repeats, and each pass through it uses what the last pass taught. When a program learns from its users this way, it is said to be **closing the loop**, meaning the information collected turns into a change that users can then feed back on again. The moment the information goes in and nothing comes back, the loop is broken.

The reason the loop is hard is that most of the information you could collect is distorted. The cleanest way to see this is a distinction that runs through all of the behavioral research: the difference between **stated intention** and **enacted behavior**. What a person says they intend to do, or says they are doing, and what they actually do are two different things, and the gap between them is large enough to build a whole program on the wrong evidence.

Consider the study that measures this gap directly for AI tools. Researchers surveyed 310 university students in the Netherlands about their intention to use ChatGPT and their actual use of it. Intention ran clearly ahead of behavior: 62.6 percent of respondents reported an intention to use ChatGPT as students, but only 47.1 percent reported actually using it, a gap of more than fifteen percentage points. [^1] The researchers also found something quietly important for an adoption owner: the factors that predicted intention were not the same as the factors that predicted use. Trust predicted whether someone said they would use it, but not whether they actually did. Observability, how visible acceptable use was around them, predicted actual use but not intention. [^1] People who felt good about the tool in the abstract did not necessarily use it, and people who used it did so partly because they could see others doing the same. If you manage to how people feel, you are tracking the wrong number.

![Stated intention versus enacted behavior: among 310 Dutch university students surveyed about ChatGPT, 62.6 percent reported an intention to use it while only 47.1 percent reported actually using it, and trust predicted intention while observability predicted actual use](</diagrams/feedback-loops-learning-from-what-people-actually-use/2-stated-vs-enacted-behavior.png>)

## Why what people say is not what people do

This is not a ChatGPT quirk. It is the intention-behavior gap, one of the most studied findings in applied psychology: people consistently overstate follow-through. New Year's resolutions are the homely example. Almost everyone has set one, and almost everyone has let it die by February. The plans were sincere. The behavior did not follow, because the behavior is shaped by the present moment, by friction, by whether the situation makes the action easy, while the plan was made in a calm, future-facing moment. [^2] Your people are not lying to you. They are doing exactly what all people do: reporting on their intention as if it were their action, and remembering their past action as rosier than it was.

There are three specific mechanisms at work, and each one corrupts adoption evidence in a predictable way.

**Social desirability.** People answer questions the way they think they should, especially when the question comes from leadership. If the organization has pronounced AI important, almost nobody tells the adoption owner "I am not really using it." They say they are using it more than they are, because it is the expected answer. A survey about an approved initiative measures, in part, how eager people are to comply with what you clearly want to hear.

**Recalling a feeling as a fact.** Earlier lessons showed how unreliable self-reported time savings are, because people are poor judges of how long tasks take. [^3] The same problem shadows self-reported usage. Whether someone "uses AI a lot" is a memory of a feeling, an impression, not a count, and the impression drifts toward the favorable. It is not a measurement. It is a recollection dressed up as one.

**The observer's incentive.** Anyone who collects usage by asking has quietly incentivized the wrong answer. The tool is approved and championed. There is no penalty for saying you use it more than you do, and there may be a social one for admitting you dropped it. So the report leans optimistic, and the person running the program gets a dashboard of enthusiasm that reflects hope instead of use. The whole lesson of Lesson 3 was that you measure the work, not the feeling. [^3] That discipline applies here with double force, because the feedback stream is where the real data is hiding.

## The signals users leave instead of telling you

The good news is that you do not have to rely on what people say, because people leave a trail of enacted behavior wherever they go. The discipline of the feedback loop is to read that trail. Most of these signals you already collect or can collect cheaply; the problem is collapsing them into a program, a habit, instead of treating them as one-off reports.

**What they use.** The usage metrics from Lesson 3: how many eligible people use the tool, on which tasks, and how often. Usage is enacted behavior, a fact about what happened, not an opinion about it. When usage clusters on a narrow set of tasks, that tells you where the tool genuinely fits, and by subtraction, where it does not.

**What they abandon.** Churn from Lesson 3, the people who used it and stopped, is a signal, not just a number to guard. Every quiet dropout is a person leaving behind a reason for leaving. Lesson 12 taught you to recover them one at a time. The feedback loop groups those reasons and makes them legible: this task is hard to do with the tool, this workflow got slower, this output frequency bred mistrust. The aggregate of the dropouts is a map of where the tool is not earning its place.

**Where they correct.** The cheap-check habit from Lesson 2 and the quality checks from Lesson 3 produce a stream of corrections, not just a quality rate. A pattern of corrections on a specific type of task is concrete evidence of the task-tool boundary from Lesson 12, stated in behavior instead of admitted in conversation. If people keep fixing the citations or re-drafting the same class of output, the boundary is announcing itself in action.

**Where they route around.** The most honest signal of all is the workaround. When people stop using the approved tool and quietly buy their own subscription, or paste text into another one, or reopen the old spreadsheet, they are voting with their feet. Later lessons in this course deal with shadow IT as a governance problem. In the feedback sense, it is the clearest possible statement that the approved tool is not matching the job, and it is a signal you want to hear early, not discover at audit time.

None of these requires a survey. They are all records of what people actually did, collected as a byproduct of them working. That is what makes them a feedback loop rather than a polling exercise: they are behavior, not belief.

## Closing the loop: the move that makes the others count

Collecting signals is half the loop. The other half, and the part that most programs skip, is closing it: taking what the signals say, making a decision, and feeding that decision back so users see their behavior shaped the program. This is the stage that turns data into improvement, and it is the stage that keeps the data coming.

There are two reasons people stop contributing to a feedback loop, and both are failures to close it. First, if the information you collect never changes anything, you have asked for it and ignored it, and people can tell. They stop offering signal because it had no consequence. Second, and more subtly, if the loop only ever collects and never feeds back, you cannot learn either; you are consuming information without acting, which is data hoarding, not feedback. Closing the loop is the act of turning the collected signal into a visible change: adjust the workflow, move the tool off a task it does badly, recalibrate training, expand a use case that is working, and then tell people what you changed and why.

The closing does not have to be large, but it has to be visible and it has to be honest. When a user sees that the task they kept correcting is now routed differently, or the friction they reported is gone, they learn two things at once: the system is listening, and their use of the tool actually matters. That is the mechanism that keeps the loop alive, and it is the mechanism that lets a program compound. Each pass teaches the next.

## Worked example: Amara reads the behavior, not the talk

Amara owns AI adoption at a regional insurance agency with about 200 people. After the rollout and the training from Lesson 11, she runs her check-ins the way many owners do: she holds an end-of-quarter meeting, asks each department how AI is going, and everyone says it is going great. She comes away confident. But she happens to keep the usage numbers from Lesson 3, and when she lays the enthusiasm next to the enacted behavior, they disagree.

The stated picture: every department reports strong, growing use. The behavioral picture is different. Usage is actually concentrated in one place, the claims-support team, where 80 percent of qualified work goes through the AI drafting tool. Everywhere else, usage is thin. The customer-service team shows a spike at rollout and then a slide that matches the novelty curve from Lesson 7. The underwriting team shows almost no adoption at all despite reporting the same enthusiasm.

Amara stops trusting the meeting and starts reading the signals. She looks at churn first: the slide team is dropping off, and the stated reports gave her no warning because nobody was going to tell her in a room. She looks at corrections: in the claims team, the draft acceptance is high and corrections are low, which is behavior that says the tool truly fits there. In customer service, corrections cluster on the reply-length task, a pattern that matches the complaint the team mentioned once and then dropped.

Instead of holding another meeting, Amara closes the loop on what the behavior says. She refrains from expanding the tool to underwriting on the strength of their reported enthusiasm, because the behavior does not support it yet, and expansion on talk is how a program spends its budget on a tool nobody uses. She moves the customer-service reply-length task to a place where the tool's output is shorter and needs less fixing, a concrete change she can name. And she tells both teams what the usage data showed and what she changed because of it: the claims team hears that their genuine fit is the model the program is scaling from, and customer service hears that the friction they left signals (without saying) got fixed.

![Amara compares what each department said against what the usage behavior actually showed, then closes the loop by holding back the underwriting expansion, moving the customer-service reply-length task, and telling each team what changed and why](</diagrams/feedback-loops-learning-from-what-people-actually-use/3-amara-reads-behavior.png>)

Two quarters later the shape is different. The claims fit has deepened. Customer service has recovered off the novelty dip because the specific friction was removed. Underwriting has been onboarded slowly, on a real workflow, instead of all at once on enthusiasm. None of this came from a survey. All of it came from reading the behavior people left behind and closing the loop on it. Amara did not get better at asking. She got better at listening to what the usage was already telling her.

## Try it: read the signal trail in your own org

Take out a page and build the skeleton of a feedback loop from real behavior, starting with the gap you already know is there.

First, state the intention-behavior gap honestly in your context. If you asked your people today whether they use AI, what would they say, and how likely is that to match the usage numbers? Write the stated version next to the behavioral version, and mark where you suspect they diverge. That gap is what your loop exists to close.

Second, list the signals you already have. Which of the four from this lesson do you collect or could collect: what people use, what they abandon, where they correct, and where they route around the approved tool? Write each one down and note whether it currently feeds a decision or just sits in a report. A signal that feeds no decision is not yet part of a loop.

Third, pick one signal and read it. Choose the single most honest trail in your org, likely the churn or the correction pattern, and write down what it is saying about one use case. Name the task it points to, the boundary it marks, and the friction it names.

Fourth, design the close. Decide the smallest visible change you can make from that signal: adjust one workflow, recalibrate one piece of training, pause one expansion. Then write how you will tell people you made it and why. The close is what separates a feedback loop from a data dump, and a program that learns from one that just listens.

## What's next

This lesson gave you the feedback loop: the discipline of learning from what people actually do instead of what they say, reading the signals they leave as they work and stop working, and closing the loop so the program improves from real evidence rather than going stale on assumption. You built the missing organ of an adoption program: the part that listens to behavior.

But reading behavior tells you where the tool is being trusted. It does not tell you how much trust is safe. The next lesson is the supervision bar: where a person must be in the loop before AI output is allowed to affect real decisions, and how your organization sets that bar consistently instead of leaving it to chance. Lesson 14, on the human-in-the-loop supervision bar for your org, turns the trust you are now learning to measure into a policy you can hold people to, so the feedback loop improves a program that is also safe to run.
