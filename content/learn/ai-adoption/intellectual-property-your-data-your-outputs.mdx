In the last lesson you made sure you could leave a model provider. That was the resilience move: build your program so a vendor's change does not trap you. But there is a quieter risk hiding in the same workflow, and it does not announce itself until you try to enforce a right. It is intellectual property, and it appears in two directions.

**Your data going in.** When someone pastes a client proposal, a pricing sheet, or a process document into an AI tool, that material leaves your building. The question is what the vendor is allowed to do with it. Many teams assume an enterprise tool never trains on their data and never turn that assumption into something verifiable. When it is wrong, confidential material can wind up embedded in a model the vendor serves to other customers.

**Your outputs coming out.** When your team drafts a marketing piece, a policy, or a training document with AI, who owns what comes out? The answer is more fragile than most people think. A contract that says the customer owns the output does not always mean the output is protected against copying. There is a hard legal limit, and it is the single fact to internalize in this lesson: in the United States, copyright protects only the product of human creativity, and a purely AI-generated output has no copyright at all.[^1]

This lesson is about both directions, the data you feed in and the work that comes out, and what a non-technical owner should actually do about each. You are not here to become a lawyer. You are here to stop treating intellectual property as an afterthought and to build the small set of habits that keep your data and your most valuable outputs from being quietly unclaimed.

## The words you need

**Copyright** is a set of exclusive rights that attaches to original works of authorship: the right to reproduce, distribute, display, perform, and prepare derivative works.[^2] It does not protect ideas, facts, or methods. It protects the particular expression of them. For your purposes the important feature is that copyright is the main legal tool that stops someone from copying the documents, images, and other content your organization produces. If you do not have it, you cannot sue a copier, full stop.

**Human authorship requirement** is the rule that copyright protects only material created by a human being. The US Copyright Office states this plainly: it will not register works "produced by a machine or mere mechanical process that operates randomly or automatically without any creative input or intervention from a human author."[^3] A court has held that human authorship is an essential part of a valid copyright claim.[^4] This matters because when an AI tool determines the expressive elements of an output, that output is not the product of human authorship and is not protected by copyright.[^5]

**Work made for hire** is the copyright rule that, when an employee creates a qualifying work within the scope of their employment, the employer, not the employee, is treated as the author and owns the copyright from the start.[^6] This is the rule that says "if an employee writes it for the job, the company owns it."

**Purpose limitation** is a privacy and data-protection principle: data collected for one purpose may not be repurposed for a different one without a separate basis.[^7] Applied to AI, it means a vendor that takes your data to provide a service cannot silently turn around and use that same data to train its models, unless your agreement lets them.

**Training data** is the material a model learns from. **Prompt** is the instruction you type in. **Output**, also called generation, is what the model returns. These three words separate the ownership questions: who owns the training data you guard, who owns the prompt you type, and who owns the output the model returns. Different rules and different contracts govern each.

## There is only one fact to hold, and it has sharp edges

The whole discipline of this lesson traces back to one fact: copyright follows human authorship, and a prompt alone does not make you the author of what the model produces. The US Copyright Office's March 2023 registration guidance is the clearest statement. When you give a generative AI tool a prompt and it produces text or an image, the expressive elements, the words chosen, the composition, the color, are determined by the program, not by you. The office calls prompts more like instructions to a commissioned artist than like authorship: they say what you want depicted, and the machine decides how to depict it.[^3] Because the machine determined the expression, that material is not protected by copyright.[^5]

This has a practical consequence that shocks most teams the first time they hear it. An employee who types a prompt into a tool and pastes the result almost unchanged into a company document has, in the eyes of the copyright office, produced material that nobody owns as copyright. The company has usable words, but it does not have a protectable work. If a competitor copies that exact page, there may be no copyright to enforce.[^8]

The line is not "any use of AI kills your rights." The office has been clear that using AI to assist in creation does not bar copyrightability at all. In its January 2025 report on the copyrightability of AI outputs, it concluded that outputs can be protected where a human author has determined sufficient expressive elements.[^9] That happens in three concrete ways: when a human-authored work is perceptible in the AI output, when a human makes creative arrangements or modifications of the output, or when a human combines AI-generated material into a larger human-authored work.[^9][^10]

The governing question is the one from the guidance: was the work basically of human authorship, with AI as an assisting instrument, or did the machine actually conceive and execute the traditional elements of expression?[^3] Edit, shape, combine, and the work can be protectable. Paste it out unchanged, and it is not. That distinction is the whole lesson in one sentence, and it should change how you run anything that produces client-facing or otherwise valuable content.

## What your contract says about your data is more important than what the marketing page says

Now the other direction. Every time your people feed company data into a tool, there is a separate ownership and control question the vendor's terms answer, and the answer is often buried in boilerplate. The single highest value clause in an AI vendor agreement is the one that states whether the vendor may use your data, your prompts, and your generated outputs to train or improve its models.[^11] Lawyers who draft these contracts treat this as the clause to find first, because if the vendor can train on your data, your confidential information can end up absorbed into a model the vendor later serves to other customers, including your competitors.

The safe posture is unglamorous but powerful: the default should be that the vendor does not train on your data, prompts, inputs, or outputs unless a contract expressly allows it.[^12] An enterprise tool that markets "we never train on your data" is not proof. The proof is the written data-use clause. Contracting guidance is consistent on what to ask for in that clause: an express confirmation that the vendor will not use your data for training or improvement without your consent, segregation of your data from other customers' data, de-identification standards with a prohibition on re-identification, and confirmation that these restrictions survive an assignment or change of control of the vendor.[^11][^13]

Why does this matter to a non-technical owner? Because of what trade secrets require. A trade secret, a proprietary process, a pricing model, an evaluation method, keeps its legal status only while it stays secret. The moment a valued process is typed into a prompt and sent to a model that trains on it, the secrecy that the trade secret depends on can be compromised, and the vendor's confidentiality terms may not be drafted broadly enough to cover model interaction data.[^12] The people who draft AI contracts warn that general guidance in the AI space is to avoid inputting confidential information if the model trains from customer data at all.[^12]

In plain terms, before your team routes anything sensitive into a tool, you should know the answer to one question: does the vendor train on it, and does the contract say so? If the answer is unclear, that is the moment to check, not assume. Most vendors offer enterprise terms that exclude customer data from training, but the protections vary by vendor and must be verified in the actual agreement, not taken from a marketing claim.[^14]

## Ownership is not a single answer, it is five stacked answers

People ask "who owns AI output" as if there were one line in a law book. There is not. The practical answer is that ownership resolves through five separate questions, and they stack on top of each other. Walk them in order.

![Ownership resolves through five stacked questions: is the output copyrightable, was it made within the scope of employment, did a contractor create it, what do the vendor's terms say, and what are you actually trying to protect](</diagrams/intellectual-property-your-data-your-outputs/3-five-stacked-ownership-questions.png>)

**Is the output copyrightable at all?** This is the human authorship question you just read. If a human determined the expressive elements, the work, or enough of it, can be protected. If the machine determined everything and it was pasted out unchanged, it is not protected.[^9]

**Was it made within the scope of employment?** If the copyrightable work was created by an employee within the scope of their job, the work made for hire rule gives ownership to the employer.[^6] An employee drafting marketing material as part of their role produces work the company owns, to the extent it is protectable.

**Did a contractor create it?** Contractors are different. Commissioned works count as works made for hire only in a narrow set of categories and only with a written agreement.[^15] Otherwise the contractor, not your company, initially owns the copyright unless there is a written assignment. This is why written copyright assignments matter for outside creative help.

**What does the vendor's terms say about outputs?** Many AI providers contractually assign ownership of outputs to the customer. That assignment is real and useful as a matter of contract. But it has a hard limit you need to understand: a contract can assign the rights the provider holds, and it cannot manufacture a copyright that federal law does not recognize. If the output has no copyright because there is no human authorship, then "you own the output" does not give you a copyright you can enforce against a copier.[^10]

**What are you actually trying to protect?** This is the question most organizations skip, and it is the one that turns all of the above into action. Not everything needs a copyright. A draft summary, a terse internal note, these can be useful business output with no need for the enforcement rights of copyright. What needs protection is the content that is genuinely valuable and copyable: a brand tagline, a flagship white paper, the teaching materials you sell, the case study that wins deals. Decide which outputs are load-bearing, then make sure those are the ones with real human authorship behind them.

The way these stack, in one worked example, is a firm whose marketing assistant generates copy by feeding a tool a one-line prompt and pasting the output into a brochure. No meaningful editing. That copy, however useful, is not copyrighted. If a competitor lifts the brochure, the firm has little recourse. Had the assistant instead drafted the structure, edited heavily, and added original human framing around the generated draft, the result could qualify for protection as a work in which human expression is perceptible.[^9]

## Worked example: the firm that could not protect its own best document

Let me run the full discipline through a concrete case. It is an illustration, not a real company, but the shape is common, and it shows both directions failing at once.

A consulting firm of about 200 people decides the way to scale is to use AI to draft its flagship offer documents: the thick capability statements it sends to prospects, which are its single best selling asset. The approach is fast and the team loves it. A writer feeds each document's requirements into a tool, gets a draft, tightens a headline here and a paragraph there, and ships it. Clients respond well. Six months in, leadership asks a question nobody had asked before: are these documents protected?

The answer is uncomfortable. Because the writers accepted most of the machine's expression and edited only lightly, the office's analysis makes the outputs largely unprotectable: the traditional elements of expression were determined by the AI, not by the writers, and light editing does not necessarily rise to the level of human authorship over the whole.[^3][^8] The firm's crown jewel content has no copyright it can enforce. A departing employee could hand the same draft to a competitor, or a competitor could mirror it, and the firm would have little legal recourse. The tool produced volume, and volume without protectable authorship is not an asset.

The data side fails in the same run. The contract the firm signed with the tool is a standard self-serve agreement, and the firm never read the data-use clause. Buried there is language allowing the vendor to use customer data, inputs, outputs, and usage information in aggregated or de-identified form to improve the service and its models.[^16] The writers, who do not know the clause exists, have been pasting including confidential client strategy and pricing assumptions into the tool. That material is now available for the vendor to fold into model training. The firm's confidential process detail is a trade secret only while it stays secret, and the secrecy is now doubtful.[^12]

Now rerun it with the two habits in place. First, before any procurement, the firm found the data-use clause and insisted on a written confirmation that the vendor does not train on customer data, prompts, inputs, or outputs, with segregation and de-identification standards and survival through change of control.[^11][^13] Now the confidential strategy and pricing stay usable without becoming training material. Second, the firm reclassified its flagship documents as load-bearing and changed the workflow. Writers now produce original human-authored drafts, use AI to draft alternatives and suggest improvements, then select, arrange, and substantially edit the material into a final work with clear human expression. That final work can be protected, and the firm knows to disclose, not hide, the presence of AI-generated material when it registers, claiming protection only for its own human contributions.[^5][^10]

The lesson is not "don't use AI." It is "know what you are producing and feed it only what your contract lets a vendor keep." A firm that treats its most valuable output as something to protect, and its confidential data as something to guard, gets the speed of AI and keeps the rights.

## What a non-technical owner actually does

This sounds like law, and some of it ends with a lawyer. But most of it is governance a non-technical owner can run. The moves that keep your data and outputs protected are these.

**Find the data-use clause before you sign.** For every AI tool your team uses on real data, your procurement owner should confirm in writing whether the vendor trains on customer data, prompts, inputs, and outputs. The clean answer to shop for is: no training without your consent, with segregation and de-identification standards.[^13] This one clause decides most of your data-side risk, and it is cheap to check at procurement and expensive to discover later.

**Route sensitive data by the color rule you already built.** The red, yellow, green data classification from the policy and data-safety lessons applies here with extra force. Red data, confidential strategy, pricing, client records, should not go near a tool whose training terms you have not verified as no-train. When in doubt about a tool's training, treat the data as red.[^8]

**Decide which outputs are load-bearing, then protect those.** Not every piece of AI output needs to be copyrightable. Make a short list of the content your business genuinely depends on: the flagship documents, the registered brand asset, the teaching material you sell. For those, require real human authorship: original drafting, selection, arrangement, and substantial editing that leaves human expression perceptible in the final work. That is what the law protects, and it costs nothing but discipline.[^9]

**Tell employees the authorship rule, not just the safety rule.** The person doing the work cannot protect the work if they do not know the difference between a pasted output and an authored document. Add two sentences to your acceptable use policy: pasted, unedited AI output is not owned as copyright, and content the company needs to protect must have genuine human authorship behind it. That one instruction changes behavior at the point of creation.

**Assign rights from contractors in writing.** Any outside creator who uses AI, or who might, should sign a written work made for hire or copyright assignment. Without it, a contractor who produces protected material owns it, not you, and change of control and early stage rights can drift.[^15]

**Keep the record of human authorship.** If you ever need to register or defend a protected work, you need to show the human contribution. Preserve drafts and notes that show a real person selecting, arranging, and substantially editing the final work, not just pasting a generation. The copyright office looks for the human expression in the output; show it exists.[^10]

## Try it

Take your organization's single most valuable content asset that AI now helps produce, a flagship brochure, the case study library, the standard response library. Do two audits on it.

First, the authorship audit. Look at the actual workflow. Is a person drafting, selecting, arranging, and substantially editing the final work, with their own expression perceptible in it? Or is the machine's output being accepted largely unchanged? Write down which it is. If it is closer to pasted output, the asset is not protected as copyright, and you have found the gap: the fixed workflow should introduce real human authorship into the final work for anything that is load-bearing.

Second, the data audit. Pick the one AI tool that touches your most confidential material. Find the contract, not the marketing page, and locate the clause that says what the vendor may do with your data, your prompts, and your outputs. Specifically, does it allow training or model improvement on your data, in any form, even aggregated or de-identified? Write down the exact answer and the sentence that supports it. If it allows training without your clear consent, that is the clause to renegotiate at the next renewal, and the data it touches should be treated as red until you do.

Keep both findings where your program documentation lives, and revisit them at the regular vendor health review. An output you know is protected is an asset. A data use you have verified is a risk you have closed. Both are one page of work, and both are the difference between using AI at speed and giving your rights away with it.

## What's next

You now know the two directions of intellectual property risk: the data you feed into a tool and the outputs you get back, who owns each, and how to protect the work your business depends on. But there is a boundary this lesson has been circling the whole time. IP is about the stuff you put in and get out. The next question is about the systems that increasingly do the work on their own once you let them run.

Lesson 25 is Guardrails for agentic and automated AI. Watch for an AI agent that your team sets loose to draft, file, or act without a person at the wheel on every step. That is the step beyond prompts and outputs, and it raises a harder control problem: not what data goes in or what content comes out, but whether an autonomous system should be doing the action at all, and what limits, oversight, and human boarding pass keep it from running past the line. IP is about your rights to the work. Guardrails are about keeping the work, and the actor doing it, inside the boundaries you set. That is the natural next step after you know what you own.

For now, hold the two facts that carry this lesson. Copyright protects only what a human wrote, so protect the outputs that matter with real human authorship. And a vendor trains on your data only if the contract lets them, so check the data-use clause before you route anything sensitive through a tool. Data in, outputs out, both owned deliberately rather than assumed.
