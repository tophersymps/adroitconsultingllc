# Lesson 1: Mapping Business Requirements and KPIs to Agentforce Sales Capabilities

A sales operations director sends you one line: make our reps faster on inbound leads. Two days later you have an agent in a sandbox that drafts a reply to every single inbound message, plus a token bill from the many people who only wanted a price list. The director is not impressed. You built the newest thing you could build instead of the thing the metric asked for, and the number that mattered did not move.

That failure has a specific cause. Agentforce Sales is not one product. It is a family, and the members of that family answer different questions at very different costs. The tempting move when a requirement lands is to reach for the agentic layer, because it is the most capable and the most visible, and because it is what the customer is excited about. Capability without fit is just cost. The exam is built around exactly this judgment, and it rewards the candidate who reads the requirement and names the layer before naming a feature.

This lesson serves requirement sl-1a in the Sales Lifecycle domain, which carries 20 percent of the exam. You will learn to take a business requirement, a metric, or a stated challenge and decide which piece of Agentforce Sales is the right answer. That means knowing what each layer does, which mechanism moves which metric, and how to tell a predictive feature from a generative one by the verb the business used.

## Deep Dive

### Agentforce Sales is a family with four layers

Agentforce Sales names a stack, not a button. The bottom layer is the Salesforce object model and the processes a rep already works in: Leads, Accounts, Contacts, Opportunities, Quotes, Campaigns, and the stages and record types that govern them. On top of that sit the Einstein predictive features, which train a model on your historical data and return a score or a prediction. Beside those sit the Sales Cloud productivity apps, chiefly Sales Engagement for rep cadences and Salesforce Inbox for email and calendar work. The top layer is Agentforce agents, which reason in natural language and take multi-step actions through topics and actions defined in Agent Builder.

![The four layers of Agentforce Sales as a stack: the Salesforce object model of Leads, Contacts, Opportunities, Quotes and Campaigns at the foundation, Einstein predictive features that train on historical data and return a score, the Sales Cloud productivity apps Sales Engagement and Salesforce Inbox, and Agentforce agents that reason and act through topics and actions in Agent Builder, with a note on how each layer is bought.](</diagrams/day-01-sl-1a-mapping-business-requirements-and-kpis-to-agentfor/1-four-layers.png>)

Your job on every exam item is to name which layer the requirement actually points at. Two options often sound plausible because both are real features of the same platform, and only one matches the layer the business named. A stem that asks for better forecasting is not answered by a dashboard that displays the pipeline; it is answered by the forecasting engine that produces the roll-up. A stem that asks for reps to work a fixed sequence is not answered by a scoring model that ranks work; it is answered by a cadence engine that schedules the steps.

The four layers also differ in how they are bought, and that shapes the right answer. Einstein predictive scoring and the Sales Engagement app are per-user add-ons, while Agentforce agents draw on consumption that meters each conversation and action [^1]. When a scenario hands you a budget or a headcount, the defensible answer is usually the one that puts the expensive layer where the value is highest and leaves the high-volume, low-judgment steps to a cheaper mechanism.

### Read the metric before you pick the mechanism

KPI first, feature second. Every requirement in this objective carries a metric or a pain the business named, and the correct feature is the one whose mechanism moves that exact metric. Lift lead conversion and you are talking about lead scoring and agent-assisted qualification, where a predictive score ranks the queue and a retrieval action pulls the history a rep needs. Cut the rep hours burned on manual email sequences and the answer is Sales Engagement cadence steps and Inbox, because those are the tools that compose and schedule the touches. Improve forecast accuracy and you are configuring Opportunity Forecasting with forecast categories and a real forecast hierarchy, not building another dashboard. Shorten quote turnaround and you are looking at Opportunities with products on a price book, Quotes, and the sync settings that keep the two in step.

The strongest distractor is almost always a real feature aimed at a different metric. If a stem says managers cannot see whether the touch sequence was followed, an answer about Einstein Lead Scoring is wrong even though scoring is real, because a score ranks leads and does not schedule or track steps. Read the noun inside the metric, then ask which mechanism produces that noun. Hours saved on a sequence, a conversion rate, a forecast number, and a quote turnaround each have a different owner, and mixing them up is the most common way candidates lose points on this objective.

One worked example makes the method concrete. A stem reads: our reps do not follow up consistently, and managers cannot see whether a sequence of calls and emails was worked. The metric is consistency of touches, the noun is a sequence, and the mechanism that produces a sequence is a cadence with a work queue. Options about scoring, forecasting, and data unification are all real, and all aimed elsewhere. The cadence is the answer because it is the only option that owns the noun.

### How an Agentforce agent reasons and acts

An agent is defined in Agent Builder with a role, a set of topics, and, under each topic, actions. A topic is a category of work the agent handles, carrying the instructions and business rules that scope it, and an action is the concrete thing the agent can do. Each action is backed by a reference action: a Flow, an Apex class, or a prompt template, and every action declares at least one input and one output [^4]. The agent picks the best matching topic for a request, then decides which actions to run, checking each result before it answers. That loop runs on the Atlas reasoning engine, which classifies a request and drives the next step, deciding whether to run another action, ask a question, or respond [^5].

Grounding is the part that decides many exam answers. An agent can only reason over the data you expose to it, typically through a Data Cloud source or through a Flow that retrieves CRM records. An agent that cannot retrieve Opportunity history cannot reason about pipeline, and no amount of clever wording in the topic instructions will change that. If a scenario describes an agent giving vague or wrong answers about a record it was never wired to read, the fix is the retrieval action or the grounding source, not a longer prompt.

Keep the design narrow. A topic with a sprawling instruction and twenty attached actions is hard to test and easy to misuse, which is why the platform expects each action to declare its inputs and outputs explicitly. Reuse the standard topics and actions that ship for sales where they fit, attach an existing Flow or Apex action rather than writing new code, and keep the agent close to the object model the reps already use so its actions write to the records your reports read.

### Predictive Einstein and Agentforce answer different verbs

Einstein predictive features and Agentforce are not the same thing, and the exam tests whether you can separate them. Einstein Lead Scoring, Einstein Opportunity Scoring, and Opportunity Insights produce a score or a prediction from a model trained on your history. Lead scores rank leads by how much they resemble leads that converted before [^3]. Opportunity scores put a number from 1 to 99 on each open deal to say how likely it is to be won, alongside the factors that moved the score up or down [^2]. These features rank and surface. They do not converse, and they do not take a sequence of actions on their own.

Agentforce agents reason and act. They hold a conversation, plan steps, call actions, and write results back to records. A stem that says predict which leads will convert wants Einstein scoring. A stem that says an agent should research an inbound lead, draft a reply, and book a meeting wants an Agentforce agent. When both look possible, read the verb: rank and predict is Einstein, reason and act is Agentforce. A requirement to know which opportunities to prioritize is a number, and it belongs to Einstein Opportunity Scoring, so an answer that proposes a custom agent topic for that job is answering a different question.

![A side-by-side comparison of Einstein predictive features and Agentforce agents: Einstein trains a model on your historical data and returns a score or a prediction such as opportunity scores from 1 to 99, it ranks and surfaces, it does not converse and does not take a sequence of actions on its own, and it answers a stem that says predict which leads will convert; Agentforce runs on the Atlas reasoning engine grounded in data you expose, returns a conversation and actions written back to the records your reports read, and answers a stem that says research an inbound lead, draft a reply, and book a meeting.](</diagrams/day-01-sl-1a-mapping-business-requirements-and-kpis-to-agentfor/3-verb-split.png>)

Predictive features also carry data prerequisites the exam likes to probe. Einstein Lead Scoring needs a real volume of historical leads and conversions before it builds much beyond a default model, and scoring models refresh on a schedule rather than in real time. When a scenario says the org has almost no history, the honest answer may be that the predictive feature will not perform yet, which is a different response from naming a generative feature instead.

### Cadences, the mailbox, and captured activity

Sales Engagement, formerly High Velocity Sales, runs rep cadences. A cadence is a defined sequence of steps, such as a call, an email, a LinkedIn touch, or a custom step, that a rep works against a set of targets. The cadence surfaces the next due step in the rep's work queue and to do list, and it creates the tasks that drive the sequence. The target is the lead, contact, or person account being worked, and the assignee is the rep who works the step. A manager gets reports on cadence effectiveness and on the steps that convert best, which is exactly the visibility a scenario about inconsistent follow-up is asking for [^1]. Sales Engagement needs the relevant add-on license and work queue configuration before it does anything useful.

The distinction that trips people up is autonomy. A cadence follows fixed steps and a human works each one. It is not an autonomous sender, and it is not a forecasting tool. A requirement for autonomous outreach points at an Agentforce agent layered on top, not at a standard cadence.

Salesforce Inbox is the other half of the email story, and it works differently. It lives inside Outlook or Gmail and on mobile, adding email logging to Salesforce, open tracking, send later, and meeting scheduling links, so it works with the rep's existing mailbox rather than imposing a sequence. Where the requirement is that reps live in Gmail and need their mail logged and tracked, Inbox is the answer. Einstein Activity Capture is the third piece: it syncs email and events from a connected Microsoft or Google account and relates them to records, storing captured activity on the Activity Platform rather than as rows a rep logs by hand [^6]. That storage difference is why reports built on standard Task records can undercount captured activity and look thinner than the rep's actual day.

### Territories and forecasting model two different questions

Enterprise Territory Management and Opportunity Forecasting both answer questions about coverage and revenue, and the exam splits them cleanly. Territory is about ownership. You build a territory model, define territory types, place territories in a hierarchy, and write assignment rules that add or remove accounts and opportunities from territories, then assign users to territories so opportunity access can follow the account's territory. A model sits in planning until you activate it, which is what lets you test a carve before it goes live [^7]. Territory adds access on top of the existing sharing model rather than replacing the role hierarchy.

Forecasting is about the number. Collaborative Forecasts rolls amounts up a forecast hierarchy by forecast category, and the Opportunity's Forecast Category field maps from its stage, so the number moves as the deal advances. Forecast types let you roll up revenue, quantity, or product family side by side, and quotas attach to forecast managers per period. The forecast hierarchy is a separate roll-up you configure, and it can follow a different chain of managers than the role structure. A scenario about who owns an account is a territory question. A scenario about rolling commit and best case against quota is a forecasting question.

A requirement about improving forecast accuracy is therefore not answered by a dashboard or a territory rule. It is answered by mapping the forecast categories correctly, building the forecast hierarchy, and setting quotas, because forecasts enabled without a configured hierarchy and quota produce roll-ups nobody trusts.

### Experience Cloud is the outside-in answer

Some requirements come from outside the company, and those do not resolve to a report or an internal feature. When partners need to register deals and see only their own opportunities in a site that carries your brand, the answer is an Experience Cloud partner site with sharing rules, because an external login surface is the one thing a report subscription, a collaborative document, or a territory rule cannot provide. The same logic covers customer-facing sites and any requirement where an outside user must see a scoped slice of the data. Each of these is a licensing and sharing decision as much as a feature decision, so a scenario that names external partners is checking whether you reach for the external site and its sharing model rather than a manual export.

## Exam Traps

Three traps dominate this objective. The first is believing Agentforce and Einstein predictive scoring are interchangeable. They are not. Einstein scoring predicts and ranks from a trained model and returns a number, while Agentforce reasons and acts. A stem that asks you to predict which lead converts is an Einstein question, and offering an agent topic as the answer misses the verb the business used. The second trap is assuming Sales Engagement sends email autonomously. Cadence steps create tasks that a human works, so a requirement for autonomous outreach points at an Agentforce agent instead of a standard cadence. The third trap is treating Einstein Activity Capture as identical to hand-logged tasks. Because captured email and events are stored on the Activity Platform rather than as standard Task rows, reports and automation that expect Task records behave differently and can look empty, and the fix is the reporting model, not a rebuilt report.

## What a consultant should walk away with

Read the requirement for its metric and its verb before you touch a feature, then name the layer. Lift conversion and rank the queue: Einstein scoring plus agent-assisted qualification. Fix a broken sequence of touches: Sales Engagement cadences with a work queue. Log and track mailbox activity: Inbox and Einstein Activity Capture. Roll revenue against quota: Opportunity Forecasting with categories, hierarchy, and forecast types. Model who owns which account: Enterprise Territory Management. Reason over unstructured input and act: an Agentforce agent, grounded through a retrieval action or Data Cloud, with topics and actions narrow enough to test. Give external partners their own scoped view: an Experience Cloud site with sharing rules. When two answers both sound right, the one that matches the layer and the mechanism the metric names is the one that scores.
