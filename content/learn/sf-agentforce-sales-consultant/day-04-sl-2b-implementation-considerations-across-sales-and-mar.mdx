# Lesson 4: Implementation Considerations Across Sales and Marketing

A manager enables Collaborative Forecasts on a Friday and expects roll-ups on Monday. Monday arrives and every number is flat or blank. Nothing failed. Forecasts were turned on without a configured forecast hierarchy and without quotas, so there was no structure for the amounts to roll up into and no target to compare against. The feature was live and the outcome was useless, and the gap was never in the technology.

Every process you configure raises an implementation question, and this objective wants you to see those questions coming. Lead management raises a data integrity question at the entry point. Campaigns raise an attribution question underneath the report. Forecasting raises a hierarchy question. Territory raises a governance question. The exam rarely asks you to configure a feature in isolation; it hands you a process and expects you to name the consideration that decides whether the build survives contact with real data and real users.

This lesson serves requirement sl-2b in the Sales Lifecycle domain, at 20 percent of the exam. You will learn the implementation considerations behind the standard sales and marketing processes, and the sequencing that turns a configuration into a rollout that holds.

## Deep Dive

### Lead management starts at the entry point

Lead management begins at entry and routing, and the implementation consideration a scenario names is almost always data integrity at that point. Web-to-Lead and imported lists create Leads, matching and duplicate rules catch repeats before they become duplicate Accounts, and lead assignment rules route by region, product, or a marketing score. Get the entry controls right before you automate anything downstream, because a bad matching rule quietly corrupts your account data over time and no report will flag it for you.

At conversion, the field mappings decide what survives, and an unmatched Lead can merge into an existing Account [^1]. That merge is where the risk concentrates. If your matching rules are loose, two genuinely different companies fuse into one Account and both historical pipelines now point at the wrong record. The implementation sequence is rules and mapping first, automation second. A scenario that describes duplicate Accounts appearing shortly after a data load is pointing at matching, not at the import tool.

### Campaigns carry a setup burden

Campaigns look simple and carry more setup than people expect. Member statuses are a picklist you configure per campaign, and reports and influence depend on them staying consistent across campaigns [^3]. If every team invents its own statuses, your roll-up reports fragment and no one can compare programs. The standard practice is to build one set of statuses for each type of campaign you run and document it, because you cannot retroactively normalize months of mismatched statuses without a cleanup project.

Customizable Campaign Influence adds a second layer of prerequisites. It needs a model, a time window, and populated member and contact role data before revenue attribution is trustworthy, because influence models scan active campaigns for members who also hold a contact role on an open opportunity [^2]. Turn it on with empty data and it silently credits nothing, which is the worst outcome because the report looks correct and the numbers are wrong. At volume, CampaignMember records multiply quickly, so a large marketing org also plans for data storage and for the sync limits of Account Engagement or Marketing Cloud Connect. Name the consideration plainly: attribution is only as good as the member data underneath it.

### Forecasting has hard prerequisites

Forecasting is where the prerequisites bite hardest. You enable Collaborative Forecasts and pick forecast types, and you must configure a forecast hierarchy and quotas before the numbers mean anything, because the rollup follows the hierarchy rather than the role tree. Forecast types let you roll up by revenue, quantity, or product family side by side, and each type is a separate definition you activate before it appears on the forecasts page [^7]. A sales manager who wants product family rollups in addition to total revenue is asking for a second forecast type, not a second report.

The forecast category mapping is the other moving part. The Opportunity's Forecast Category maps from its stage, so a stage change or a category edit shifts the forecast, and editing forecast categories changes historical reporting, which is why orgs set them once and leave them alone [^4]. Sales reps forecast by category while managers roll up by role, so the implementation decision is who owns the number and whether that person sits on the right node of the forecast hierarchy. A scenario about a manager who cannot see the rollup is usually asking about that hierarchy rather than about the forecast feature itself.

Worked through: a VP asks why the western region's forecast is lower than the sum of the rep forecasts they can see. The reps are forecasting by category correctly, so the category mapping is fine. The gap is that the VP is enabled in the role hierarchy but sits on a different node in the forecast hierarchy, so the roll-up assembles from a narrower set of users than the role view implies. The fix is a forecast hierarchy change, not a report and not a data fix. That is the shape of a prerequisite problem: the feature is working exactly as configured, and the configuration is what is wrong.

### Territory management is the heaviest lift

Enterprise Territory Management is the heaviest lift of the sales features. You build a model, define territory types such as geographic and named account, place territories in a hierarchy, and write assignment rules that add or remove accounts and opportunities, all while the model sits in planning [^5]. Only when you activate it does assignment run, and once it is active, membership and rules need ongoing maintenance as accounts move. Territory-based sharing adds access for users assigned to a territory, but it does not replace the role hierarchy, so you reason about both together [^5].

The implementation consideration here is governance rather than configuration. Who owns the territory model? How often are accounts reassigned, and who runs the assignment rules when they are? How do the territory and forecast hierarchies relate to each other? Decide those before you activate, because a model that goes live without a maintenance owner drifts out of date within a quarter. A scenario that asks how opportunity access should follow territories is also asking whether the model is active and whether opportunity assignment rules exist, since neither accounts alone nor a planning model will produce that access.

### Data management runs underneath everything

Data management runs under all of it. Matching and duplicate rules keep Leads and Accounts clean, validation rules protect the key fields the process depends on, and Einstein Activity Capture fills the activity gap with a catch that the exam loves. Captured email and events are stored on the Activity Platform rather than as standard Task rows, so standard Task-based reports undercount them and can look empty [^6]. When a scenario complains that activity reports went quiet after Activity Capture was turned on, the answer is the external store, not a broken report or a deleted task.

Captured activity also requires user consent, which makes the capture policy a change management item rather than a checkbox. Users need to grant consent before their email syncs, and exclusions and sharing rules govern what is captured and who can see it, so plan the reporting model around captured activity before you turn it on org-wide [^6]. The sequencing to remember is: fix the data, decide the capture policy, communicate it, then enable it, and only then build the reports that depend on it.

Validation rules are the quieter half of this. The key fields the process leans on, such as Amount and Close Date on an opportunity or a status on a lead, are the ones a forecast and an agent both read, so protect them at the record level rather than trusting habit. A validation rule that stops an opportunity from moving forward without a populated forecast category stops bad data at the source. Cleaning data after a rollout is a project; preventing it at the entry point is a configuration you set once.

### Collaboration, visibility, and external access

Collaboration and visibility are the softer considerations the exam still tests. Quip puts a shared document on a record so a deal team works one version, Chatter keeps the conversation on the record, and Salesforce Meetings brings recordings and insights back to the opportunity. Each of these is a licensing decision as much as a feature decision, because the users who need it may need a license you have not provisioned.

Where partners or customers need access, an Experience Cloud site with sharing sets and the right licenses gives them a scoped view without exposing internal records. A scenario about external partners checks whether you reach for Experience Cloud and sharing sets rather than a report or a manual export, because neither of those provides an external login surface with its own access model. The implementation consideration is that external access is a sharing design you have to get right before you invite anyone in.

### Plan the rollout and name the sequencing

Finally, plan the rollout. Build in a sandbox, move changes with change sets or a deployment tool, put users on role-based permission sets, and train before you enable. Phase the release: clean the data, configure the process, pilot with a small group, measure a baseline KPI, then widen access. Each phase depends on the one before it, and skipping a phase is how an org ships a feature that nobody trusts.

The current release context matters too, because Agentforce features ship and change with each release, so confirm what is generally available in the org's release before you promise a feature in a plan. The strongest exam answers name the sequencing rather than the features: data, then configuration, then pilot, then rollout, measured against the KPI the business actually cares about. When a scenario asks how to land a large change, the answer is the order of operations, and a proposal to turn everything on at once is always the wrong one.

Write the sequence into the plan and the rollout becomes defensible. Data first, because everything downstream reads it. Configuration second, so the process exists before anyone is asked to use it. Pilot third, on users who can give you a real transcript and a real number. Rollout last, once the baseline has moved. When a stakeholder asks to compress the timeline, the honest answer names which phase would be cut and what breaks if it is, which is more persuasive than refusing outright.

## Exam Traps

Three traps dominate this objective. The first is assuming Enterprise Territory Management replaces the role hierarchy. ETM layers on top, so the shared role hierarchy still governs most record access and ETM adds territory-based access rather than removing anything, which means you reason about both at once. The second is believing captured email creates Tasks. Einstein Activity Capture stores email and events on the Activity Platform and relates them to records, so standard Task-based reports undercount activity and can look empty. The third is enabling forecasts and expecting accurate rollups without a forecast hierarchy and quotas. Collaborative Forecasts needs a configured hierarchy, and usually quotas, before the numbers are trustworthy, so a live feature with a flat roll-up is a configuration gap rather than a product limit.

## What a consultant should walk away with

Answer the process question and the implementation question together, because they are rarely separate on the exam. Lock down matching and mapping at lead entry before you automate routing, and treat conversion as the point where careless rules quietly corrupt Accounts. Give campaigns one documented set of member statuses and populate your contact role data before you promise an influence report. Configure the forecast hierarchy and quotas before you enable forecasts, and set forecast categories once. Activate a territory model only when you have a maintenance owner and opportunity assignment rules in place, and remember it adds access on top of roles. Plan the reporting model around captured activity, get user consent, and sequence every rollout as data, configuration, pilot, then access, measured against a KPI you recorded before you started.
