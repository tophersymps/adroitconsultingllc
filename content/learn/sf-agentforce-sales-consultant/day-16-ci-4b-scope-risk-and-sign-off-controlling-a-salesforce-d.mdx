# Lesson 16: Scope, Risk, and Sign-off: Controlling a Salesforce Delivery

It is month five of a fixed-price Salesforce build. The functional design was signed in month two, the first two releases went out clean, and the steering committee has started to relax. Then the operations director catches you after a demo and asks for one more approval step on the discount process, because the regional managers will need it before the fiscal year ends. It sounds small. It is one Flow, one page layout tweak, and a couple of hours of testing. You say yes, because saying yes is easier than saying no, and because nobody wants to be the consultant who blocks a simple request.

That yes is where deliveries die. Six weeks later the same operations director asks for a second approval step, then a third, and each one arrives as a small favour rather than a decision. The build absorbs them quietly, the test cycle stretches, and the contingency you were holding for a real problem is gone before the real problem shows up. When a data migration misfires in month seven there is no slack left, the go-live date slips, and the client's first memory of the engagement is the one where you missed the date you promised. Nothing in that story was dramatic. Every wrong turn was a small accommodation made to keep a room comfortable.

This lesson covers the exam guide's requirement ci-4b, in the Consulting and Implementation Strategies domain, which carries 25 percent of the exam. The objective asks you to use the project management lifecycle to deliver a successful solution on time and within scope.[^1] Scope, risk, and sign-off are not three topics; they are one discipline seen three times. You make the boundary explicit, you manage what could push against it, and you get someone with authority to sign the result. By the end of this lesson you will be able to write a statement of work that holds up in month nine, run a risk register that earns its keep before anything goes wrong, choose a delivery methodology you can defend, and drive a UAT to cutover to sign-off sequence that ends with a decision rather than a shrug.

## Deep Dive

### Scope is a contract before it is a plan

A statement of work is not a summary of good intentions. It is the instrument that decides who pays for the next argument, and it earns that status through five parts: what is in scope, what is explicitly out of scope, the assumptions the estimate rests on, the dependencies the client must supply, and the change control clause that governs everything after signature.[^2] Junior consultants rush the out-of-scope list because it feels hostile. Senior consultants argue hardest about it, because every unbilled hour in month five traces back to a boundary someone left fuzzy in month one. If the SOW says the build covers "sales reporting," then nothing stops a request for a marketing dashboard from looking like it was always in the deal. If the SOW says the build covers "the four pipeline reports enumerated in Appendix B, and excludes marketing analytics and external data ingestion," the same request becomes a change order.

The assumptions carry the estimate. When you price a build, you are pricing a set of beliefs: that the client's data is clean enough to migrate, that the named integration endpoints exist, that the client will supply a product owner for two hours a week. Write each belief down. If a belief turns out false, the assumption is what lets you reopen the estimate honestly instead of eating the loss. Dependencies work the same way in reverse. They are the things you need the client to provide: a named decision maker, sandbox access, a data extract, a subject matter expert available for testing. When a delivery stalls, it is usually a dependency the client owed and nobody tracked.

Before you plan anything, know the commercial shape you are working under. Fixed-price puts the risk of a bad estimate on you, so the scope line has to be tight and the change clause has to be strict. Time-and-materials puts the risk on the client, because they pay for the hours the work consumes, which is why you still owe them accurate estimates and honest status. Capped time-and-materials sits in the middle: the client is protected by a ceiling, and you are protected only if you watch the burn rate against that ceiling from week one. The three shapes shift who carries estimate risk, and a consultant who does not know which one the engagement signed is flying blind.

### Scope creep and scope gain are different problems

Creep and gain look similar in a hallway conversation and behave nothing alike in a budget. Scope creep is the accumulation of small unapproved additions that never pass through the process, and it kills margin quietly because no single item looks large enough to justify a difficult conversation. Scope gain is a genuine new requirement that the client would happily pay for if you framed it as a purchase. The distinction matters because gain is an opportunity: route it properly and it becomes revenue rather than overrun.

Both go through the same door. When a change arrives, you raise a change request with an impact assessment on cost, schedule, and quality, which together are the triple constraint, and you let the sponsor decide.[^4] The cost impact is the added effort and the added licence or consumption implications. The schedule impact is the weeks the change adds or the other work it displaces. The quality impact is the testing and hardening you would have to skip to hold the date, and naming it out loud is often what makes a client withdraw the request. Once the sponsor sees the trade in writing, they can choose to buy the change, defer it, or drop it. All three are acceptable outcomes. What is not acceptable is the fourth option, where you absorb the change on the quiet to keep everyone happy.

The absorbed change is the single behaviour that wrecks deliveries. It spends contingency that the next real problem needs, and it teaches the client that the process has no enforcement behind it. When the real problem lands, the same client who saw you absorb five small requests expects you to absorb the sixth, which is the one that breaks the date. Change control is not obstruction.[^5] It is the mechanism that keeps the delivery date honest, because every decision goes to the person who owns the consequence.

### The RAID log is where you earn the fee before anything goes wrong

The risk register is the artifact that separates a consultant from a contractor. It carries four kinds of entry, which is why practitioners call the combined document the RAID log: risks, which have not happened yet; assumptions, which the plan treats as true without proof; issues, which already have happened; and dependencies, the things you need from others.[^3] Nothing hides in an email thread when the log is current, and every entry carries an owner and a date, so no item can drift without someone's name against it.

Score each risk on probability times impact, and the register sorts itself. A risk that is likely and expensive rises to the top; a risk that is unlikely and cheap sinks. For every high score, name two responses. A mitigation is what you will actually execute to reduce the chance the risk lands, such as running a data-quality assessment early because a dirty migration is the risk you are trying to defuse. A contingency is what you will spend if the risk lands anyway, such as the reserve hours you will draw on to re-run the migration. The exam likes this distinction because it is the difference between a plan that prevents and a plan that responds. A register with twenty risks and no owners is decoration. A register with five risks, named owners, dates, and a mitigation and contingency for each is governance, and it is what lets you stand in front of a sponsor and show that you saw the problem coming.

### Methodology is a trade-off you have to defend

Methodology choice is not a matter of taste. It is a trade-off between schedule certainty and the freedom to learn, and the exam expects you to pick the shape that fits the situation. When a regulatory deadline is fixed and the scope is auditable, a predictive, waterfall-shaped plan gives the client the certainty they are buying, because the plan commits to dates and the work follows in defined stages. When the goal is a pilot whose real purpose is to reveal whether adoption is even possible, a Scrum shape with two-week sprints and a demo every sprint lets the backlog absorb what you learn. The Salesforce Adaptive Methodology used on Salesforce implementations blends the two: it uses a predictive shape to confirm the customer understands and approves the scope and design, and an agile shape to let development teams pull the most important work first.

The failure modes are symmetrical, and both are worth naming because the exam turns them into scenarios. A rigid waterfall build ships a perfect system nobody adopts, because no one saw it working until user acceptance testing, by which point the design decisions were locked. A pure agile build drifts past a date the client needed, because nobody fixed the milestone that the business plan depended on. Hybrid is usually right: fixed milestones and a frozen scope for the release, iterative sprints inside those milestones, and a backlog the client's product owner owns.

The failure modes worth naming against these shapes are exactly the traps you will see in scenarios. The demo trap is building the flashiest configuration to win the room and skipping adoption, training, and data quality until there is no time left. Compressed testing means the UAT scripts are written by the same people who built the feature, so the test proves the build works rather than that the business can use it. Cutover without a rollback plan turns a bad Friday into a lost weekend. Status reporting that never says red, because red is uncomfortable, leaves the sponsor unable to help and blindsided when the date slips. Every one of these has the same shape: a decision deferred past the point where it was cheap to make.

### Escalation is a lever, not a complaint

Escalation is the mechanism that moves a decision to the tier that has the authority to make it. A working escalation path has four parts: defined tiers, a named owner at each tier, a threshold that triggers the climb, and a decision latency the client has agreed to, so nobody escalates into a void. Tier one is the delivery lead and the client's product owner, where day-to-day trade-offs get resolved. Tier two is the engagement manager and the client's sponsor, where budget and date questions surface. Tier three is the steering committee, where scope, date, and budget trade-offs actually get decided.

Most escalations that go wrong were not escalated too late. They went to the wrong tier, or they arrived as blame rather than as a decision the recipient can make. When you escalate, bring the small committee, the two options, the recommendation, and the consequence of no decision, because that last part is what moves a room. "We can hold the date and cut the marketing dashboard, or keep the dashboard and move the date three weeks; I recommend the first; if we decide nothing, the date moves by default and the training plan has to be rebuilt." That is a decision brief. "The client keeps changing things and we cannot keep up" is a complaint, and complaints do not get decided.

### UAT, cutover, and sign-off

The last controlled sequence of a delivery runs on entry and exit criteria, and defining them in advance is what keeps the endgame rational. Entry to user acceptance testing requires a code freeze, a test data set that has been migrated, and scripts the business has reviewed rather than scripts the build team wrote for itself. UAT then runs, defects get triaged by severity in a daily stand-up, and the exit criterion is not zero defects. The real exit criterion is no open severity 1 defects plus a signed agreement on what is deferred, because an answer that promises a defect-free build is answering the wrong question.

Cutover runs a sequenced list: the data loads in order, the integrations switch on, and the activation checklist completes, all with a rollback plan and a dry run behind it. Then comes the moment the exam loves to test. Two weeks before go-live, the testers have forty defects open, twelve of them severity 1. The wrong moves are to go live anyway and fix everything in hypercare, or to extend testing indefinitely with no reporting. The right move is to run a defect triage with the business to agree which severity 1 and 2 items block go-live and which can be deferred with a date, then take a scope or date decision to the steering committee, which is the tier that owns that trade-off. Sign-off references the criteria written in the SOW, so the document that set the boundary in month one is the document that closes it at the end. Hypercare is a window with its own exit criteria, not an open-ended promise, and adoption is measured after it, against the business outcomes the project was funded to deliver.

## Exam Traps

Three traps dominate this objective. The first is believing that absorbing a change keeps the client happy. It does the opposite over the life of the engagement: quietly folding a change into the build spends contingency you will need for a real risk, and it trains the client to expect concessions. The correction is that every change, whether it looks like creep or genuine gain, goes through a change request with an impact assessment on cost, schedule, and quality, so the sponsor decides against the triple constraint. Refusing outright is also wrong, because it removes the client's ability to buy the change; the door is what matters, not a locked gate.

The second trap is believing a mitigation and a contingency are the same thing. They are the two halves of a risk response. A mitigation reduces the probability that a risk lands, while a contingency is the money or time you spend if it does. Mixing them up leaves you with a plan that cannot respond: you might have identified the risk and even taken a preventive step, but with no reserve set aside you have nothing to spend when prevention fails. When the exam hands you a high-probability, high-impact risk, the strong answer names both.

The third trap is believing zero defects is the exit criterion for user acceptance testing. A defect-free build is not the standard and promising one is a sign you have not run a real UAT, because testing always finds defects. The real exit criterion is no open severity 1 defects plus a signed agreement on what is deferred. A scenario that offers you a defect-free promise, or a go-live that ignores the open severity 1 list, is testing whether you know that triage and an explicit deferral decision are the professional answer.

## What a consultant should walk away with

Hold the three disciplines as one habit. Make the boundary explicit in the SOW, with an out-of-scope list and a change clause you are willing to invoke, and route every change through a change request rather than absorbing it. Keep a RAID log with owners, dates, and both a mitigation and a contingency for each high-scoring risk, and read it in every status meeting. Pick the methodology you can defend for the situation, hybrid more often than not, and escalate early to the tier that owns the decision, bringing options and a recommendation rather than a complaint. Then close on criteria: define UAT entry and exit up front, keep a rollback plan behind cutover, and sign off against the SOW. Do those things and the delivery date stops being a hope and becomes a decision.
