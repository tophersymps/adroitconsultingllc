# Lesson 41: Trusted AI Principles in Practice: The Nine Principles and the Einstein Trust Layer That Implements Them

A prospect's security team joins a call with one question and no interest in the demo. Where does their data go when a rep hits the generate button, and who else can see it. You have two answers available. The weak one is to say that Salesforce takes AI ethics seriously. The strong one is to name the mechanism: dynamic grounding pulls the record data into the prompt inside Salesforce, data masking replaces sensitive values with placeholder tokens before the prompt leaves the org, zero data retention with the model provider means the prompt and the response are not stored or reused, and the audit trail records what happened. The second answer closes the procurement conversation in ten minutes. The first one loses the deal.

That gap between principle and mechanism is the whole subject of this lesson. A set of ethical principles with nothing behind it is a poster. What makes it real is that each principle maps to something a consultant can configure, point at, and show to an auditor. The exam tests that mapping relentlessly, because it is the difference between someone who can recite the principles and someone who can apply them.

This lesson serves requirement pg-3b, "Identify ethical challenges of AI and apply Salesforce's Trusted AI Principles to sales scenarios," in the Predictive and Generative AI domain, which carries 13 percent of the exam. Lesson 40 covered the challenges; this lesson covers the principles and the product layer that implements them, and it closes the AI domain and the course. By the end you can name the nine principles and give each one a sales consequence, pair each principle with the Trust Layer control that serves it, answer a scenario by choosing the control that matches the principle at stake, and describe the governance operating model that keeps a live model honest after go live.

<TLDR>

- Each Trusted AI Principle maps to an Einstein Trust Layer control you can configure and show an auditor
- Zero data retention governs storage and reuse, not whether the prompt was sent to the model
- An operating model with owners, monitoring and a change gate keeps principles true after go live

</TLDR>


## Deep Dive

### The nine principles, and how Salesforce groups them

The nine duties this course works from are Accuracy, Honesty, Empowerment, Inclusivity, Accountability, Transparency, Security, Reliability and Privacy. Treat that nine as the course's working composite: it draws together the duties Salesforce publishes across two separate groupings rather than repeating any single published list. Each one is a duty, and each one has a sales consequence rather than an abstract one. Accuracy means the output is grounded in reliable data and the org works to keep it that way. Honesty means the AI does not mislead about what it is or what it knows. Empowerment means the technology augments the person rather than displacing their judgment. Inclusivity means the system works fairly across the people it serves and does not exclude by design. Accountability means a human or an organization stays answerable for the system's effects. Transparency means people can understand when AI is involved and how a decision was reached. Security means the data and the model are protected. Reliability means the system performs consistently and is monitored. Privacy means personal data is handled lawfully and minimally.

Worth knowing for the exam: Salesforce publishes these duties under more than one grouping, and recognizing the overlap is a sign you understand them rather than memorized a list. Salesforce's Trusted AI page groups its commitments as Responsible, Accountable, Transparent, Empowering and Inclusive,[^1] and its generative AI guidelines are summarized as a separate five, Accuracy, Safety, Honesty, Empowerment and Sustainability.[^2] The names shift, the substance does not: empowerment is the one duty both lists name, and neither grouping sets out every duty on its own, which is why this course works from the composite above. If a question uses a word you did not expect, map it to the duty rather than freezing on the label.

### Each principle lands somewhere concrete in a sales deployment

Accuracy is grounding the prompt and governing the data the model reads, so the answer reflects the record rather than a plausible invention. Honesty is telling the user when text was machine generated instead of letting it pass as the rep's own reasoning. Empowerment is keeping the score advisory and the rep able to override it, so the human keeps the judgment the organization hired them for. Inclusivity is reviewing scoring outcomes across segments so a group is not systematically excluded from contact. Accountability is naming an owner for every model and every automated action. Transparency is the factors panel for the rep and disclosure for the customer. Security is the Trust Layer's masking and the access model around it. Reliability is monitoring a live model for drift so a fairness problem months after a clean launch is caught rather than discovered. Privacy is consent, minimization and retention limits.

Read that list as a design checklist rather than a values statement. Every line has a setting, a report or a documented process behind it, which is exactly why the principles are answerable in a sales scenario. When a sales leader pushes for an agent that closes deals autonomously, the answer comes from empowerment and accountability at once: you can automate a reversible, low-consequence action and keep a human on the consequential one, and you name who owns the outcome either way.[^2]

### The Einstein Trust Layer is the mechanism behind the principles

The Einstein Trust Layer is how Salesforce implements much of this in the product, and the exam likes the mapping.[^3] Dynamic grounding pulls real record data into the prompt, which serves accuracy.[^4] Data masking replaces sensitive values with masked tokens before the prompt leaves Salesforce, which serves security and privacy. Zero data retention with the model provider means prompts and completions are not stored or reused for training, which is the answer to the most common security question. Toxicity scoring evaluates the generated response before it reaches the user. Prompt injection defenses try to stop content in retrieved data from hijacking the instruction. The audit trail records the prompt, the response and the data retrieved, which is the accountability evidence you hand an auditor. Access is governed by permission sets and a running user, so the agent sees only what that user can see.

Three details keep a strong answer from becoming a wrong one. First, zero data retention means the prompt is still sent so the model can answer; what it governs is whether the prompt and the response are stored or reused afterward, so an answer that says the data never leaves Salesforce is wrong even though the principle is privacy. Second, masking is configurable rather than universal. Trailhead's data masking material notes that masking is available for embedded generative AI features such as Einstein Service Replies and Work Summaries and is configured in Einstein Trust Layer setup, and that data masking for LLMs is currently disabled for agents, so a general claim that masking is always on everywhere overstates the product.[^5] Masking tokens are restored, or demasked, in the response, and Salesforce logs masking activity as part of the audit trail. Third, the Trust Layer applies whether the model is Salesforce hosted or one the customer connected through Model Builder, because the controls sit around the call rather than inside the model.

### Applying the principles means choosing the right control

Applying the principles to a scenario means choosing the control that matches the principle at stake, not reciting the list. A customer asks whether reps will be replaced. The principle at stake is empowerment, and the design answer is a human in the loop with an override rather than a promise about intent. A regulator asks how the company knows a score is not discriminatory. The principles are inclusivity and accountability, and the design answer is outcome monitoring by segment with a named owner accountable for the finding. A prospect asks whether their conversations are recorded. The principles are transparency and privacy, and the design answer is disclosure plus a lawful consent process confirmed before the feature is enabled. A security team asks where the data goes. The principles are security and privacy, and the design answer is masking, zero data retention and regional control through the model you connect.

Practice reading a scenario as a principle question. The word "discriminatory" or "excluded" points at inclusivity. The word "explain" or "understand why" points at transparency. The word "stored" or "reused for training" points at security and privacy. The word "replaced" or "override" points at empowerment. The word "who is responsible" points at accountability. And the word "drift" or "still working months later" points at reliability. Naming the principle first narrows the correct control to one or two options, which is faster than evaluating every answer on its own merits.

### Principles need an operating model, and principles need a change gate

Principles without an operating model are a poster. The practical layer is a governance cadence: an owner for each AI feature, a documented purpose and permitted data scope for each, a regular review of model performance and outcome distribution, a defined human review requirement by risk level, and a record of the decisions so the next auditor or regulator receives evidence rather than recollection.

Add a change gate as well. When someone edits a prompt template, changes the fields a model reads, or connects a new model, that change goes through the same review as a code change, because it changes what the system will say to a customer. This is the control that addresses reliability over time. Models drift, data changes, and a scoring model that launched clean can develop a fairness problem months later, so an answer that ends at go live is incomplete. In Setup you review the AI features enabled in the org and record an owner and a stated purpose for each, which is the accountability half of the principles. You open the Trust Layer settings and confirm data masking, zero data retention, toxicity scoring and the audit trail are configured, and note where the audit trail is viewed. You open Model Builder and confirm which models are connected and who can select them, so it is documented which provider answers a given prompt. You build a report or dashboard comparing conversion or win outcomes by segment across the scored populations, so inclusivity can be monitored rather than assumed. You document the human review gate and the override path for scores and generated customer-facing content and assign an owner to each. Then you add the change gate to the release checklist so edits to prompt templates, model field sets and connected models pass the same review as a deployment.

### The AI domain is one idea with two arms

This closes the AI domain, and the domain is one idea with two arms. Predictive AI learns from your closed history and produces scores, predictions and recommendations that rank and guide, and it is limited by record volume, field discipline and the activity data you feed it. Generative AI composes new language, and it is limited by grounding and governed by the Trust Layer. They share one substrate, which is your data, and they carry one set of duties, which are the Trusted AI Principles. A consultant who can say which arm a requirement needs, what data it consumes, what its failure modes are, and which principle governs it is answering the way the exam asks.

The exam tip is worth keeping as a reflex: every AI question on this exam resolves to two questions. What data does this feature read, and which principle governs what it does with the answer. A predictive feature reads closed history and activity; a generative feature reads grounded records and prompts. Then the Trust Layer, the factors panel, the consent process and the review gate are the answers to the second question. That two-part habit turns a long list of features into a short set of decisions.

### The three moves that repeat across all five domains

Across all five domains of this course the same three moves repeat. Translate the requirement into a specific Salesforce construct rather than a category. Name the prerequisite the construct needs, whether that is a license, a data volume, a sharing rule or a governance process. Then name the failure mode the customer hits when the prerequisite is missing. You mapped requirements to capabilities in the Sales Lifecycle domain, planned the delivery in Consulting and Implementation Strategies, went deep on the product in Practical Application of Agentforce Sales Expertise, governed the record and the field in Data Management, and finished here with the models that read all of it. Nothing in the AI domain stands apart from the CRM underneath it, and nothing in this course stands alone.

Read that back over an AI scenario and it writes the answer for you. A predictive feature needs a license and a volume of closed history, and it fails on a renamed stage or a disabled sync. A generative feature needs grounding and the Trust Layer, and it fails on stale data or an absent review gate. A recommendation needs a strategy layer, and it fails when nobody encoded the business rule. Every one of those sentences is a construct, a prerequisite and a failure mode, and that is the shape the exam rewards.

## Exam Traps

<Callout kind="warning">

Three traps dominate this objective. The first is believing the Trusted AI Principles are marketing language with no product behind them. Each one maps to a mechanism, and the exam pairs the principle with the Einstein Trust Layer control that implements it, so an answer that treats the principles as aspirational will miss the control that is the real answer.

The second trap is believing zero data retention means the data is never sent to the model. The prompt is sent so the model can answer; what zero retention governs is whether it is stored or reused afterward. An option that says nothing leaves Salesforce is wrong even though zero data retention is the right mechanism to cite.

The third trap is believing governance ends at go live. Models drift and data changes, so an unmonitored scoring model can develop a fairness problem months after a clean launch, and an answer that omits monitoring is incomplete. Inclusivity and reliability are ongoing duties, and the control is a review cadence with a named owner rather than a launch checklist.

</Callout>


## What a consultant should walk away with

<KeyTakeaways>

- Accuracy is grounding, honesty is disclosure, empowerment is a human override, and accountability is a named owner per model
- Inclusivity is outcome monitoring by segment, transparency is the factors panel plus disclosure, security is masking, and privacy is consent and retention limits
- Answer a scenario by naming the principle at stake first, then choosing the control, which narrows the options faster
- Put an operating model behind the principles: an owner and purpose per feature, a review cadence, a human review gate and a change gate

</KeyTakeaways>


Carry the principles as a mapping rather than a recitation. Accuracy is grounding, honesty is disclosure and content moderation, empowerment is a human in the loop with an override, inclusivity is outcome monitoring by segment, accountability is a named owner per model, transparency is the factors panel plus customer disclosure, security is masking and access control, reliability is drift monitoring, and privacy is consent, minimization and retention limits. Answer a scenario by naming the principle at stake first, then choosing the control, because that narrows the options faster than reading each one cold. Be precise about the Trust Layer: zero data retention governs storage and reuse, not whether the prompt was sent, and masking is configurable and not universally on. Then put an operating model behind all of it, an owner and a purpose for every AI feature, a review cadence on outcomes, a human review gate by risk level, and a change gate on prompt edits, model field sets and connected models. That is what turns the principles from a poster into something you can hand an auditor.
