# Lesson 17: Discovery Workshops: Stakeholder Mapping and Requirements Capture

Three weeks into a Salesforce engagement, a consulting team holds a discovery workshop with the head of sales operations. The room is friendly, the notes are thorough, and the head of ops says the thing that ends up in the design document: "The new system just has to work exactly like the old one." The consultants write that down as a requirement, build a pipeline that mirrors the legacy tool stage for stage, and ship it. At user acceptance testing, the account executives complain that the new pipeline still forces them through two approval gates that only existed because the old system could not handle conditional routing. The line everyone agreed to in week three was never a requirement. It was a description of a workaround, and the team built the workaround into a platform that did not need it.

That failure is cheap to avoid and expensive to miss, and it is why discovery is not a meeting. It is a bounded phase with a defined output, and most failed Salesforce projects made their fatal decisions in the first three weeks, before anyone configured a single field, while nobody noticed because the document that recorded those decisions was a set of slide bullets. A disciplined discovery runs two to six weeks, ends with a solution definition and a prioritized use-case inventory, and has an estimate attached to that backlog. The length is a scope decision you make with the sponsor, not a courtesy you extend until the room runs out of energy.

This lesson serves the exam guide's requirement ci-5a, in the Consulting and Implementation Strategies domain, which carries 25 percent of the exam. The objective asks you to conduct discovery workshops, analyze business requirements, define solutions, and prioritize use cases. Discovery earns its keep the moment you can point at one requirement, the design element that satisfies it, and the test script that proves it, all carrying the same ID. By the end of this lesson you will be able to map the stakeholders who can decide, block, or derail the work, run a workshop that produces information rather than a performance, pick the elicitation technique that gets closest to how work actually happens, and write a requirement a sponsor can sign.

<TLDR>

- Discovery is a bounded phase with a decision-grade output, not a meeting that drifts until invitations stop
- Map stakeholders on a power and interest grid, then match each elicitation technique to the fidelity the question needs
- Capture outcomes rather than solutions, and write requirements a sponsor can sign with acceptance criteria

</TLDR>


## Deep Dive

### Discovery is a bounded phase, not a meeting

Discovery has an output, and the output is decision grade. That means a sponsor could sign a statement of work against it without needing another round of questions. The artifact is a solution definition, and it carries a prioritized inventory of use cases with an estimate attached to each. If discovery ends with a pile of notes and a promise to consolidate them later, the phase did not finish, because notes are not something a person can sign. Treat the phase as a piece of work with an owner, a start, and an end, and put a document at the end that states what was decided.

The length of the phase is a negotiation, not a default. Two to six weeks is the normal band, and where you land inside it depends on how much is already known and how willing the sponsor is to spend calendar time now to save rework later. A tight discovery on a small, well-understood org might be two weeks. A complex, multi-cloud discovery with integrations and a data migration might run six. What matters is that the phase is bounded: it starts on a date, it ends on a date, and it produces a signed document, rather than drifting until the workshop invitations stop getting accepted.

### Stakeholder mapping is the first artifact

Stakeholder mapping comes first because it tells you who can decide, who can block, and who has to be carried along. The workhorse is the power and interest grid.[^3] High power and high interest are your partners, the people who own the outcome and should be in every working session. High power and low interest are the ones you must keep satisfied with concise updates, because they can overrule the project from a distance and will do so on the strength of a single bad impression. Low power and high interest are often the people who will tell you the truth about how work really happens, so they are worth listening to even though they cannot approve anything. Low power and low interest get monitored and left alone.

Power is not just seniority. It is the ability to say no and have it stick, whether that comes from budget authority, from controlling a dependency, or from the ear of the sponsor. Interest is how much the person cares about this specific change. A stakeholder map that lists names without a stance beside each one is a phone directory, not a map. Add a column for attitude, positive, neutral, or negative, and a column for the engagement plan you will run against each person.[^2] An unengaged high-power stakeholder is a risk to the project who needs a targeted engagement plan, because the person who is not in the room when a design is agreed is the person most likely to reject it later. Layer a RACI across the decisions you will need, naming who is accountable, who is consulted, and who is informed, so that when two functions disagree it is already written down who breaks the tie.

### Workshop craft decides whether you leave with information or a performance

A workshop is a skill, and the mechanics decide the outcome. Set a single objective per workshop and write it on the wall, because a room with two objectives achieves neither. Send a pre-read and a question set at least three days ahead so people arrive with their thinking done, which is the difference between a discussion and an improvisation. Park the topic of who is to blame the moment it surfaces and write it on the parking lot list; blame is a conversation about the past, and you are there to design the future.

Keep one person talking at a time, and when two functions contradict each other, do not try to resolve it in the room. A workshop does not hold the authority to settle a disagreement between departments, so attempting it produces an answer nobody is bound by. Record both statements, name the conflict in the readout, and take it to the accountable owner, who is the only person who can actually decide. Have a named scribe who is not you, because the person running the room cannot also be the person capturing it, and a facilitator who is typing is not watching the room. Close every workshop by reading back what you heard and what you will do next, so misunderstandings surface while they are still cheap to fix.

### Elicitation technique separates reality from the official story

Every elicitation technique trades fidelity against reach, and knowing which one to reach for is what separates a consultant from a note-taker.[^1] Interviews with the accountable owner give you intent, the goals and constraints the owner carries. Workshops give you shared understanding and the arguments you need to surface early, while everyone is still in the same room. Shadowing an end user through a full transaction is the highest-fidelity technique you have, because people do not report their own workarounds; they have stopped noticing them. A survey is cheap and reaches everyone, but it tells you only what people believe about themselves. Card sorting helps when no one agrees on a taxonomy. Process mapping, drawn live on paper with the people who do the work, exposes the handoffs and the exceptions that break an integration.

The rule of thumb is that the more physical and specific the technique, the closer to truth it sits, so pair every workshop with at least one observation session. A survey that says the team spends two hours a week on data entry is a claim; watching them re-key the same account three times in an afternoon is evidence. When you can only afford one high-fidelity technique, spend it on the process that carries the most risk or the most volume, and let surveys fill in the rest.

### Capturing a requirement is a discipline with a name

A requirement describes an outcome the business needs. A solution describes how you would deliver it. Mixing the two is the most common defect in a requirements document, and it is the trap that opens this lesson. When a department head says the new system must work exactly like the old one, you have not captured a requirement; you have captured a solution preference that hides the real requirement. The correct move is to keep asking why until you reach the underlying outcome the person is protecting. Why does the old approval gate exist? Because discounts over fifteen percent needed a manager's sign-off and the old tool could not express that as a rule. Now you have a real requirement: discounts over a threshold route to a manager for approval. The build can satisfy that with a condition rather than a manual gate.

Write requirements as user stories, with a role, a capability, and a benefit: as a sales manager, I can approve a discount over fifteen percent so that margin is protected without slowing every deal.[^4] Attach acceptance criteria in given, when, then form,[^6] because the criteria are what turn a story into something testable. Separate functional requirements from non-functional ones. Functional requirements describe what the system does. Non-functional requirements describe how fast, how many records, what happens at a concurrency peak, and what audit trail is required. Non-functional requirements are where the platform limits bite, and they are the ones clients forget to state, so you have to ask for them deliberately.

### Documentation has a shape that survives the client

The documents from discovery have a structure, and following it is what makes them reusable in month six. A requirements document carries a unique ID per requirement, the source stakeholder who asked for it, the priority, and the acceptance criteria. A solution design document maps each requirement to the configuration or component that satisfies it, so the design is traceable to a need rather than to a preference. A traceability matrix is the join between the two: every requirement traces forward to a design element and a test script, and a requirement with no test is either untested or unnecessary.[^5] That matrix is what lets you answer a scope question with evidence instead of memory.

Keep a glossary, because two departments using the same word for different things will cost you more than a missing field ever will. "Account" might mean a customer to sales and a billing entity to finance, and if the requirements document never resolves the difference, the build resolves it by accident. Version the documents and record who approved each version. The document you did not version is the document you will re-argue in month six, when someone remembers a different draft and nobody can prove which one was signed.

### Requirements anti-patterns cluster

Four anti-patterns show up in almost every weak requirements document, and a quick sweep for all four before you present the readout catches most of the damage. The first is the solution disguised as a requirement, the "work exactly like the old one" line, which you fix by asking why until the outcome appears. The second is the unbounded request, a phrase like "we need a report for management" that has no columns, no filters, and no named consumer, and which will expand until someone bounds it. Ask who reads it, what decisions it drives, and which fields it must show, and the report snaps into a scope.

The third is the missing non-functional requirement, the story that names only the happy path and says nothing about volume, speed, or audit. The fourth, and the most dangerous, is the requirement with no named source, which means no one can confirm it and no one owns its acceptance test. A requirement with no source is the one that gets invented into scope and then blamed on you when the client's own users dispute it at user acceptance testing. Before the readout, walk the list and confirm every requirement has a source, a priority, and acceptance criteria. Anything missing one of the three is not ready to present.

## Exam Traps

<Callout kind="warning">

Three traps dominate this objective. The first is believing that a statement of how the old system worked is a requirement. It is a solution preference that hides the real requirement, so the strong response is to keep asking why until you reach the business outcome the user is protecting. Treating the old workaround as a functional requirement locks a legacy process into a modern platform, which is exactly the failure that opens this lesson.

The second trap is believing a workshop is where you resolve disagreement between functions. It is not, because a workshop cannot grant authority it does not hold. You record both statements, name the conflict in the readout, and take it to the accountable owner. An answer that has you arbitrating the dispute on the spot is wrong, because whatever you decide in the room will not be binding and will be reopened the moment the losing department talks to the sponsor.

The third trap is believing a survey can stand in for observation of the work. Surveys report what people believe about their own process, which is the official story, while shadowing an end user through a full transaction shows you the workarounds they do not notice well enough to report. When a question asks which technique gives the most reliable view of how work actually happens, the answer is the physical, specific one: watching the transaction, not asking about it and not reading the existing process documentation.

</Callout>


## What a consultant should walk away with

<KeyTakeaways>

- Run discovery as a bounded phase with a signed output, and keep a traceability matrix tying each requirement to a design element and a test
- Map stakeholders on a power and interest grid with a stance and an engagement plan, and treat the unengaged high-power stakeholder as a named risk
- In workshops hold a single objective, a pre-read, a scribe who is not you, and a parking lot, and pair each with an observation session
- Match the elicitation technique to the fidelity needed, capture outcomes not solutions, and write stories with acceptance criteria, an ID, a source, and a priority

</KeyTakeaways>
