# Lesson 38: Predictive AI in Sales Cloud: Opportunity Scoring, Lead Scoring, Forecasting, and Activity Capture

A sales ops lead asks you to turn on opportunity scoring before the end of the month. You enable it, wait, and nothing appears. Two days later the scores arrive, but the deals everyone agrees are safest are sitting in the sixties and a long shot is ranked first. You dig in and find the stage picklist was renamed last year without anyone remapping the values to their stage types, so the win rate the model learned from is quietly wrong. The feature was never broken. It was fed a story about the business that the business does not recognize.

That is the character of this domain. Predictive features in Sales Cloud are models trained on your own closed history, and they inherit whatever discipline your CRM has. Turn them on in an org with clean stage history and a year of synced activity and they earn their keep. Turn them on in an org with renamed stages, duplicate contacts and no calendar sync and you get a number that looks authoritative and quietly misleads. The consultant's job is to check the inputs before promising the output.

This lesson serves requirement pg-2a, "Understand what predictive AI tools are available in Agentforce Sales," in the Predictive and Generative AI domain, which carries 13 percent of the exam. The exam tests these features by their thresholds, their cadence and their licensing, so the details in this lesson are the deliverable rather than color commentary. By the end you can state the exact data requirements for Einstein Opportunity Scoring and Einstein Lead Scoring, explain why an org falls back to a global model, describe how often scores refresh, and explain what Einstein Activity Capture contributes to the models that read it.

## Deep Dive

### What a predictive feature is, and the score versus the factors

A predictive feature in Sales Cloud is a model trained on your own closed history that outputs a number a rep can act on. Einstein Opportunity Scoring gives each opportunity a score from 1 to 99 representing the likelihood it will be won, and it also shows the factors that pushed the score up and down. Einstein Lead Scoring does the same for leads against conversion. The distinction the exam tests is between the score, which is a ranking, and the factors, which are the model's explanation of itself.

Keep those two ideas separate because they answer different questions. The score orders one record against another, so a score of 82 is meaningful relative to the other open deals, not as an absolute probability. The factors are the model's account of why, and they are the part a rep can actually argue with. A rep who acts on the score without reading the factors is guessing with extra steps, and a manager who cannot see the factors cannot coach the rep on why a deal is slipping. The exam tip is worth internalizing: the score is the model's ranking and the factors are its explanation.

Scores appear where reps work. They show on the record, in list views and in reports, and opportunity scores also surface on the forecasts page. Both the Opportunity Score and the Lead Score field can be read by Flow, which is how you turn a score into an action such as assigning a task or routing a deal for review.

### Einstein Opportunity Scoring and the thresholds that decide everything

Einstein Opportunity Scoring has published data requirements, and they are worth memorizing exactly.[^2] You need at least 200 closed won opportunities in the last 24 months and at least 200 closed lost opportunities in the last 24 months, and each closed opportunity must have a lifespan of at least two days. Opportunity history must show an average of at least one update per closed opportunity, and for each opportunity the change history needs at least two records with open stages rather than only the final closed stage. Opportunities whose history jumps straight to a closed stage both fail to score themselves and degrade the model for everyone else.

You must use the standard opportunity Stage field, because win rates are calculated from stage and the mapping of each picklist value to the Open, Closed Won or Closed Lost stage type is what the model reads. This is where the opening scene of this lesson lives. If someone renames the stages and forgets to remap the types, the model still runs and the win rate it computes is wrong. A related caution from Salesforce's setup guidance: if your win rate is extremely high or low, scores skew.[^1] An org winning above 90 percent of its deals can produce a large block of opportunities scored above 90, and the fix is to make sure deals are set to the correct closed stage rather than to distrust the model.

If your data falls short of the thresholds, Einstein does not simply refuse to work. It falls back to a global model trained on anonymous data from many Salesforce customers, and it moves to a model built on your data once you have enough of it and that model performs better. So an org with 240 closed won and 180 closed lost opportunities does not get a local model, because the closed lost requirement is not met, and it gets scores from the global model in the meantime. The correct answer to that scenario is "no local model yet, global fallback," not "no scores at all" and not "a local model on partial data."

### How fresh the number is

Cadence matters because questions ask how current the score is, and candidates answer from intuition rather than from the documented behavior. After you enable Einstein Opportunity Scoring it can take up to 48 hours before the first scores appear while the data is analyzed and the model is built. After that, opportunity scores are updated every few hours, while the underlying model is reanalyzed roughly monthly or whenever an admin changes the settings. A previously scored opportunity that is more than three months past its close date receives a default score of 5, which keeps dead records from ranking high in a list. If the analysis is still running after 48 hours, Salesforce's guidance is to turn the feature off and on again or edit the settings, and the setup page shows the analysis status while it runs.

![Freshness ladder for the three predictive clocks. After enabling Opportunity Scoring the first scores can take up to 48 hours, open-deal scores then refresh every few hours, and the model is reanalyzed roughly monthly. A deal more than three months past its close date receives a default score of 5. Lead scores refresh multiple times each day and a lead inactive for 200 days is not scored, while Activity Capture can take up to 24 hours to appear in reports.](</diagrams/day-38-pg-2a-predictive-ai-in-sales-cloud-opportunity-scoring-l/2-freshness-ladder.png>)

Read that set of numbers as a defense against over-promising. Nothing here updates in real time. A deal a rep updated five minutes ago is scored against a model that may be weeks old and a score refresh that happens a few times a day. When a scenario offers "scores are static once assigned" or "scores update once every 30 days," both are wrong for different reasons: they refresh every few hours, and the model behind them is reanalyzed on a roughly monthly cadence.

### Einstein Lead Scoring uses a different threshold set

Einstein Lead Scoring is a separate feature with a separate set of requirements, and the exam uses the mismatch as a trap. At least 1,000 leads must have been created in the last 200 days, and of those, at least 120 must have converted to an account and contact. There is an optional third threshold for orgs that want the model to look at leads that convert with an opportunity: of the leads created in the last 6 months, at least 120 converted to an account and contact with an opportunity created at conversion time. The model itself is built from patterns in your closed leads over the past three years, and it considers standard and custom lead fields together unless you configure a custom field set.


Two behavioral details matter. First, lead scores refresh multiple times each day. Leads with no new or updated activity for 200 days are not scored, while brand new leads with no activity at all are still scored, so "an inactive lead has no score" is true only after the 200-day mark.[^3] Second, scores cannot be exported. They are available only on lead records, list views and reports, though the Lead Score field can be used by Flow. A scenario that proposes building a data extract of the Lead Score field is wrong, and it is wrong for this reason rather than for a permission reason.

Licensing splits the two scorers. Einstein Lead Scoring requires a Sales Cloud Einstein license, sold at extra cost in Enterprise and included in Performance and Unlimited editions.[^4] Einstein Opportunity Scoring is available to users with a Sales Cloud Einstein license and also to eligible users without one, which is why a question can legitimately place opportunity scores in an org that never bought Einstein. Neither feature changes your data storage usage, because the models run on Salesforce infrastructure outside your org, and neither asks you to stand up a warehouse of your own.

### Segments, the global model trade-off

Lead scoring lets you split leads into segments so different departments get their own model, and this is a design decision with a real cost. You define a segment with up to 35 field criteria, and the data requirements apply to each segment you create, including the All Leads default segment. The catch is the global model. When you score all leads together and lack conversion data, Einstein uses the anonymized global model. The moment you create segments the global model becomes unavailable, and a segment without enough data to train produces unscored leads.

So segmentation buys precision and can cost you scores entirely in a thin segment. Picture a company that splits leads into six regional segments, four of which have plenty of conversion history and two of which are new and quiet. The four busy segments get sharper models than a single global one; the two quiet ones get nothing, where under a single combined model they would at least have received global-model scores. Before you segment, check that every segment you plan can independently clear the 1,000-lead and 120-conversion bar. You can also opt out of contributing your data to the global model, which is a governance choice worth raising with a privacy-conscious customer rather than silently accepting.

### Einstein Forecasting and Einstein Activity Capture

Einstein Forecasting projects which opportunities are likely to close in the period and adjusts the forecast accordingly. The important design point is that it works on top of forecast categories rather than replacing them, so the Forecast Category on the opportunity still governs which number the deal contributes. The model refines how the pipeline rolls up; it does not take over the field the reps and finance rely on.

Einstein Activity Capture is the signal source underneath the predictive features. It syncs email and calendar events between the rep's mail and calendar and Salesforce, associates them with the right records through matching rules, and supplies the engagement history the models read as evidence.[^5] It has its own user, profile and connected account settings, and configurations decide what is captured and how it flows. By default events and contacts sync bi-directionally between Salesforce and the connected account, and exclusions can be set org-wide to filter specific addresses, domains and keywords, though adding an exclusion does not remove data already captured. Two practical limits are worth knowing: it can take up to 24 hours after the initial setup before captured data appears in standard reports, and only emails and events tied to the user's primary Salesforce email address sync, so a rep who works from an alias gets a partial timeline.[^6]

Because Activity Capture writes activity records on its own schedule, sharing and field access for the synced objects belong in the design rather than in cleanup. Activity sharing rules control how users see captured event data, and if email is synced as a Salesforce activity those rules govern email visibility too. There is also a floor on the data: the org needs at least 30 accounts, contacts, leads or opportunities for Activity Capture to run. And a mismatch here is not a cosmetic problem. If a synced email attaches to the wrong record, the model reads engagement that did not happen on that deal, so duplicate records that split a timeline and contacts stored with inconsistent email addresses actively mislead the scorer rather than merely annoying a rep.

### The build sequence and what to check before you switch it on

In Setup you search for Einstein, open Opportunity Scoring, and review the data requirements before enabling, because the feature will not build a local model when closed volumes are too low and you want to set expectations before you flip the switch. On the same page you confirm the opportunity Stage field is the standard one and that every stage picklist value is mapped to Open, Closed Won or Closed Lost. You open Lead Scoring settings, choose how many days of activity to consider, and either accept all lead fields or select the custom field set the model will read. You create a segment for each lead group that needs its own model, staying inside the criteria limit and remembering that segments remove access to the global model. You open Einstein Activity Capture settings, connect the mail accounts, and review the matching and exclusion rules so synced activity lands on the correct records. Finally you add the Opportunity Score and Lead Score fields to the relevant list views and reports and decide whether scores should also show on the forecasts page.

## Exam Traps

Three traps dominate this objective. The first is believing Lead Scoring needs the same volumes as Opportunity Scoring, or that 200 converted leads will do. Lead Scoring needs at least 1,000 leads created in the last 200 days with at least 120 converted to an account and contact. The 200 and 200 pair belongs to Opportunity Scoring, and mixing the two threshold sets is the most common way to lose a point here.

The second trap is believing you can export lead scores. Scores exist only on records, list views and reports, so an answer that builds a data extract or a report export of the Lead Score field is wrong. The score is a display and a Flow-readable field, not a bulk-exportable asset.

The third trap is believing segmentation always improves results. Creating segments removes access to the global model, so a segment with thin data leaves those leads unscored entirely, where a combined model would at least have scored them globally. Segmentation is a precision-for-coverage trade, and a scenario that segments a data-thin region should be read as a risk rather than an improvement.

## What a consultant should walk away with

Before you enable any predictive feature, check three things: the license, the volume, and the field discipline. Lead Scoring needs Sales Cloud Einstein and 1,000 leads created in the last 200 days with at least 120 converted; Opportunity Scoring needs 200 closed won and 200 closed lost in 24 months with a two-day lifespan each, and it reaches eligible users without an Einstein license. Confirm the standard Stage field is in use and every picklist value maps to its stage type, because a renamed stage with a stale mapping is a silent corruption of the win rate. Assume nothing is real time: first opportunity scores can take up to 48 hours, open-deal scores refresh every few hours, the model is reanalyzed roughly monthly, lead scores refresh several times a day, and Activity Capture can take up to 24 hours to show up in reports. Keep the score and the factors distinct in your own head so you coach from the explanation rather than the number. Then treat Activity Capture as load-bearing rather than optional, because it is the signal source the models read, and switching it off for a team degrades their scores whether or not anyone asked for it to be on.
