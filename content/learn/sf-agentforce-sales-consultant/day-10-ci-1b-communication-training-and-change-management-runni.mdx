# Lesson 10: Communication, training, and change management: running the plan under pressure

Two weeks before go-live, integration testing has the only Full sandbox and will not give it up. The trainer needs realistic records. The sponsor, who signed off on the budget in the spring, has gone quiet. A department you marked as unaffected turns out to be the loudest voice in the first steering meeting after launch. Every one of these has a defensible answer, and the defensible answer is almost never the most convenient one.

The naive response is to share the Full sandbox across phases, deliver one big training session, and hope the sponsor reappears. That combination is how the same failure recurs on project after project: a refresh destroys someone's work, the skills from a long ago session have decayed by the time the user needs them, and the message has no local voice once the sponsor stops repeating it. The pressure points are predictable, which is good news, because predictable problems have rehearsed answers.

This lesson serves objective ci-1b in the Consulting and Implementation Strategies domain, worth 25% of the exam, and covers the same readiness ground as lesson 9 but from the angle of running the plan when reality pushes back. By the end you will be able to choose the right training environment, defend a training approach under pressure, name the change management failure modes, and hold the project to outcome metrics rather than vanity numbers.

## Deep Dive

### The pressure points are always the same

The scenarios on this objective repeat four pressure points, so learn them as a set. The training environment is the only Full sandbox and UAT also needs it. The sponsor goes quiet after approval. A group you classified as unaffected turns out to be the loudest in the room. Your metric moves because people are logging in, not because anything improved. Each one has a defensible answer, and the exam tests whether you pick it over the convenient one, such as borrowing the Full sandbox or pointing at logins as proof of success.

The reason these four recur is that they all come from the same place, a readiness plan that was built for the happy path. When the plan assumes the sandbox will be free, the sponsor will stay engaged, nobody unexpected will resist, and any usage number will do, it breaks the first time reality contradicts it. Reading a scenario, look for the moment the plan meets pressure and ask which trade-off the scenario is really testing. Usually it is the training environment or the metric, because those are the two with the cleanest right answers.

Take the sandbox squeeze as the model case. You have one Full sandbox, integration owns it for the next three weeks, and the trainer needs a realistic org before go-live. The convenient answer is to share the Full sandbox and hope the refresh cycle does not collide with anyone's work. The defensible answer is to stand up a Partial Copy sandbox for training, accept that it carries a sample of the data rather than all of it, and leave the Full sandbox to the phases that genuinely need every record. That is the shape of every answer on this objective: find the phase that can tolerate less than production, and give it the lighter environment, rather than promising the heaviest environment to everyone and rationing it by luck.

### The training environment is a real trade-off

A Full sandbox is the best replica of production. It carries all the data and the storage to match, which makes it the right place to validate an integration end to end. It also refreshes on a long cycle and you usually have exactly one per org, which means it is also the integration and UAT environment. That scarcity is the whole problem. Sharing it across phases means a refresh wipes someone's work, and you cannot refresh it often enough to keep the trainer and the testers happy at once.[^2]

A Partial Copy sandbox is the usual right answer for training. It copies metadata plus a sample of production data drawn by a sandbox template, gives you a few gigabytes of data, and refreshes on a short five day cycle.[^1] That combination trains users on realistic records without consuming the single Full sandbox the other phases need. A Developer or Developer Pro sandbox refreshes daily but holds only metadata with a small data allowance, so it is a build environment, not a user environment, and training on it teaches reps a simplified process that does not hold up in production. Choose Partial Copy unless data volume is literally the point of the training.

### Training modality: the blended answer wins

Instructor led sessions surface role confusion fast, because you hear the questions in the room and can correct a misunderstanding before it hardens. They also consume calendars and cannot be repeated for free, which limits how many people you reach. Self paced modules scale to hundreds of users and rot the moment the configuration changes, because a video that shows the old screen teaches the old process. Embedded in-app guidance and prompts scale, sit on the exact page where the work happens, and stay current as long as you maintain them.[^3]

That is why the blended answer wins on the exam: a short formal session to install the mental model, then in-app guidance for the last mile on the record page. Timing matters as much as mode. Train close to go-live, because skills delivered six weeks early have decayed by the time the user ever needs them, and plan a refresher at roughly thirty days, once the questions have become specific and worth answering. When usage slips after go-live, the fastest fix is prompts on the high friction pages, not another calendar heavy session, because the guidance lands exactly where the confusion is.

### Change management failure modes and their counter-moves

Change management is where projects actually fail, and the failure modes are predictable enough to name in advance. The silent majority never reports friction and keeps a shadow process alive, so your usage numbers look acceptable while the business value never appears. The vocal minority dominates the feedback channel, so you risk building for the loudest three users. The backlog of small frictions gets logged for after go-live and silently disappears at project close, because nobody owned it. Each one is a quiet failure that a plan can prevent.

The counter-moves are unglamorous. Put a named champion in every team, so the message has a local voice and friction has somewhere to go. Keep a friction log with a single owner, so the small annoyances do not vanish. Stand up a 30, 60, and 90 day review on the calendar before go-live day, so the reinforcement is planned rather than improvised. And make sure the sponsor is willing to say out loud that the old process is over, because a sponsor who approves the budget but never speaks to the users is a name on a slide, and a name on a slide does not change anyone's Monday.

### Separate vanity metrics from outcome metrics

Take the metrics half of the objective seriously, because it is where a project gets defended with the wrong number. Separate vanity metrics from outcome metrics and say so plainly. Logins count presence, nothing more. Active user percentage is better but still measures presence. Feature level adoption, such as the percentage of quotes created through the new flow, measures behaviour. Outcome metrics measure the business, such as quote turnaround time or opportunity close rate. Capture all three, but never defend the project with the first, because a rising login count proves only that people opened the door.

Two tools give you the raw material. The Lightning Usage App shows which app is being used, how often, and how pages are performing. The Adoption Dashboards app shows active users and adoption trends by user group, so you can see where usage is concentrated and where it is stalling.[^6] Set the target and the review date when you set the metric, and put the dashboard in front of the sponsor rather than in a folder, because a metric nobody reviews is not governance, it is decoration.

### What Agentforce changes: session-level adoption and the Trust Layer

Agentforce changes two things about this lesson. First, adoption of an agent is measured at the session level, not just the user level. Agent Analytics reports sessions, resolutions, and the share of sessions escalated to a human, and that escalation share is the most actionable number you will have, because it points directly at the topic, prompt, or data that needs work.[^5] A rising escalation rate on one intent tells you exactly where to spend the next sprint, which a login chart never could.

Second, the Einstein Trust Layer settings, including data masking and zero data retention, live in org settings and do not travel with a deployment.[^4] That means you must confirm they are enabled in both the training and the production org before anyone uses an agent, because a setting that exists in one org and not the other is a surprise waiting for go-live. Train users on what the agent does with their data, because that question always comes up, and an unanswered version of it stops adoption cold even when the agent works perfectly.

### Sequencing is the exam's favourite angle

Sequencing is where this objective gets tested hardest. Readiness work happens before go-live, not during it. If a scenario describes training delivered two weeks after the launch, or a metric chosen at the launch party, or champions recruited only after the complaints arrive, the correct answer is almost always to move the activity earlier. The scenario is offering you a plan that is technically complete but badly ordered, and the fix is the order, not more effort.

Reinforcement is the one element that legitimately continues past go-live, and it is the element teams cut first when the budget tightens. Keep it. The projects that hold their adoption figure at month three are the ones that planned the 30, 60, and 90 day review before they planned the go-live party, because they treated adoption as something to sustain rather than something to announce. When a question asks what to do four weeks after launch, the answer usually reaches for reinforcement, and the reinforcement usually lives on the page where the friction is.

## Exam Traps

Three traps dominate this objective. The first is believing a login is a useful adoption metric. Logins count presence, not use, so an answer that reports logins instead of a feature level or outcome metric describes activity without measuring success. The second is assuming one Full sandbox can serve integration, UAT, and training at once. A Full sandbox has a long refresh interval and you usually have exactly one, so sharing it across phases means a refresh destroys someone's work, and Partial Copy is the usual training environment instead. The third is thinking training is a one time event before go-live. Skills from instructor led and self paced training decay fastest in the first month, which is why reinforcement and embedded in-app guidance carry the last mile.

Watch two more. A sponsor who approves the budget but never speaks to the users is not an engaged sponsor, so a scenario describing that sponsor points at the missing champions and the missing voice, not at the training. And Agentforce adoption is measured at the session level, so a scenario about improving an agent reaches for Agent Analytics and the escalation share rather than a login count.

## What a consultant should walk away with

Run readiness under pressure by answering each trade-off on its merits. Put training on a Partial Copy sandbox and keep the Full sandbox for integration and UAT. Blend a short formal session with in-app guidance, train close to go-live, and plan a thirty day refresher plus a 30, 60, 90 review before you plan the launch. Name a champion per team, keep a friction log with one owner, and make the sponsor speak. Defend the project with outcome metrics, not logins, and for an agent, watch the session level escalation share because it tells you where to fix the topic, the prompt, or the data. Do the readiness work early and the reinforcement late, and the adoption figure holds at month three.
