# Lesson 28: Opportunities, Splits, and Teams: Choosing the Right Construct

A services client sends you a requirements document with four bullet points that sound unrelated. New business deals and renewals must show different stages. The forecast must be more accurate. A solutions engineer and an account executive both need credit on the same deal. Sales leaders want to know who signs the contracts. Each of those is a different feature on the same Opportunity object, and the reason the document reads as one lump is that the author thinks of all four as "the opportunity." Your job in the next meeting is to pull them apart and name the construct behind each one.

That is the actual skill this objective tests. Given a requirement, you have to name the object or the mechanism it is really about, and the failure mode is choosing the wrong construct because it sounds close. A team that wants different stages sometimes gets a validation rule that hides stage names; a team that wants an accurate forecast sometimes gets told to use the right stage name; a team that needs shared credit sometimes gets an overlay note in a description field. Each of those is a plausible-sounding answer that does not do the job.

This lesson serves requirement pa-5b, in the Practical Application of Agentforce Sales Expertise domain, which carries 24 percent of the exam. The objective asks you, given a set of requirements, to determine how to support different business and sales process scenarios for Campaigns, Leads, and Opportunities. By the end you can split one object into several motions with record types and sales processes, trace a forecast number to the mapping that produces it, tell contact roles from opportunity teams without hesitating, and put credit on more than one rep with splits that actually reach the forecast.

<TLDR>

- Record types mapped to sales processes give each motion its own stages
- The forecast follows the forecast category a stage maps to, not the stage name
- Contact roles name the customer side, teams grant access, splits put credit on more than one rep

</TLDR>


## Deep Dive

### Record types and sales processes serve several motions at once

A sales process is an ordered set of stages, and it is bound to a record type. The record type is the key and the sales process is the value that record type gets. That relationship is the mechanism behind the whole pattern. A new business sale and a renewal both live on the Opportunity object, but the New Business record type shows the New Business sales process with its own stages while the Renewal record type shows its own shorter set. The help page on managing multiple business processes puts it directly: you assign the process to a record type, and the record type determines which user profiles see that process.[^8]

The stage picklist on a given record is limited to its sales process's stages. That is the detail that answers the scenario. Two teams never see each other's stage names, because their records are carrying different processes. When a requirement mentions two sales motions, two sets of win criteria, or different required fields at different points in the cycle, the answer starts with two record types, each mapped to its own sales process. A single sales process with every stage both teams use is the wrong answer, because that is exactly the situation the client is complaining about.

The same mechanism generalizes, and knowing that saves you from inventing a custom solution. Lead processes work this way, support processes work this way, and solution processes work this way, all under the same business processes setup, all bound to a record type. So when a requirement asks for different lead statuses per channel, or different case statuses per support tier, the answer is another record type and another process rather than a new field or a validation rule. One record type per object is the default, and the master record type holds every value in the master picklist. You cannot delete the values a record type depends on while that record type exists, which is why the sequence is process first, then record type, then page layout.

![One Opportunity object carrying two record types: the New Business record type maps to the New Business sales process, and the Renewal record type maps to the Renewal process. Each record's stage picklist is limited to its own process's stages, so the two teams never see each other's stage names.](</diagrams/day-28-pa-5b-opportunities-splits-and-teams-choosing-the-right/1-record-type-process-binding.png>)


One design note that pays off later: decide record types and sales processes before you build automation that routes records between them, because a conversion or a flow picks a record type whether you planned for it or not. If a lead conversion drops a deal into the Renewal record type, the rep sees the renewal stages, and if that was not the intent you have a training problem that looks like a data problem. If the client later asks for role-specific page layouts, those ride on the record type too, so a record type decision is also a layout decision whether you wanted it to be or not.

### The stage-to-forecast-category mapping is under every forecast number

Each stage maps to a forecast category: Pipeline, Best Case, Commit, Closed, or Omitted, plus whatever else the org has. The forecast is driven by the forecast category, not by the stage name. The help page on stage-to-category mappings shows a sample table where Prospecting maps to Pipeline, Perception Analysis maps to Best Case, Negotiation/Review maps to Commit, Closed Won maps to Closed, and Closed Lost maps to Omitted so it drops out of the forecast.[^4] When you change a stage's mapping, the amounts of opportunities sitting in that stage move between forecast categories.

That single fact explains a whole class of forecast defects. Renaming a stage changes what reps see but not what finance forecasts, because the mapping is untouched. A stage mapped to the wrong category misstates the forecast even when every rep updated their stage faithfully, which is the exact scenario the exam likes: reps did nothing wrong and the number is still wrong, so the answer is the mapping. You edit the mapping from Object Manager on the Opportunity object, on the Stage field, where each picklist value shows the category it is mapped to.

There is also a policy choice inside the mapping. By default the stage drives the category, and users can edit the Forecast Category field on opportunities they own, overriding the default. In Lightning Experience all forecast categories are available for all stages, whereas in Classic the stage limited which categories were selectable.[^5] You should say plainly which behavior the client chose, because it decides whether an optimistic rep can override a forecast. The trap is a requirements document that talks about stage names and never mentions the mapping underneath them; the consultant who asks about the mapping is the one who finds the real problem.

### Contact roles name the customer side

An OpportunityContactRole is a join between a contact and an opportunity, carrying a role such as Economic Buyer, Decision Maker, or Technical Evaluator, and one role is flagged as primary.[^1] The object reference is precise about the primary flag: each opportunity has only one primary contact. Contact roles are what reporting uses to see who influenced or signed a deal, and they are the records many BI tools read to attribute revenue to people rather than campaigns.[^9] You add them from the Contact Roles related list on the opportunity, choosing from the account's contacts, and you can add many at once.

The distinction that matters is which side of the deal the person is on. A contact role describes a person on the customer side: the buyer, the evaluator, the signer. When a requirement asks who signs the contract or who on the account is the decision maker, the answer is an opportunity contact role with the appropriate role value, and a primary contact designated. Contact roles do not grant any internal user access, because they are not about internal users at all.

### Opportunity teams name your side and grant access

An opportunity team is a set of OpportunityTeamMember records that name the internal users working the deal and their team role, such as Sales Rep, Solutions Engineer, or Executive Sponsor.[^2] The object reference notes the object is available only in orgs that have enabled team selling, and that the team grants those users default access to the opportunity through the sharing model. That access is the reason the construct exists: when a solutions engineer joins a deal, adding them to the team hands them visibility without a manual share.

So contact roles describe people on the customer side, and opportunity teams describe people on your side. They are not the same list and they answer different questions, and swapping them is a common distractor. A requirement about who on the account signs points at contact roles. A requirement about which of our people work the deal, or about internal users needing access, points at opportunity teams. One more consequence worth knowing: opportunity teams interact with ownership changes, and if the former owner is on the team, the interface lets you choose their access when ownership moves.

### Splits and split forecasts put credit on more than one rep

An opportunity split is an OpportunitySplit child record, and splits divide either revenue credit or overlay credit across team members by percentage, controlled by split types you configure.[^3] The default split sits on the Opportunity Amount and is validated to total 100 percent. Splits differ from the simple overlay field on an opportunity team member: the overlay field is a lightweight credit note, while a split record drives split reporting and can feed split forecasts. That is the distinction the exam leans on, because a client who needs forecast credit for two people cannot get it from an overlay note.

The setup has a shape worth knowing. You enable splits in Setup under Opportunity Splits Settings, after enabling team selling, and the enablement runs as a background process that emails you when it finishes.[^6] During that window the help page recommends having users leave splits alone. You can create up to six custom split types, specify whether each must always total 100 percent, and choose which currency field a split type is based on, though not a formula currency field.[^7] When the opportunity owner is not already on the team, the enablement adds the owner and assigns that owner 100 percent of the split amount. Then you add the Opportunity Splits related list or component to the page layout so reps can see and edit the percentages.

The validation rule is the one that catches people: a set of splits that does not sum to 100 percent is refused, so a design that lets percentages be entered freely will produce friction, not flexibility. When a requirement says a solutions engineer and an account executive both need forecast credit on the same deal, the answer is opportunity splits with split types that feed split forecasts, not a note in a description field and not the overlay field.

### How the org gets built, end to end

The order here matters because the pieces reference each other, and the record type you build second depends on the process you built first.

1. In Setup, under Feature Settings, open Sales and then Sales Processes to define the stages for each sales motion before you create any record type.
2. In Setup, under Object Manager, create an opportunity record type per motion, then assign each record type to its sales process.
3. In Setup, under Feature Settings, open Sales and then Forecasts Settings to map every stage to a forecast category, and decide whether reps can set the category manually.
4. In Setup, under Feature Settings, open Sales and then Opportunity Splits to enable splits, create the split types, and add them to the page layout.
5. In Setup, under Object Manager, confirm the Opportunity Contact Roles and Opportunity Team related lists and the role values and team roles the client uses.
6. On the opportunity record, confirm the record type shows the right stages, add the contact role with a primary contact, add the team members, and enter splits that sum to 100 percent.

### Reading a requirement and naming the construct

The objective's phrase about a given set of requirements is where this lands, and the reasoning is a lookup once you separate the questions. Requirements that mention different stage names or different required fields per team point at record types and sales processes. Requirements that mention forecasting confidence by deal point at stage and forecast category mapping, and the choice about whether reps can override the category. Requirements that mention splitting commission or forecast across contributors point at opportunity splits with split types that feed split forecasts. Requirements that mention who on the customer side signs point at contact roles, and who on your side works the deal points at opportunity teams. Requirements about inbound interest and routing point at leads, assignment rules, and queues. Requirements about measuring a marketing touchpoint point at campaigns, campaign members, and campaign influence.

Two cross-object traps close the objective. First, campaign influence and opportunity data are separate relationships: crediting a campaign does not create or change an opportunity's amount, and an amount does not attribute to any campaign unless influence records exist, so a build that assumes the link is automatic will report zero attribution. Second, lead conversion and opportunity record types interact: the opportunity a conversion creates uses a record type, and the sales process that record type maps to is where its stages come from. A conversion that drops a deal into a record type with the wrong process leaves reps with stages that do not match their motion. Read the requirement, name the object it is really about, then name the setting, and the four bullets from that requirements document resolve into four different answers.

## Exam Traps

<Callout kind="warning">

Three traps dominate this objective. The first is believing a forecast follows the stage name. The forecast follows the forecast category the stage maps to, so a renaming that leaves the mapping alone changes nothing, and a stage mapped to the wrong category misstates the forecast no matter how faithfully reps updated it. When a scenario describes accurate stage hygiene and a wrong forecast, the answer is the mapping.

The second trap is believing contact roles and opportunity teams are the same list. Contact roles name people on the customer side, with exactly one primary contact per opportunity, while opportunity teams name your internal users and grant them default access through the sharing model. A requirement about signers is contact roles; a requirement about collaborators or access is teams. An answer that offers a team member role for a buyer, or a contact role for an internal engineer, has the two constructs confused.

The third trap is believing the overlay field on a team member can replace an opportunity split. The overlay field is only a credit note, while a split record drives split reporting and can feed split forecasts, and splits are validated to total 100 percent. When a requirement says credit must reach the forecast for two people on one deal, the answer has to be a split record, because an overlay note never reaches a forecast total.

</Callout>


## What a consultant should walk away with

<KeyTakeaways>

- Different stages or required fields per team mean two record types, each mapped to its own sales process, decided before building any conversion flow
- A forecast that does not match the work means checking the stage-to-forecast-category mapping before blaming reps, and deciding whether reps can override the category
- Who signs on the customer side is an opportunity contact role; who works your side is an opportunity team that grants access
- Credit that must reach the forecast for more than one person is an opportunity split validated to 100 percent, kept separate from campaign influence

</KeyTakeaways>
