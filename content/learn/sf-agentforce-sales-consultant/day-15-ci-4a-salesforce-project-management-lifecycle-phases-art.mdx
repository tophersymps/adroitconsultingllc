# Lesson 15: Salesforce Project Management Lifecycle: Phases, Artifacts, and Gates

A steering meeting ends with smiles. The business lead says the system looks good, the status report is green, and everyone agrees the user acceptance testing is complete. Three months later the same business lead argues that a feature nobody built was always in scope, and the vendor has no signed document tying the delivered solution to anything the client agreed to. The delivery was real. The acceptance was sentiment. The gap between those two is the whole subject of this lesson.

The naive view of project management is that it is administration, a set of status updates and slide decks that sit beside the technical work. That view fails at exactly the moment it matters. The examinable skill is not reciting phase names. It is knowing that every phase has an entry criterion, a defined output, and an exit gate that someone signs, and that the artifacts are what make the difference between a delivery you can defend and a delivery you can only describe.

This lesson serves requirement ci-4a in the Consulting and Implementation Strategies domain, which carries 25 percent of the exam. It covers the lifecycle skeleton, the artifact set that fixes scope and proves requirements, methodology as a decision, the environment and promotion choices that a Salesforce build makes load-bearing, and the acceptance that closes a phase. After it, you should be able to name the gate, the artifact, and the signer for any phase you are asked about.

<TLDR>

- Every phase has an entry criterion, a defined output, and an exit gate that someone signs
- Keep the requirements document and the design document separate: one says what, the other says how
- Choose the methodology by the constraints, and match the sandbox to the test, Partial Copy for realistic volume

</TLDR>


## Deep Dive

### The skeleton every delivery shares

Every Salesforce delivery you will be asked about runs through the same skeleton, whatever the client calls it. Initiation produces the project charter, the team roster, and the governance model. Discovery captures what the business actually needs. Design turns those needs into a solution definition and a data model. Build configures the platform and writes only the code that configuration cannot honestly cover. Test proves it against scripts written before the build, not after. Deploy is the cutover, and Run is hypercare plus a handover to the client's admin team. The phase names are not the examinable part. What is examinable is that each phase has entry criteria, a defined output, and an exit gate that someone signs.

That framing is what turns the lifecycle from a diagram into a control. A phase without an exit gate is a slide. A phase whose output nobody signs is a phase you cannot prove you finished. When a scenario asks why a delivery slipped or why scope grew, the answer is usually that a gate was skipped and the artifact it would have produced does not exist.

### The artifact set, and the two documents consultants conflate

The artifact set matters more on the exam than the phase names. A project charter fixes scope, budget, and the decision rights. A business requirements document records what the business wants and why. A solution design document records how you will configure the platform to satisfy those requirements, including objects, record types, page layouts, the sharing model, and integrations. A test plan names the scripts and the pass criteria. A cutover plan sequences the data loads and the activation steps. A go-live readiness review is the last gate before users touch production.

Two deliverables that consultants conflate and the exam separates are the requirements document and the design document. One says what, the other says how, and if you write them as one file you have already lost the ability to argue about scope, because a change to the design will read as a change to the requirements. Keep them apart, and a disagreement about a page layout stays a design conversation instead of becoming a scope fight. That separation is worth real money on a fixed-price engagement, and it is why the exam keeps returning to it.

### Methodology is a decision, not a religion

Methodology is a decision, not a religion. Waterfall runs scope, then schedule, then cost as fixed in that order, and it survives where the client is regulated and the date is contractual. Scrum fixes time and cost and lets scope flex, which suits a platform where a configuration change lands in an afternoon and a demo can reshape the backlog. Kanban fixes nothing and limits work in progress, which suits a support or enhancement stream rather than a greenfield build.

A waterfall delivery, because the scope is fixed and the date is contractual, is the honest choice for a regulated launch, and the exam expects you to say so rather than default to agile because agile sounds modern. The scenario it hands you is a fixed-price statement of work, a locked scope, and a hard regulatory go-live, and the correct answer is waterfall, because Scrum would fix time and cost and let scope flex, which breaks the locked scope. Most real Salesforce engagements are hybrid: predictive planning for the milestones and iterative build between them, which is the honest description of how experienced teams actually run.

![Methodology decision guide: waterfall fixes scope then schedule then cost for a regulated contractual date, Scrum fixes time and cost and lets scope flex, Kanban fixes nothing and limits work in progress for a support stream, and hybrid runs predictive milestones with iterative build between them.](</diagrams/day-15-ci-4a-salesforce-project-management-lifecycle-phases-art/2-methodology-decision.png>)

### Environment strategy is load-bearing for a Salesforce build

Agentforce work makes the environment strategy load-bearing rather than administrative. Sandboxes come in tiers with hard limits you must be able to state. A Developer sandbox holds 200 MB of data and is for a single developer. A Developer Pro sandbox holds 1 GB and is for development with light testing against a subset of records. A Partial Copy sandbox uses a sandbox template to pull a defined subset of production records into 5 GB of storage, which is the tier you use when a test needs realistic data volumes without copying every record.[^1] A Full Copy sandbox mirrors production data and storage, and its main constraint is the refresh cadence: a Full sandbox can be refreshed every 29 days, Developer and Developer Pro sandboxes once per day, and a Partial Copy sandbox every 5 days.[^2]

Partial Copy is the sweet spot for performance and integration testing, because it gives you production-like volume without a full copy and without consuming the single Full sandbox. The exam phrases the question as needing production-realistic data volumes for a performance test without the risk of copying every production record, and the answer is Partial Copy, because it uses a template to pull a defined subset into 5 GB. Full Copy is the trap, because it mirrors everything but refreshes only every 29 days, so a test cycle that needs frequent fresh data is the wrong fit for it.

![Sandbox tier comparison: Developer at 200 MB for a single developer, Developer Pro at 1 GB for light testing against a subset of records, Partial Copy at 5 GB using a sandbox template to pull a defined subset of production records, and Full Copy mirroring production data and storage but refreshing only every 29 days.](</diagrams/day-15-ci-4a-salesforce-project-management-lifecycle-phases-art/3-sandbox-tiers.png>)

### Promotion tooling is a judgment call, not a click

Promotion tooling is another place the exam tests judgment rather than clicks. Change sets move metadata between orgs that share a connection, they do not move data, they do not handle deletions, and they do not version.[^3] Unlocked packages bring versioning and dependency management, which suits a modular build that will be extended over years.[^5] Scratch orgs plus the Salesforce CLI give each developer a disposable org defined by source, which is how you run a real continuous integration pipeline.[^6] Lifecycle Center, formerly DevOps Center, is the low-code front end for teams that cannot run pipelines from a terminal, adding work items and pipeline stages over the same source-driven model.[^7]

The sequencing rule that matters is that you choose the promotion mechanism before the build starts. Retrofitting packages onto a project that shipped with change sets is a migration, not a task, because the source of truth has to move from the org to a repository and every artifact has to be re-cut.[^4] Decide early, and the decision is cheap. Decide late, and you are rebuilding the delivery you already shipped.

### Estimation, status reporting, and the decision log

Estimation decays unless you keep replenishing it. Story points measure relative effort against your own delivery history, so a velocity measured on this team beats a benchmark from a blog. T-shirt sizing is legitimate when the sponsor needs a range, not a number, and when you say so plainly. Three-point estimation, optimistic plus pessimistic plus four times likely, all divided by six, gives you a defensible number for the schedule-critical items. Add an explicit buffer that the sponsor can see and can spend, rather than hiding slack inside each task. The trap is re-estimating every week and calling that rigor: a velocity that moves with the wind tells the sponsor nothing and destroys your credibility at the moment you most need it.

Status reporting exists to force decisions, and its structures are examinable. A weekly status report carries a RAG status, the work completed, the work planned, the current blockers, and the decisions needed. Burn-down and burn-up charts show trend, not truth on a single day. The RAID log carries risks, assumptions, issues, and dependencies in one place so nothing hides in an email thread, which is why practitioners call the combined artifact the RAID log. The escalation matrix names who calls whom and at what threshold, and a steering committee is the tier where scope and date decisions actually get made. The single best habit is a decision log: every decision, who made it, when, and what it ruled out. It is the artifact you reach for when someone asks in month six why the sharing model is the way it is.

### UAT, sign-off, and hypercare

UAT and sign-off are where the project becomes contractually real. Acceptance criteria are written before build, in given, when, then form, and they map one to one to the test scripts the business will run. Defects get severities: a severity 1 defect stops the process, a severity 2 breaks a core task with no workaround, and lower severities get deferred with the sponsor's agreement. A signed acceptance document that references the acceptance criteria in the statement of work is what closes the phase, not a green status report and not a happy steering meeting.

The exam will hand you a scenario where everyone is smiling and ask what is missing, and the answer is usually the signed document that ties the delivered solution to the criteria the client already agreed to. A verbal thank-you in the minutes is not acceptance. A green report from the delivery lead is sentiment, not acceptance. A successful cutover rehearsal proves the plan works, not that the business accepted the result. Hypercare is a defined window with an exit criterion, not an open-ended favor, and the exit criterion is the same kind of signed artifact that closed the earlier phases. Name the gate, the artifact, and the signer, and the exam stops asking you to guess.

## Exam Traps

<Callout kind="warning">

Three traps dominate this objective. The first is believing a Full Copy sandbox is the default for testing. Full Copy mirrors production but refreshes only every 29 days, so a test cycle that needs frequent fresh data belongs on a Developer Pro or Partial Copy sandbox, not Full Copy. The second is believing change sets deploy deletions. Change sets carry metadata changes, they do not delete components in the target org and they do not version, so a project that needs either must choose unlocked packages or a source-driven pipeline. The third is believing UAT ends when the business stops logging defects. The phase ends when the acceptance document that references the statement of work's acceptance criteria is signed, so a quiet inbox is not the gate and a green status report is not acceptance.

</Callout>


## What a consultant should walk away with

<KeyTakeaways>

- Name the gate, the artifact, and the signer for any phase, rather than reciting the phase names
- Keep the requirements document and the design document separate, so scope and solution stay distinguishable
- Choose the methodology by the constraints: waterfall for a locked scope, Scrum for flex, Kanban for support, hybrid when both
- Match the sandbox to the test, Partial Copy for realistic volume and Full Copy for the release candidate, and close on a signed acceptance document

</KeyTakeaways>


The exam rewards the person who can name the gate, the artifact, and the signer, not the person who can recite the phase names. Keep the requirements document and the design document separate so scope and solution stay distinguishable. Choose the methodology by the constraints: waterfall when the date is contractual and the scope is locked, Scrum when scope can flex, Kanban for a support stream, and hybrid when the honest answer is both. Match the sandbox to the test, with Partial Copy for realistic volume and Full Copy saved for the release candidate, because Full Copy refreshes only every 29 days. Choose the promotion mechanism before the build starts. Write acceptance criteria before the build, map them to the test scripts, and close the phase on a signed acceptance document that references the statement of work, not on a happy meeting. Then give hypercare a window and an exit criterion, so the delivery ends on a decision rather than a drift.
