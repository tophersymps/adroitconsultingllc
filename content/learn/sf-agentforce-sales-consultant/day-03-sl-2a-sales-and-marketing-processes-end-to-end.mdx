# Lesson 3: Sales and Marketing Processes End to End

A rep has qualified a deal and wants to send a formal quote. She opens the opportunity, builds it accurately, then calls you because the quote button will not produce anything useful. Nothing is broken. The opportunity has no products on a price book, so there is nothing for a quote to be generated from, and the deal sits in a process the org never finished configuring. The feature worked exactly as designed. The process around it had a gap.

That gap is what this objective tests. The exam does not ask you to recite object names; it asks whether you understand the standard selling motion well enough to wire it up and to spot the missing link when a scenario describes one. A question about where a field lives is really a question about the object that owns that stage. A question about why attribution numbers look wrong is really a question about the data underneath the model. Read the process, find the owner of each stage, and the answers fall into place.

This lesson serves requirement sl-2a in the Sales Lifecycle domain, at 20 percent of the exam. You will learn the end-to-end sales and marketing process, the objects that own each stage, and the implementation considerations a scenario is fishing for when it describes a conversion, a campaign, a quote, a forecast, or a territory.

<TLDR>

- The selling spine is Lead to Opportunity to Quote to Close, and each stage has an object that owns it
- Campaigns roll up through a hierarchy and multi-touch credit comes from a configured influence model with a time window
- Forecast categories map from stages, the forecast hierarchy is separate, and territory adds access on top of roles after activation

</TLDR>


## Deep Dive

### The spine is Lead to Opportunity to Quote to Close

The selling motion has a spine, and you should be able to walk it without pausing. A Lead is a person or a company that has not been qualified. Converting it creates an Account and a Contact, and, when the org sells that way, an Opportunity. Conversion is not a copy; it is a mapping, and the standard Lead fields map to Account, Contact, Person Account, and Opportunity fields according to rules the admin controls. Company becomes the Account Name, the Contact's Account, and the Opportunity's name and account, while Lead Source lands on the Account's Account Source, the Contact's Lead Source, and the Opportunity's Lead Source [^1]. Custom Lead fields map wherever the admin points them, and that is why field mapping is a design decision rather than a default.

The two behaviors to hold in your head are that conversion can create a new Account or merge into an existing one, and that not every conversion produces an Opportunity. Those two facts generate most of the scenario questions about this stage. A stem that says converted leads are polluting the Account list is describing a matching problem at conversion, where the wrong rule merged a Lead into an existing Account. A stem that says a field went missing after conversion is describing a mapping that was never set. In both cases the object that owns the stage is the Lead, and the fix lives in its configuration.

Keep the sequence of owners straight as you move down the spine. The Lead owns qualification and conversion. The Account owns the relationship and the billing context. The Contact owns the person. The Opportunity owns the deal, its stage, its products, and its forecast category. The Quote owns the formal offer and its lines. When someone asks where a status or a number lives, answer with the object that owns that stage, because a scenario is almost never testing whether you can name a field; it is testing whether you know which record to configure.

### Campaigns, members, and influence

Leads and Contacts are joined to marketing effort through the Campaign object and its CampaignMember records. A CampaignMember is a Lead or a Contact, and it carries a member status such as Sent or Responded that the marketing team manages through a status picklist configured per campaign [^6]. Member statuses matter more than they look, because reports and influence calculations depend on them being consistent across campaigns, and the standard advice is to build one set of statuses for each type of campaign you run.

Campaigns form a hierarchy through the parent campaign, which lets roll-up reports total a channel, a quarter, or a program. For revenue attribution you enable Customizable Campaign Influence, which applies an attribution model and a time window to attribute Opportunity revenue across the multiple campaigns that touched the deal, rather than crediting only the primary campaign [^2]. Influence models scan active campaigns to find members who also hold a contact role on an open opportunity, and once an opportunity closes, influence records stop being created. That distinction between one primary campaign and multi-touch influence is a recurring exam point, and it is also the difference between a single-source report and an attribution report you can defend.

### Sales process, stages, and the quotable opportunity

On the Opportunity, the Stage field records where the deal sits, and a sales process is bound to a record type, so different product lines or divisions can run different stages with a Path that matches. Sales process and record type have to agree: change the record type and you change which stages are valid for that opportunity. This is why an admin who renames a stage in a sales process without checking the record type ends up with a Path that no longer matches the process, and why a scenario about stages that vary by division is really a question about record types.

Products and quotes are where many deals stall. You add products from a price book to build OpportunityLineItem records, and you generate the Quote from the Opportunity so the quote lines follow the opportunity products. The Quote has its own stage and a sync status, and once it is synced to the opportunity, changes flow both ways: the opportunity Amount tracks the quote's total price, and a change to a quote line item syncs to the opportunity product and back [^3]. After the deal is won, the path continues through Contract and Order. A scenario asking what makes an opportunity quotable is really asking whether the opportunity has products on a price book, because without them there is nothing to generate a quote from.

### Forecasting has its own object model

Forecasting runs on its own objects rather than on a view of the pipeline. The Opportunity has a Forecast Category field, and each stage maps to a category such as Pipeline, Best Case, Commit, Closed, or Omitted, so the number rolls up even before the stage changes [^4]. Think of it this way: the stage is where the rep says the deal is, and the forecast category is how the business wants that stage counted. Collaborative Forecasts rolls amounts up a forecast hierarchy by category, and forecast types let you roll up revenue, quantity, or product family side by side. Quotas attach to forecast managers and can be customized per user per period. Enable forecasts without a configured hierarchy and quotas and the rollups are meaningless, which is the implementation consideration a scenario about forecast accuracy is fishing for.

The forecast hierarchy is the detail candidates miss most often. It is a separate roll-up you configure, and it can follow a different chain of managers than the role structure. That means a manager who should see a roll-up has to be enabled in the forecast hierarchy even if the role hierarchy already puts reps beneath them. When a scenario says a manager cannot see a team's roll-up, the forecast hierarchy is usually the missing piece.

### Territory models coverage, not just roles

Enterprise Territory Management models coverage separately from roles. You build a territory model, define territory types, and place territories in a hierarchy, and the model sits in a planning state until you activate it [^5]. Assignment rules add or remove accounts and opportunities from territories, and users are assigned to territories, which grants opportunity access that follows the account's territory. Territory-based sharing layers on top of the existing sharing model and grants additional access; it does not replace the role hierarchy for most sharing [^5].

That last sentence is the whole exam point, and it also shapes design. Territory and forecast sit beside the object model rather than inside it, so a scenario about who can see which opportunities usually combines sharing rules, the role hierarchy, and territory access, and the wrong answer is the one that claims territory replaces the other two. The planning state is the other thing to remember: a model that has not been activated grants nothing, no matter how well it was built.

### Where marketing joins in

The marketing side connects through the same objects. Account Engagement (Pardot) and Marketing Cloud feed CampaignMembers, scores, and grades into Salesforce, and those scores often drive lead routing. Einstein Lead Scoring adds a predictive score from your own conversion history, which is distinct from the marketing score and frequently confused with it on the exam [^7]. The marketing score reflects behavioral engagement; the Einstein score reflects how much a lead resembles leads that converted before. A scenario that names one of these is usually checking that you do not reach for the other.

Lead routing itself is a third decision. A scenario that says a marketing-qualified lead should route to a named rep is describing lead assignment rules driven by a status or a score, not a territory rule and not a forecast. Keep the marketing system sync, the CampaignMember status picklist, and the assignment rule as three separate steps in your head, because the exam likes to blend them, and each has a different owner in Setup.

### Reporting makes the process visible

Reporting is where the process becomes visible, and most visibility gaps are solved with configuration rather than a custom build. Reports and dashboards over Lead, Opportunity, Campaign, and Forecast objects are the standard surface, with joined reports for cross-object views and reporting snapshots for point-in-time history. The Sales Console gives reps a single workspace that shows leads, opportunities, and cadences together so they work without tab-switching. When a scenario says a manager wants daily visibility into pipeline and cadence, the answer leans on a dashboard and the console layout, not on a new object.

The judgment to carry into the exam is that visibility problems rarely need custom code. A manager who cannot see forecast category totals needs a forecast report or a configured forecast type, not an Apex component. A manager who cannot see whether cadence steps were worked needs cadence reporting. Reach for configuration first, and the answers stay inside the process you just mapped.

## Exam Traps

<Callout kind="warning">

Three traps dominate this objective. The first is assuming the forecast hierarchy is the same as the role hierarchy. The forecast hierarchy is a separate roll-up you configure, and it can follow a different chain of managers than the role structure, so a manager who is above a rep by role is not automatically a forecast manager for that rep. The second is believing campaign membership is limited to Leads. A CampaignMember can be a Lead or a Contact, and influence can attach to opportunities through the primary campaign or through contact roles, which is the mechanism behind multi-touch attribution. The third is treating a planning territory model as live. A territory model grants nothing until you move it from planning to active and run the assignment rules for accounts and opportunities, so a scenario that describes territory access that never appeared is usually describing a model that was never activated.

</Callout>


## What a consultant should walk away with

<KeyTakeaways>

- Know which object owns each stage; get lead conversion mapping and matching rules right before you automate anything downstream
- Campaigns roll up through a hierarchy, and multi-touch credit comes from a configured influence model with a time window, not a single campaign
- An opportunity holds products on a price book and quotes sync their lines back; forecast categories map from stages with a separate forecast hierarchy
- Territory adds access on top of roles only after activation, and reach for reports, dashboards, and the console layout for visibility before a custom build

</KeyTakeaways>
