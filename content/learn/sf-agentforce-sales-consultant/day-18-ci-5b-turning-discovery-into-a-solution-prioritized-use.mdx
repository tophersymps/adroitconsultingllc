# Lesson 18: Turning Discovery into a Solution: Prioritized Use-Case Backlog

The discovery readout lands well. Forty requirements are captured, every one has a source and acceptance criteria, and the steering committee nods through the session. Then the sponsor asks the question that decides whether the next six months go smoothly: so what do we build first? The consultant who has not prepared for that question reaches for the safest answer, the highest-value use case, and starts building a customer-360 view on account records the team has never audited. Four weeks later the build discovers that forty percent of the account records are duplicated and the ownership fields point at users who left two years ago. The high-value use case was never the problem. The data underneath it was, and by the time anyone found out, the team had taught the sales organization that the new platform shows numbers they cannot trust.

A backlog is where discovery stops being a conversation and becomes a plan someone can fund, and that transition has rules. The ranking has to use a framework the sponsor understands, value has to be estimated before effort, and data readiness has to gate the sequence, not trail behind it. Get those three right and the backlog becomes the document that ends scope arguments, because every item traces back to a requirement a named person owns. Get them wrong and you ship a demo that makes the organization distrust the whole program.

This lesson serves the exam guide's requirement ci-5b, in the Consulting and Implementation Strategies domain, which carries 25 percent of the exam. The objective asks you to conduct discovery workshops, analyze business requirements, define solutions, and prioritize use cases. A backlog is only a plan when someone signed it, the scoring is explicit, and every item traces back to a requirement someone owns. By the end of this lesson you will be able to pick and defend a prioritization framework, estimate value and effort in a way that survives challenge, decide what ships first when data quality is poor, and slice work so that the first thing you build proves the riskiest assumptions.

<TLDR>

- A backlog becomes a plan when the ranking is explicit and every item traces to a requirement someone owns
- Four prioritization frameworks: MoSCoW, a value and effort map, WSJF, and the Kano model
- Estimate value before effort, gate the sequence on data readiness, and slice work into a walking skeleton

</TLDR>


## Deep Dive

### Prioritization frameworks are a small toolset

There are four frameworks worth carrying, and each behaves differently under pressure, so the skill is knowing which one fits the room. MoSCoW splits items into Must, Should, Could, and Will not have.[^1] It works only when the Must list is genuinely small, because Must usually carries a contractual obligation and everything below Must is the contingency you spend when a date is threatened.[^2] A value and effort map plots items on a two by two grid so you can see the quick wins and the money pits at a glance. Weighted Shortest Job First, WSJF, divides a cost of delay by a job size so items sort by true urgency rather than by whose voice is loudest, and it conveniently ignores sunk costs.[^4] The Kano model separates basic expectations from delighters, which is useful when you are deciding what to ship in a pilot and need to know which features would go unnoticed and which would generate the goodwill you want.[^3]

Pick the framework the sponsor can use without you in the room, then say which one you used and why. A framework the client cannot apply on their own becomes a framework the client resents, because it looks like a device for hiding the ranking behind a formula. State the inputs, state the scores, and let the client re-rank with you present. The point of the exercise is a shared ranking, not a consultant's private verdict.

### Value and effort estimation decide whether the ranking is real

Score value on concrete levers rather than on a feeling: revenue affected, cost to serve removed, risk retired, and adoption gained. Each lever is something a finance or operations lead can argue about with numbers, which is what makes the score defensible. A use case that removes a manual reconciliation step has a cost-to-serve number attached. A use case that retires a compliance risk has a risk number attached. When the levers are named, two stakeholders who disagree about a ranking are disagreeing about an input, and you can settle that with evidence instead of seniority.

Score effort in points relative to work you have already delivered, never in hours. Hours invite false precision and an argument you cannot win, because the person who wants a feature will always find a reason the estimate is too high, and the person who owns the budget will always find a reason it is too low. Points anchor on the team's own history: this is about the size of the integration we shipped last quarter, so it is a five rather than an eight. The one trap that wrecks most rankings is estimating effort before anyone has assessed the data. A use case that looks like a one-week configuration job becomes a two-month project the moment you discover that the account records are forty percent duplicated and the ownership fields are wrong. Estimate value first, then check data readiness, then estimate effort. Reverse that order and you will prioritize a build that cannot start.

### MoSCoW has specific failure modes

MoSCoW is the most common framework and the most commonly misused, so its failure modes are worth naming. When twelve of twelve use cases are marked Must, the tier has stopped discriminating and the list is a wish rather than a priority. The Must threshold has become meaningless, and the fix is not to think harder; it is a forcing constraint. A real constraint, a budget, a date, or a team's capacity, is what re-ranks the list, because a constraint makes the sponsor choose instead of wish. The second failure is a Must that is contractual sitting next to a Should that is emotional, which means you ship the compliance need and disappoint the loud executive. Surface that trade-off early and in writing, so the disappointment is a decision the sponsor made rather than a surprise they suffered.

The third failure is treating the framework as final. Re-rank at each release boundary, because value shifts as the business changes and a backlog frozen in month one is a backlog no one trusts by month three. The lesson the exam draws from all three is the same: a framework is a device for forcing a conversation, not a substitute for one, and a Must list that includes everything has told you the client has not made a decision yet.

### Which use case ships first is a real trade-off

The question of what ships first is a genuine disagreement inside most steering committees, and the exam will set it up as one. One faction wants a visible quick win that buys political capital and proves the platform can deliver. The other wants the foundational work that everything else depends on. Both factions are sometimes right, and the tie-breaker is dependency and data readiness, not volume. When the target objects are dirty or unverified, the honest first ship is a data-remediation foundation slice, because a use case is only as good as the data underneath it, and a flashy pilot on bad data teaches the organization to distrust the platform. The foundation slice is less visible and harder to celebrate, but it is what makes the visible win trustworthy when it arrives.

When the data is sound and the risk is adoption rather than architecture, ship the quick win and use the goodwill it generates to fund the foundation phase that follows. The direction of the decision turns on what is actually uncertain. If the thing you do not know is whether the data can support the solution, prove the data first. If the thing you do not know is whether people will use the solution, prove adoption first. Sequencing on the uncertainty is what separates a plan from a wish list, and a pilot on clean-looking data proves nothing about the design if nobody has verified that the data is clean.

### Use cases become buildable when you slice them

A use case becomes buildable when you slice it into pieces that each prove something end to end. Group requirements into epics, break each epic into stories with acceptance criteria, and sequence them so the first slice is a walking skeleton: one vertical slice through the real stack that proves the integration and adoption risk early. A walking skeleton runs from the user action through the automation to the integration and the report, using real architecture and real data where it can, and it is deliberately thin.[^5] It is not a demo, and it is not a prototype in a sandbox with fake data. It is a thin true path through everything that could break, and it exists to expose the mismatches while they are still cheap to fix.

Choose a walking skeleton over a wide horizontal layer. A horizontal layer builds one capability across every use case, then the next, and integrates them all at the end, which is precisely when the cost of finding a mismatch is at its highest. A vertical slice front-loads the integration and the adoption questions, so the first thing the client sees is a working path rather than a promising start. When a client wants a thin end-to-end pilot before committing budget to the full build, the walking skeleton is the artifact that sequences it.

### The solution definition makes the backlog defensible

The solution definition is the artifact that turns a ranked list into an architecture someone can challenge. It carries an architecture on a page, a capability map of what the platform will and will not do, the data model with the objects and the key relationships, an integration inventory with the direction and the frequency of each integration, and the org strategy, single org or multi-org, with the reasoning stated. It also carries the licensing implications, because Agentforce, Data Cloud, and the various AI features all consume entitlements and credits, and a design that ignores the commercial model produces a solution the client cannot afford to run.

A solution definition a skeptical architect can read in ten minutes and challenge is worth more than a fifty page design document no one reads. Keep it short enough to be argued with, because a document too long to review is a document nobody reviewed, and an unreviewed design is a design that fails the first time it meets a real integration. Put the org strategy and the licensing implications near the front, since those two decisions constrain everything behind them and are the most expensive to reverse.

### Discovery exits when the backlog is signed

The discovery phase exits cleanly only when the prioritized backlog is signed by the sponsor. That signature is what converts an agreement in a meeting into a scope baseline, and it is what you point at when the first out-of-scope demand arrives in month four. Without it, the backlog is a working draft, and drafts get reopened. From there the backlog becomes the agile plan: epics into stories into sprints,[^6] with a product owner the client names and a definition of done the team writes and the client accepts.

Keep your paper through to the end. The consultant who can trace a sprint story back to the requirement, the stakeholder, and the acceptance criterion the sponsor signed is the consultant who never has to argue about scope, because the paperwork already argues for them. The signature, the explicit scoring, and the traceability are the three things that make a backlog a plan rather than a wish list, and a backlog missing any one of them is a conversation waiting to be reopened.

## Exam Traps

<Callout kind="warning">

Three traps dominate this objective. The first is believing MoSCoW is done once the tiers are filled. When everything is a Must, the threshold has become meaningless, and the fix is a forcing constraint, a real budget, date, or capacity limit that re-ranks the list, followed by a re-rank at each release boundary. Signing an undifferentiated Must list into the statement of work turns every wish into a contractual obligation, which is the opposite of what you want.

The second trap is believing a pilot on clean-looking data proves the design. Data quality is the gating factor, because a use case is only as good as the data underneath it. When the target objects are dirty or unverified, a data-remediation foundation slice usually comes before the visible win, because sequencing the highest-value use case onto unreliable data teaches the organization to distrust the platform. An answer that ships the high-value use case first regardless of data state is wrong.

The third trap is believing a horizontal layer proves each capability before integration. Nothing proves the design until a walking skeleton runs one true path through the real stack, because integrating separate layers at the end finds mismatches when they cost the most. When a client asks for a thin end-to-end pilot before committing budget, the walking skeleton, one vertical slice from user action through automation and integration to output, is the correct artifact to sequence it. A full design document, a change order, or a charter does not prove anything end to end.

</Callout>


## What a consultant should walk away with

<KeyTakeaways>

- Pick the prioritization framework the sponsor can use without you, then state which one you chose and the scoring so it can be challenged
- Estimate value on named levers first, check data readiness second, and estimate effort in points third, because reversing the last two blocks the build
- Decide what ships first on dependency and data readiness rather than volume, proving the data or the adoption that is actually uncertain
- Write a short solution definition with the org strategy and licensing up front, and leave discovery only when the sponsor signs the backlog

</KeyTakeaways>


Pick the prioritization framework the sponsor can use without you, say which one you chose, and state the scoring so it can be challenged. Estimate value on named levers first, check data readiness second, and estimate effort in points third, because reversing the last two is what makes a backlog that cannot start. When everything lands in Must, reach for a forcing constraint rather than a longer argument, and re-rank at every release boundary. Decide what ships first on dependency and data readiness rather than on volume: prove the data when data is the uncertainty, prove adoption when adoption is the uncertainty, and sequence a data-remediation slice ahead of a visible win when the records are dirty. Slice work into a walking skeleton so the first release proves the integration and adoption risk while it is cheap to fix. Write a short solution definition with the org strategy and the licensing implications up front, and do not leave discovery until the sponsor has signed the backlog. The signature plus the scoring plus the traceability is what makes a backlog a plan.
