# Lesson 34: Sales Data Quality: The Six Dimensions, Why Agentforce Inherits It, and How to Measure It

A sales director tells you the data is bad and asks you to fix it. That is not a specification, and if you start fixing before you translate it, you will spend the budget on the wrong fields. He means something specific, and you cannot know which thing until you ask. Is the problem that Amount is blank on a third of open deals, that the same account exists three times, that "SF" and "San Francisco" are two different cities in the reporting, or that the contact you have for your biggest customer left eighteen months ago? Those are four different failures, they have four different controls, and they cost four different amounts to fix.

The naive move is to open the duplicate rule page and start merging records. That treats data quality as a merge task, when duplicates are only one of six dimensions, and it skips the measurement step that tells you where to spend effort. It also misses why the customer cares now: their new Agentforce deployment is answering questions from the same CRM, and every defect in the records becomes a defect in the agent's answers. Cleanup without measurement is expensive guesswork, and cleanup without ownership decays within a quarter.

This lesson serves the exam guide's requirement dm-3a, in the Data Management domain, which carries 18 percent of the exam. The objective asks you to discuss approaches for managing sales data quality in Salesforce. By the end of this lesson you will be able to translate a vague complaint into one of six dimensions, explain why the same fields feed reps, managers, and AI, measure completeness with reports, and tell the difference between standard Field History Tracking and the licensed Field Audit Trail down to their limits.

## Deep Dive

### Data quality is six dimensions, not one

Data quality is not one property, it is six, and the exam expects you to name the right one. Completeness asks whether the field is populated at all. Accuracy asks whether the value matches reality. Consistency asks whether the same fact is stored the same way everywhere, so that SF and San Francisco are not two cities. Uniqueness asks whether one real world entity has exactly one record. Validity asks whether the value is allowed by the field definition. Timeliness asks whether the value is current, which is what a contact's email from a person who left the company violates.

When a stakeholder says the data is bad, your first move is to translate that complaint into a dimension. The dimension determines the control, and the control determines the cost. A complaint about blank amounts is completeness and the control is a validation rule. A complaint about duplicate accounts is uniqueness and the control is a matching rule paired with a duplicate rule. A complaint about "USA" and "US" and "United States" all appearing in the country field is consistency and the control is a picklist, not a report. Name the dimension before you name the tool, because an answer that reaches for a tool when the question asked for a dimension is the wrong answer.

Two of the six are easy to confuse, so pin them down. Accuracy is about truth: a phone number that is well formed but wrong fails accuracy, not validity. Validity is about the field definition: an email field that rejects a value with no at sign enforces validity, and a picklist that only allows the defined stages enforces validity too. A record can be valid and inaccurate at the same time, which is why validation rules catch typos in shape but not lies in substance, and why accuracy usually needs a human process such as a quarterly contact refresh rather than a formula. Consistency is the dimension that costs the least to fix and the most to ignore, because a single controlled country picklist can repair segmentation, territory assignment, and address validation in one change.

### Why sales data quality matters more, and why Agentforce inherits it

Sales data quality matters more than in most domains because the same fields feed three different consumers. Reps read them to work a deal, managers read them to forecast, and Einstein and Agentforce read them to score, recommend, and act. Opportunity forecasting depends on Amount, Close Date, Stage, and Forecast Category being populated and honestly maintained, so a forecast built on blank Amounts is arithmetic on nothing. Predictive scoring depends on historical outcomes, so a model trained on sloppily closed opportunities learns the sloppiness and reproduces it.

Generative agents grounded in the CRM inherit every inconsistency in it. An agent that routes a case from a duplicate Account, or quotes a customer from a stale Price Book entry, is not failing at language. It is faithfully reporting the state of the records it was pointed at. That is why the consultant's framing matters: when a customer says the agent is wrong, the honest first question is whether the underlying record is wrong, because prompt engineering does not repair a duplicate account or an expired price. Data quality is a precondition for the AI features the customer is paying for, not housekeeping that runs behind them.

### Measuring first: build completeness reports before you fix anything

Measuring quality is where most implementations skip straight to fixing, and that is the trap. Build completeness reports first. A report on Opportunities grouped by Stage with a filter for Amount equal to blank. A report on Accounts with a blank Billing Country. A report on Leads missing a Lead Source. Each one converts a vague complaint into a number with an owner, and the number is what lets you argue for the fix and prove it worked later.

The mechanics are simple and worth walking through. In Setup, open Object Manager for Opportunity and compare the fields marked required on the page layout with the fields a validation rule enforces, because the two enforce different things. Then in Reports, create a new Opportunity report, group by Stage, add a filter for Amount equal to blank, and save it into a Data Quality report folder. Add the Account and Lead reports the same way, then schedule a subscription so the numbers arrive weekly without anyone remembering to run them. What you have built is a measurement loop, and it is the difference between a cleanup project and a quality program.

Deciding which fields are business critical is the scoping decision that saves you from boiling the ocean. Pick the fields the forecast, the territory assignment, and the agent's answers actually read: Amount, Close Date, Stage, and Forecast Category on Opportunity; Account Name, Owner, and Billing Country on Account; Lead Source and Status on Lead; Email and Phone on Contact. Those are the fields you make required, validate, and report on first. Everything else waits for the next cycle, because trying to fix all of them at once is how a quality program stalls before it delivers its first number.

### Salesforce Optimizer and the field usage review

Salesforce Optimizer runs a platform scan and reports unused fields, fields that are populated almost nowhere, and objects consuming storage, which is the fastest way to find fields the business has already abandoned. The catch is that Optimizer is being retired: Salesforce is retiring the Optimizer app with the Winter '26 release for organizations on Hyperforce, and other organizations lose access by Summer '26, with Lightning Experience Insights named as where development effort moves.[^5] So teach it as a tool with a shelf life, and check an org's current access before you build a process around it.

The durable habit is the field usage review, however you run the scan. Once a quarter, look at which custom fields are populated almost nowhere, ask who asked for each one, and retire the ones whose requester has moved on. A field nobody populates still costs you on every page layout, every report list, and every integration mapping, and it accumulates the same way a duplicate does: quietly, one request at a time. Retiring fields is a data quality control, not a clean-up afterthought.

### Field History Tracking and Field Audit Trail, to the exact limits

Field history is how you prove who changed what, and the two tools that provide it have specific, testable limits. Standard Field History Tracking covers up to 20 fields per object, and Salesforce retains that history for up to 18 months, or up to 24 months through the API. It stores the history on the object's own history object, such as AccountHistory or MyCustomObject__History, and it is readable both in the UI and through the API.[^1][^2]

Field Audit Trail is the licensed extension that raises the ceiling. It covers up to 200 fields per object, retains the data until you delete it, copies the history to the FieldHistoryArchive big object, and is access through the API only.[^2] It requires the purchase of a Salesforce Shield or Field Audit Trail license, on Enterprise, Performance, or Unlimited editions.[^2] By default Salesforce archives data after 18 months in production, and the retention policy itself is defined and deployed through the Metadata API.[^2]

Both tools share a set of field types they cannot track, and you should know this list before you promise a customer an audit trail. Neither can track formula fields, roll-up summary fields, or auto-number fields. Neither can track long text fields or multiselect fields. And none of them can track the Expected Revenue field on Opportunity.[^2] If a compliance requirement lands on one of those, the audit trail cannot be built on the field as it stands, and the fix is a different field or a different mechanism, so surface it in discovery rather than after go-live. The compliance answer usually pairs the two tools: standard tracking for the near term question of who changed a value six months ago, and Field Audit Trail for a multi year retention policy.

### Where bad data enters: five doors to guard

Where bad data enters determines what you can do about it, and there are five distinct doors. Manual entry is governed by page layout required fields, validation rules, and picklists. Bulk import is governed by the tool: the Data Import Wizard enforces duplicate rules and is the tool Salesforce recommends when the goal is to prevent duplicates, while Data Loader is designed for volume and complex mappings and does not police duplicates the way the wizard does.[^6] Integration and Web to Lead bypass the UI entirely, so the control must live on the object rather than on the page. Lead conversion creates Account, Contact, and optionally Opportunity records in one action, so a poorly governed Lead becomes three poorly governed records. And external sync tools such as Einstein Activity Capture write activity data on their own schedule and their own matching rules, so they can create records the sales team never entered.

Web to Lead deserves a line of its own because it is the door people forget. A form on the company website writes a Lead directly, and it does not run the UI's required fields or the page's picklist defaults, so a Lead that arrives through Web to Lead can be born incomplete. The control belongs on the object, in a validation rule or a universally required field definition, not on a page layout the Lead never sees.

The rule that ties this together: for every control you describe, name the entry path it covers. A validation rule covers every save path. A page layout required field covers the UI and nothing else. A duplicate rule covers the UI and API save paths but not a Data Loader run unless that checking is turned on. An answer that names a control without naming the path it protects is incomplete, because the exam builds scenarios around exactly those gaps.

### The business cost, and the operating model that keeps it fixed

The business cost of each dimension failure is specific, and that is how you justify the spend. Duplicates split the relationship history so no one sees the whole account, inflate pipeline counts, and cause territory and quota disputes. Incomplete Amount and Close Date fields destroy forecast reliability. Inconsistent country and industry values break segmentation, territory assignment, and campaign attribution. Stale contact data means sequences and cadences fire at people who left the company. For a customer deploying Agentforce, each of these becomes a visible agent failure.

Measurement without ownership decays within a quarter, so the last piece is the operating model. Name a data steward per object or per domain. Agree which fields are business critical. Publish the definitions in a data dictionary. Put a cadence behind the review: a weekly exception report, a monthly duplicate sweep, a quarterly field usage review that retires abandoned fields. Then tie the quality metrics to the same success metrics agreed before the implementation, so quality is measured in forecast accuracy and adoption rather than in record counts. The exam tests the pairing, not just the tooling: every control has a monitoring counterpart, and every metric has an owner.

## Exam Traps

Three traps dominate this objective. The first is naming a tool when the question asked for a dimension. A complaint about duplicate Accounts is a uniqueness failure, not a duplicate rule failure, and the exam often wants the dimension identified before the control. Read the question for the words "which dimension," and answer completeness, accuracy, consistency, uniqueness, validity, or timeliness before you reach for a feature.

The second trap is assuming page layout required fields protect the data. A required field on a page layout is enforced only in the UI, so Data Loader, the API, and some integrations write records with that field blank. The answer that relies on the layout alone is wrong; the control that holds is a validation rule, which applies to new and updated records regardless of page layout, or a universally required field on the field definition itself.[^3][^4]

The third trap is treating data quality as a one time cleanup. The exam favors the answer that pairs a control with ongoing monitoring and a named owner, because an unmonitored cleanup decays. When a scenario asks for an approach, it is usually testing the operating model: the report that runs weekly, the steward who owns the object, the quarterly field review, and the success metric the work rolls up to.

## What a consultant should walk away with

Translate every data complaint into one of the six dimensions before naming a tool, because the dimension picks the control and the control picks the cost. Remember that the same fields feed reps, managers, and the AI features, so an agent failure is usually a data failure wearing an AI costume, and fix the record rather than the prompt. Measure before you fix: completeness reports grouped by stage, blank country on accounts, missing lead source, subscribed so the number arrives without anyone asking. Know the audit limits cold: standard Field History Tracking covers 20 fields per object for 18 to 24 months, Field Audit Trail covers 200 fields per object and retains until deleted, is API only, and needs a Shield or Field Audit Trail license, and neither tracks formula, roll-up summary, or auto-number fields, long text, multiselect, or Expected Revenue on Opportunity. Guard all five entry doors, name the path every control covers, and put a steward and a cadence behind the whole thing, because a cleanup with no owner is a cleanup you will be doing again next year.
