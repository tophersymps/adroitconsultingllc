# Lesson 29: When to Unify: Use Cases, Identity Sprawl Risk, Consent, and Segmentation and Activation

A loyalty team wants its consolidation rate to look impressive before a leadership review, so someone loosens the match criteria. The number climbs. Two weeks later a customer receives an offer addressed to her husband, under his name, at her email, and the retention team spends a weekend on a privacy incident instead of a campaign. The rules did exactly what they were told. The team chased a metric instead of a customer outcome.

That incident is the shape of requirement hu-1d. Part of the Harmonization and Unification domain worth 17% of the Salesforce Certified Data 360 Consultant exam, hu-1d asks you to define the purpose, process, and supported use cases of unification, covering which use cases justify unification and the privacy and governance tradeoffs a consultant must weigh. The previous lesson covered the mechanics of match and reconciliation rules. This lesson covers the judgment: when to turn unification on, when to refuse, and what you owe the customer once you do.

The unit of the decision is a use case, not a feature. Unification earns its cost when one customer view has to be assembled across sources and something downstream depends on that single view. It earns nothing when a single system already holds one record per person. The lessons here are the ones that separate a consultant from an administrator: naming the use case before promising the capability, and treating consent as a design input rather than a checkbox at the end.

## Deep Dive

### Four families of use case justify unification

Unification exists to serve four families of use case, and a consultant should be able to place any request into one of them before answering.

The first is segmentation. A segment should count one member per person, not one per source record. If the business offers the same customer two email addresses across two systems, an un-unified segment sees two people and sends two messages. Segmentation is the most common reason to unify, and it is why the platform recommends a specific segment target once identity resolution is in play.

The second is activation. Activation publishes one row per unified individual with its contact points, so an audience built on a unified profile goes out once per person rather than once per record. Identity resolution is what makes that de-duplication possible at the point of send.

The third is personalization and agent experiences. Next best action, real time personalization, and agent-assisted journeys all want the complete customer in front of them. A partial view produces a partial answer, and an agent told only half the story gives the customer advice that does not fit.

The fourth is analytics. Calculated insights, data graphs, and metrics join on unified keys, so a metric built on the unified layer counts people rather than records. The same calculated insight run over raw source rows will inflate or double, because it is counting the same human more than once.

The pattern is simple to apply. If the customer's stated goal is any of these four across multiple sources, unification is in scope. If the data comes from a single system, unification adds cost with little benefit, because that system already holds one record per customer.

### Unification is not free, and identity sprawl is the cost of doing it badly

Unification consumes credits, adds processing, and creates governance obligations. That price buys a cross source view, so it is worth paying only when a cross source view is what the business actually needs.

There are three clear cases to refuse or defer. Do not unify when the sources describe different domains of entities, because matching a person against an organization against a product produces nonsense rather than a customer. Do not unify when the business does not need a cross source view, because the benefit is the view and there is no other payoff. Do not unify when matching would be unreliable, because an unreliable unified profile is worse than no unified profile: it looks authoritative and is wrong.

The risk of over unifying has a name. Identity sprawl is what happens when loose criteria collapse distinct people into one profile and blend one customer's data into another's, harming personalization, consent, and trust. A shared household email, a recycled phone number, or a common name with a low precision fuzzy match can pull two humans into one profile, and every downstream insight, segment, and activation then speaks to the wrong person.

The tradeoff is explicit and worth saying out loud to a stakeholder. Stricter rules with more criteria reduce false merges but raise duplicate counts, while looser rules merge more but risk joining people who are not the same. There is no setting that eliminates both problems at once. Your job is to choose which failure the business can live with, and to make that choice on purpose.

### Consent and data spaces are design inputs, not afterthoughts

Unification inherits every source's consent and privacy posture, so the consultant must confirm the sources are permitted to be combined for the intended purpose. This is not a legal nicety appended to the end of the build. It is a constraint that shapes which sources go into the ruleset in the first place.

The reason is that a unified profile aggregates data across systems, and combining data can create inferences the customer never consented to. Combining data across sources can create inferences and uses beyond the original consent, so permitted purposes and data spaces must be confirmed. A customer who agreed to a purchase receipt did not thereby agree to be profiled alongside their support history and web behavior, and the act of joining those sources is itself a new use.

Data spaces give you a governance boundary to work inside. A data space is a logical partition that organizes your data for profile unification, insights, and marketing, letting you segregate data, metadata, and processes by categories such as brand, region, or department and control user access through permission sets.[^1] You can partition by brand, by region, or by regulatory zone and keep unification for one market from touching another's data. When a customer data program spans markets with different rules, that partition is how you keep each market inside its permitted scope.

One honest caveat belongs in every design conversation: data spaces control what a user can see and work on, not where data physically lives, so they are not a data residency or geographic storage mechanism. Use them to keep work inside a permitted boundary, and do not promise residency from them.

The last point is the one consultants forget. Consent management is a design step, not something unification infers for you. The matching engine does not read your consent records and decide what may be combined. You configure the sources, the data spaces, and the consent model as deliberate choices, and you treat the consent posture as configuration rather than a default the platform will get right on its own.

### Contact points stay on the profile, so reach and risk arrive together

Reconciliation does not apply to contact points, so every email and phone number collected across sources stays on the unified profile. That single behavior is both the power and the hazard of unification. The power is reach: a person whose email you only learned in one system is still addressable when a segment is built on the unified profile. The hazard is consent, because a contact point collected under one purpose can be surfaced for another. Combining data across sources can create inferences and uses beyond the original consent, so permitted purposes and data spaces must be confirmed.

The control for this at send time is source priority in activation. When contact points come from multiple sources, Data 360 selects the contact points you prioritized in source selection, and if a single source has several contact points it picks the one with the highest engagement score, falling back to the lowest alphanumeric ID when scores are absent or tied.[^2] Use source priority in activations to deliver the contact point from the intended source, and confirm the consent and lawful basis for the channel and region before the audience goes out. When customer data spans markets with different rules, that is a reason to segment not just matching but activation as well, because the channel and the consent that governs it vary from market to market.

### Downstream, membership choice decides whether you count people or records

The mechanics of unification show up most visibly in segment counts. A segment that uses the Unified Individual data model object as its membership counts one row per unified profile rather than one per source record. Choosing the source Individual object instead can produce duplicate entries in the count, because the segment then counts records rather than people. The platform guidance is direct: segmenting on the Individual instead of the Unified Individual can result in duplicate or skipped entities in your count.[^3]

When a customer is activated, Data 360 sends one row per unified individual and selects contact points according to the source priorities you set, preferring higher engagement scores and lower IDs as tiebreakers within a source.[^2] This is why ruleset quality is an operational concern and not just a data quality one. The ruleset decides who receives a message and how many messages go out, so a change to the ruleset is a change to the customer experience and to the sends budget.

The recommended membership is not optional advice for a multi-source org. To take advantage of identity resolution, use the Unified Individual data model object as the activation membership, and if you have data from multiple data sources, selecting Individual instead of Unified Individual can result in duplicate entries in your count.[^2] The activation membership that avoids duplicate counts for a unified customer is The Unified Individual data model object.[^2] Reach for anything else only when you have a specific reason and you understand the counting consequence.

### A weak ruleset fails quietly

The habit that separates a reliable unification program from a risky one is measurement, because a weak ruleset does not fail loudly. It quietly produces the wrong customer.

Too loose, and you merge different people. One person receives another's offer, and you have a privacy incident and a broken trust relationship. Too strict, and you leave duplicates, which inflates segment counts, doubles sends, and skews metrics and lifetime value. Because every insight, graph, segment, and activation sits on top of the unified layer, one matching mistake propagates everywhere at once. The profiling error you cannot see at the ruleset page reappears as a wrong number in a board deck.

The habit to build is a loop, not a setting. Publish, measure the consolidation rate, sample unified profiles for false merges, and iterate before the output reaches a customer. Salesforce frames the review the same way: after creating a ruleset, verify that summary statistics and unified profiles make sense against what you know about how clean your source data is, and review the consolidation rate in the Identity Resolution Summary or the Identity Resolution Tableau Dashboard.[^4] Random unified profiles reviewed in Profile Explorer are how you catch the false merge before a customer does.

Here is the walkthrough for deciding and then doing it.

1. Start from the use case: identify which of segmentation, activation, personalization, or analytics needs one record per customer across sources.
2. Confirm every contributing data stream is mapped to the required Customer 360 Data Model objects so identity resolution can match on them.
3. In Data 360, open Identity Resolutions, tune the criteria and reconciliation rules, then publish and record the consolidation rate before trusting the output.
4. Assign the relevant data spaces so unification and its output stay inside the permitted brand, region, or regulatory boundary.
5. When building segments, select the Unified Individual data model object as the activation membership rather than the source Individual object.
6. Verify in an activation which contact points and source priorities are used, and confirm consent for each channel and region.

### Try it

Before your next unification conversation, write one sentence that names the cross source use case and the decision it drives: which segment, which activation, which insight, and which source records have to combine to produce it. If the sentence will not come together, the business probably does not have a unification use case yet, and the right answer is to say so rather than build a ruleset that costs credits and creates obligations for no return.

## Exam Traps

Three traps dominate this objective.

The first is unifying by default. Without a cross source use case, unification adds credit cost, processing, and governance burden for no benefit the business asked for. The alluring wrong answer is to unify now so that future sources match more easily. That is speculative value bought with present cost, and it is the wrong default. So the guidance to give a customer who ingests from a single system is that unification is not justified without a cross source use case, since it adds cost and governance burden for little benefit. A single source already holds one record per customer, so there is nothing to assemble.

The second is chasing a high consolidation rate as a goal. Looser criteria merge different people, which causes misdirected offers, privacy incidents, and unreliable personalization. The rate is a diagnostic, not a target. When a stakeholder asks to raise it, translate the request into the customer outcome at stake: more matches means more chances to merge two humans who are not the same person, and the review meeting is the place to price that risk.

The third is using the source Individual data model object as activation membership. It can produce duplicate entries in counts, so Unified Individual is the recommended membership.[^2] The wrong answers here point at raw data lake objects or contact point objects, which are not memberships that represent the unified customer at all. The membership is the object that stands for one person, and after identity resolution runs, that object is Unified Individual.

## The decision rules

Carry four rules into the next client conversation. First, name the use case before promising the capability: unification is justified by segmentation, activation, personalization, or analytics across sources, and it is not justified by a single source that already holds one record per person. Second, treat the consolidation rate as a diagnostic and tune against false merges, remembering that identity sprawl and the privacy incident it causes cost far more than a duplicate count. Third, confirm consent and permitted purpose and assign data spaces as boundaries before you combine anything, because the combination itself can create uses the customer never agreed to. Fourth, build segments and activations on Unified Individual so you count people, and verify source priority and channel consent at the activation so the right contact point reaches the right person.
