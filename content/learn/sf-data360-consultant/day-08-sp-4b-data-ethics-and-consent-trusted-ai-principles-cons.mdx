# Lesson 8: Data Ethics and Consent: Trusted AI Principles, Consent Management, and Data Subject Rights

A marketing team celebrates a high-performing activation. The segment is clean, the audience is large, the campaign lands. Two weeks later a customer emails to say they opted out of email months ago, and the company sent them another one anyway. The team checks the destination platform: consent was configured there, and it worked for everything that ran through the platform's own send path. The activation never went through that path. Nobody owned the check at the Data 360 boundary, so nobody made it.

That is the failure this lesson prevents. Requirement sp-4b sits in the Solution Positioning domain, worth 14% of the Salesforce Certified Data 360 Consultant exam, and it asks you to describe the principles of data ethics and governance, covering ethical practice: the Trusted AI principles, consent management and enforcement at activation, and data subject rights. The exam wants you to know that consent in Data 360 is a data model concern first and a channel concern second, and that data subject rights are fulfilled through the platform's own mechanisms rather than by hand.

For a consultant, ethics is not a document you attach at the end of a project. It is a set of design decisions that shape the solution from ingestion onward: what you collect, why you collect it, how consent travels with it, and what happens when someone asks you to delete it. Get those decisions right and compliance follows. Treat them as a final checklist and you will ship the leak from the opening paragraph.

## Deep Dive

### Data ethics at Salesforce is expressed as the Trusted AI principles

Data ethics at Salesforce is expressed as Trusted AI principles, and the exam expects you to recognize them cold. The five principles are Accuracy, Safety, Transparency, Empowerment, Sustainability. Some official pages render the third principle as Honesty, others as Transparency, and both framings describe the same commitment to telling people clearly when and how AI is involved. Learn the five as Accuracy, Safety, Transparency, Empowerment, Sustainability, because that is the framing this course tests, and treat the Honesty variant as a wording difference rather than a sixth principle.

There is also a shorter internal framing that names the values rather than the outcomes: Responsible, Accountable, Transparent, Empowering, and Inclusive. If a question offers that list, it is pointing at the same ethical intent with different labels, so do not reject it for using a word you did not expect. The five that matter for the exam answer are Accuracy, Safety, Transparency, Empowerment, Sustainability.

The principles are not decorative. Salesforce runs an Office of Ethical and Humane Use, and its guidance expects AI disclosure so people know when they are interacting with AI, and qualified human review for high-impact decisions. For a consultant, that translates into being transparent about when AI is used and keeping a human in the loop where the stakes are high. Concretely: disclose in the interface that a response was AI generated, and route decisions that materially affect a person, an approval, a denial, a ranking, through a human checkpoint rather than straight to automation. The Trust Layer documentation states the same expectation from the product side, recommending that a human check each model response before sharing it with end users for the majority of use cases.[^1]

The exam tests the list itself, so anchor it. When a question asks which list best reflects Salesforce's Trusted AI principles, the answer is Accuracy, Safety, Transparency, Empowerment, Sustainability. Speed, Scale, Cost, Coverage is a generic operating list, not an ethics framework. Unification, Activation, Segmentation, Insights is the shape of the Data 360 pipeline. Zero Copy, Data Cloud One, Data Spaces, Credits is a list of product features and entitlements. Only the second option names the principles.

### Consent is a data model concern, not just a marketing preference

Consent is a data model concern, not just a marketing preference that lives in whatever channel happens to send the message. The platform makes this concrete through the Salesforce Consent Data Model, which is the standard model for managing consent at multiple levels, from global preferences to granular controls, across an individual's entire experience rather than a single contact point.[^2] Because consent is modeled as data, it can be captured and stored in Data 360, which reduces integration cost and lets consent travel with the data fed into downstream tools.

The model has layers, and knowing them helps you answer design questions. Global consent is managed on the Individual object and captures all-or-nothing privacy settings, including fields such as Don't Process, Don't Profile, Don't Solicit, Don't Track, and OK to Store PII Data Elsewhere.[^2] Engagement channel consent sits on the ContactPointTypeConsent object, so a customer can say "email yes, phone no." Contact point consent sits on the ContactPointConsent object, letting a customer approve a work email but not a personal one. And Data Use Purpose captures consent based on the reason for the communication, such as a recall notice versus a weekly newsletter.[^2] Consent preferences can be ingested from where they already live, using connectors for data extensions, standard or custom CRM objects, or external sources, so you do not have to rebuild a consent store from scratch.[^3]

The part that changes behavior is enforcement at activation. Consent-aware activation evaluates flags such as Do Not Sell or Email Opt-Out and excludes non-consented records before sharing, so only eligible data reaches an external destination. This is the mechanism that closes the opening failure: the check happens at the Data 360 boundary, on the way out, rather than being left to the channel to catch. The temptations are all wrong for the same reason. Saying Consent is enforced only in the destination channel misses platform-level enforcement, which is exactly the gap that produced the unsolicited email. Saying consent cannot be enforced at activation is simply false, and saying Consent is enforced by deleting the segment confuses a data-eligibility rule with a segment edit. Consent-aware activation evaluates flags such as Do Not Sell or Email Opt-Out and excludes non-consented records before sharing.

One real-world subtlety is worth keeping in your head for design work, even though it is not the exam answer. When a segment is built on the Unified Individual and activated on that unified profile, Data 360 can use source order and engagement history to select contact points regardless of consent or preference type, which puts the responsibility for honoring consent on the design.[^4] That is an argument for modeling consent carefully and testing it, which is what the next sections cover.

### Data subject rights are the individual's controls over their personal data

Data subject rights are the individual's controls over their personal data, and regulations such as GDPR and CCPA grant them. In Data 360 these include Data Deletion or the Right to be Forgotten, Restriction of Processing, and Data Access and Export, and all data subject rights requests are submitted using the Salesforce Consent API.[^5] The Consent API is the single submission path. It is not a back office screen and it is not a manual deletion routine.

There is a subtle exam point hiding in the identity model. You can submit data subject rights requests for individual profiles in Data 360, and when identity resolution is in use, you submit requests for the source individual profiles, because those are the records that feed the unified profile.[^5] Picture a customer whose unified profile was built from two source profiles, each with its own Individual ID. A deletion request against the unified profile alone leaves both source profiles intact. You target the sources, and you target each one.

That is why the recommended way to answer a GDPR right-to-erasure scenario is to Submit the data subject rights request through the Salesforce Consent API. Every tempting alternative fails on scope. Deleting the record manually in CRM only touches one system and misses the Data 360 data entirely. Setting the record's consent flag to opt-out is a preference change, not an erasure, and it does not remove anything. Excluding the customer from all segments hides them from marketing without touching the personal data itself. Only the Consent API request reaches both CRM and Data 360 and honors the source-profile rule.

The three rights map to three distinct obligations, and mixing them up is a common design error. Data Deletion, or the Right to be Forgotten, is a request to remove or delete personal data. Restriction of Processing is a request to stop processing data without necessarily deleting it, which is where flags such as the restriction-of-processing flag come in. Data Access and Export is a request to provide the individual their own data in usable form.[^5] Each is a different operation, and each is submitted through the Consent API.

### Privacy tooling operationalizes those rights

Privacy tooling operationalizes those rights so the obligations are enforceable rather than aspirational. Privacy Center lets an org create policies, manage requests, and monitor the status and results of jobs.[^6] Two policy families do most of the work. Right to Be Forgotten policies capture the objects you want to delete or make anonymous and run on individual customer records, and Data Management policies automate how data is deleted, masked, or retained across its lifecycle.[^6] Where raw deletion would break legitimate processing, anonymization and pseudonymization are the preferred tools, which is why the same tooling offers masking and retention alongside deletion rather than only erasure.

Privacy Center also gives you a single system of record for consent through Global Consent Manager, which tracks consent across your marketing and data processing activities and feeds the Consent Data Model, so consent can restrict processing, for example constraining what segmentation is allowed to do.[^6] That is the operational bridge between the model from the previous section and the enforcement from this one: consent captured in one place, respected across the estate. The platform documents the build order for a privacy program, and it is worth walking through.

### Ethics as a design principle, not a final checklist

For a consultant, ethics is a design principle rather than a final checklist, and three principles shape the design. Data minimization means collecting and activating only what the purpose requires, so a segment for a warranty reminder does not need browsing history. Purpose limitation means using data for the reason it was collected, so consent captured for service messages is not silently repurposed for marketing. Transparency means telling people what happens and why, which is where disclosure and human review return.

The platform backs transparency with an audit trail. The Einstein Trust Layer logs prompts, responses, and trust signals in Data 360, so an organization can show what the AI did and verify the safety of a generated response.[^7] Lineage and consent awareness built into Data 360 objects keep the organization accountable from ingestion through activation, because the controls attach to metadata that follows the data rather than to a single report at the end of the line.

### The Trust Layer is ethics made concrete in the platform

The Trust Layer is ethics made concrete in the platform. The Einstein Trust Layer is a collection of features, processes, and policies designed to safeguard data privacy, enhance AI accuracy, and promote responsible use of AI, and it operates on the journey a prompt takes out to a model and the response that comes back.[^7]

It does four things that a consultant should be able to name. It enforces zero-data retention, meaning agreements with external model providers such as OpenAI or Azure OpenAI commit that data sent from Salesforce is not retained and is deleted after a response returns, so third-party models do not train on or retain your data.[^7] It grounds prompts with secure retrieval that respects permissions and field-level security, so the prompt is grounded only with data the executing user can already access.[^7] It masks sensitive data before it reaches the model, detecting it through pattern-based and field-based methods and replacing it with placeholders, then demasking the response on the way back.[^7] And it defends against harmful prompts and flags toxic responses, using system policies to reduce the chance of an unintended or harmful generation.[^7]

Permission-aware grounding retrieves only data the executing user can access. Masking replaces sensitive data with placeholders and demasks the response. Layered on top of the mechanics are two expectations that tell you how to justify AI to a skeptical stakeholder. The first is the principle that the customer's data belongs to the customer, which is the plain-language meaning of zero-data retention and the disclosure commitments. The second is the expectation of model cards and explainability, so a stakeholder can understand what a model does, what it was built on, and where its limits are before it is put in front of a customer. When a skeptical executive asks why the AI can be trusted, the answer is the combination: zero retention, permission-aware grounding, masking, toxicity detection, and an audit trail you can inspect.

## Config Walkthrough

Standing up consent-aware ethics in Data 360 follows five steps. Work them in order.

1. Confirm the Salesforce Consent Data Model objects are in place and that consent records are captured and stored in Data 360. Ingest consent preferences from their existing location using connectors rather than rebuilding them, so the Individual, ContactPointTypeConsent, ContactPointConsent, and DataUsePurpose records are populated.[^3]

2. Identify the consent flags your activations must honor, such as Do Not Sell or Email Opt-Out, then verify that non-consented records are excluded from an outbound dataset. This is the enforcement step, and it happens at activation, before the data leaves Data 360.[^3]

3. Configure a Right to be Forgotten or Data Management policy in Privacy Center that defines which objects are deleted, masked, or anonymized. RTBF policies capture the objects to delete or make anonymous and run against individual customer records; Data Management policies automate deletion, masking, and retention.[^6]

4. Submit a data subject rights request through the Salesforce Consent API and monitor it, remembering to target source individual profiles when identity resolution is in use. Requests must be submitted and processed separately in each connected cloud.[^5]

5. Verify enforcement end to end by re-running the affected activation or query and confirming the record is no longer included or is masked. A governance control you have not tested against the live path is an assumption, not a control.

## Exam Traps

Three traps dominate this objective.

First, assuming consent is enforced only in the destination channel. Data 360 enforces consent at activation through consent-aware flows, so the channel is not the only control point, and treating it as the only one is how the opening failure happens. When a scenario asks how Data 360 enforces an opt-out, the answer is that Consent-aware activation evaluates flags such as Do Not Sell or Email Opt-Out and excludes non-consented records before sharing.

Second, assuming Right to be Forgotten deletes CRM records only. The request must cover Data 360 as well, and when identity resolution is in use it targets the source individual profiles. The correct move is to Submit the data subject rights request through the Salesforce Consent API, because only that path reaches every system and honors the source-profile rule.

Third, confusing the Salesforce Consent API with Privacy Center, or treating ethics as an optional nicety rather than a design principle that shapes the solution from the start. The Consent API is the submission path for data subject rights requests; Privacy Center is where you define and run the deletion, masking, and retention policies those requests depend on. They are partners, not substitutes, and ethics that arrives as a final checklist arrives too late to change the design.

## The decision rules to carry out

Walk away with four rules. Consent is data, so model it in the Consent Data Model and let it travel with the data rather than living in a channel. Consent is enforced at activation through consent-aware flows that exclude non-consented records before sharing, not only at the destination. Data subject rights requests go through the Salesforce Consent API and cover both CRM and Data 360, and where identity resolution is in use they target the source individual profiles. And the Trusted AI principles, Accuracy, Safety, Transparency, Empowerment, Sustainability, are a design input: disclose when AI is used, keep a human in the loop for high-impact decisions, and rely on the Trust Layer for zero retention, permission-aware grounding, masking, and an audit trail.
