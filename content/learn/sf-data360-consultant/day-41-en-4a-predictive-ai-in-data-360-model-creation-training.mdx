# Lesson 41: Predictive AI in Data 360: Model Creation, Training, Scoring, and Activation

A team trains a churn model in AI Models, watches the training metrics come back strong, and waits for the business to change. Nothing does. The model is trained, the metrics look fine, and no marketing segment can filter on churn risk because there is no field to filter on. The model exists; its predictions do not. That gap between a trained model and an actionable one is the failure this lesson exists to prevent.

The requirement behind the lesson is en-4a, in the Data Enhancements, Sharing, and Analysis domain worth 18% of the Salesforce Data 360 Consultant exam. It asks you to apply the predictive and generative AI tooling capabilities of Data 360 to customer scenarios, focusing on creating and training predictive models, scoring with them, and turning predictions into usable segments and activations. The exam wants the chain, not the checkbox: train, score, then consume.

This lesson covers AI Models as the home base for AI in Data 360, the clicks path to create a predictive model from scratch, how training and evaluation metrics work, how predictions get written onto an object, and how those predictions become ordinary customer attributes a segment can use. The thread through all of it is placement: a prediction is only useful where something downstream can read it.

## Deep Dive

### AI Models is the home base for AI in Data 360

AI Models, formerly Einstein Studio, is the home base for AI in Data 360 and appears as a tab in the Data 360 app. It sits under the Einstein Trust Layer, so models created there inherit the same governance as other Salesforce AI. AI Models offers three ways to work: create a predictive AI model from scratch using clicks rather than code, bring your own model built in an external platform such as Amazon SageMaker, Google Cloud Vertex AI, or Databricks, and work with generative models. A consultant frames the choice around who owns the model and where the predictions must be consumed.[^1]

The bring your own model path is the one the exam likes to test, because it is the path people assume does not exist. The quiz describes a customer who wants to use a model built in Databricks alongside models built natively in Data 360 and asks what AI Models provides. The answer is A bring your own model path that connects predictive models from external platforms such as Databricks so their predictions can be consumed across Salesforce. You connect the external model through AI Models, define its inputs and outputs, and activate it so its predictions can be consumed just like a native model's. The wrong options describe paths the platform does not use: there is no native only restriction, you do not copy the model into a CRM custom object, and a prompt template does not rebuild the model inside Data 360.[^5]

For the native path, the decision is about control. Creating a model from scratch is a clicks, not code experience, which means a consultant or admin can build, train, and evaluate a model without a data science team. Bringing a model means the data science team owns the model lifecycle and Salesforce consumes the output. Both are legitimate. What matters is that you can say who owns training and where the predictions will be read.

### Creating a predictive model from scratch

To create a predictive model from scratch, open AI Models, choose to add a predictive model, and select create a model from scratch. Select the data the model should learn from, which is a data model object or a training dataset prepared from Data 360 data. Choose the field the model should predict and whether to maximize or minimize it, for example maximizing a conversion flag. Then select the attributes used as training features, removing fields with no predictive value. You can let the platform pick the best algorithm for the data, or select one, and you can disable Autopilot and drive the choices yourself.[^1]

Data preparation is the first and quietest step. In its first release, the model builder trains on a single data model object, so the DMO that supplies training data has to be populated and shaped before you build anything. Effective preparation means loading and transforming data from one or more sources into a DMO whose fields carry the signal the model needs. If the DMO is empty or malformed, the model inherits the problem, and no algorithm choice rescues it.[^1]

The outcome field and the feature list are where the business thinking lives. The outcome field is the goal of the model, the thing you want to predict. The features are the inputs the model analyzes to make its prediction.[^3] A consultant picks an outcome that expresses a real business result, like whether a lead converted or a subscription renewed, and picks features that were knowable before that outcome happened. Removing features with no predictive value is not an optimization, it is hygiene: a feature that adds noise makes the model worse, not richer.

Autopilot and algorithm choice are a dial, not a gate. Autopilot lets the platform choose the best algorithm for the data, and you can disable it and drive the choices yourself when you have a reason. In a hands-on exercise, the model builder walks you through selecting the training data, setting the field to predict and whether to maximize or minimize it, choosing the training attributes, and then clicking Save and Train. The platform suggests an algorithm, and you can accept it or override it.[^2]

### Training runs after you save, then you evaluate it

Training runs after you save and train the model, and it can take a substantial time, up to roughly a day, depending on the dataset. Predictive models can take up to 24 hours to train, and a small dataset might finish in twenty minutes.[^2] When training completes, review the training metrics before using the model. You access them from the model with a View Training Metrics action. Never activate a model on a hunch; assess it with metrics and a prediction job first.

For a classification model, the AUC score rates how well the model separates the positive and negative classes, from a score of about zero point five for random guessing toward one for a perfect separator, and the ROC curve plots the true positive rate against the false positive rate across thresholds. The quiz tests exactly this, and its true statement is that The AUC score measures how well the model separates the positive and negative classes, and the ROC curve plots the true positive rate against the false positive rate. Higher AUC is better, not lower. The metrics are viewable after training, and they apply to predictive classification models, not only to generative ones.[^3]

The accuracy signal is a three way judgment, and the middle is the goal. Einstein uses area under the curve for binary classification and r-squared for regressions, and it tells you whether overall accuracy is performant, too low, or too high.[^3] Performant means the model is generally accurate and is what you want. Too low means the model is not much better than random guessing. Too high means the model is perfect or nearly perfect, which is a warning sign, not a win, because it usually indicates data leakage or overfitting. A model that looks flawless on training data is a model that learned the answer key instead of the pattern.

Two other training views are worth knowing by name. The top predictors are the inputs that have the greatest impact on predicting the outcome, which is how you explain a model to a stakeholder. The variable distribution is the spread of actual values for the outcome variable, which is how you check that the training data is representative rather than skewed.[^3]

### Predictions are produced by running the model

Predictions are produced by running the model, not by the model existing. A predict job enables an active model to write predictive outputs to a data model object, and you create a streaming or batch job in the model builder.[^4] Batch data transforms can also write the model's predictions onto a Data 360 object, so the scored rows become queryable like any other data model object. Flow Builder actions can consume predictions at run time. Activating a model is what enables consumption across Salesforce.

The quiz turns on this gap. A team has trained a predictive model in AI Models, but no segment can filter on the prediction, and the question asks for the missing step. The answer is Run a prediction job or a batch data transform to write the predictions to a Data 360 object, then segment on that field. Training produces the model; the predictions must be generated and written before any segment can filter on them. Retraining changes the model rather than where its output lives, the trust layer is not something to remove, and a calculated insight is a different Data 360 object.

The mechanics of a predict job are worth walking. In AI Models you click a model to view its details, then from the Integrations tab you create a new predict job. In the predict job builder you select the data space and the DMO where the outputs are stored, then map the model schema fields to your variables. You choose a streaming or a batch job, define which fields initiate prediction updates, and create the job.[^4] A streaming job automatically updates the prediction when input data changes, which keeps predictions current, while a batch job must be refreshed manually. A new DMO is created to store predictions for streaming jobs. One constraint to remember: streaming jobs do not support externally stored data sources such as zero copy or Bring Your Own Lake, so only batch jobs work for those.[^4]

The practical point for a consultant is placement. Predictions land in a Data 360 object, and anything downstream that can read a data model object can use them, including segments, calculated insights, and personalization. You are not wiring the model to a channel or to a CRM field; you are writing a scored field onto an object inside Data 360 and letting the rest of the platform read it like any other attribute.

### Once predictions are written, they become ordinary attributes

Once predictions are written to an object, they become ordinary attributes of the customer, and that is what makes them usable. A segment can filter on a predicted propensity score or a churn risk band exactly as it filters on any other field, and the resulting segment can be activated to a channel or used to personalize an experience. Because the prediction sits on a Data 360 object, it can also be joined to unified data through the relationships already modeled, so a scored unified profile drives one activation row per customer.

This is why the segment step is not an afterthought. The value of the prediction is realized only when it changes what happens to the customer. A propensity score that nobody segments on is an expensive column. A churn band that feeds a retention activation is a business outcome. The consultant connects the three steps into one sentence: train the model, score the data, then segment and activate on the score.

The quiz's activation trap is worth internalizing. Expecting segments to consume predictions without an object to hold them fails, because predictions land in a Data 360 object, and that object is what lets a segment filter on the scored field. If a team says its segment cannot see the prediction, the first question is whether the prediction job or batch transform actually ran and wrote the field.

### Wrap the technical steps in the customer scenario

Wrap the technical steps in the customer scenario. State the decision the prediction supports, the object and outcome field it learns from, how the prediction is refreshed, and where the output is consumed. A consultant who can say all four has moved from a demo to a design. A model is not an end in itself; it is an input to a decision someone is already trying to make better.

Because AI Models sits under the Einstein Trust Layer, the model and its predictions share Salesforce AI governance, so confirm data usage and access before training on customer data. The trust posture is inherited, not invented per model, but you still confirm it. Data masking, zero data retention with the model provider, and the audit posture are part of the platform, and the consultant's job is to confirm the customer is comfortable with them before customer data feeds a model.[^1]

The failure modes to name are three: a weak outcome field, training features that leak the answer, and predictions that are written but never wired into a segment or activation, so the model has no business effect. A weak outcome field predicts something nobody acts on. Leaky features produce metrics that look strong and a model that fails in production, because the model learned from a field that would not be known at prediction time. Predictions that are never wired are the original failure: a trained model with no downstream effect. Name all three in a design review and you have covered the ways predictive AI quietly does nothing.

## Config Walkthrough

Create, train, and consume a model in six steps.

1. In the Data 360 app, open the AI Models tab, which is the former Einstein Studio, and confirm the data model object that will supply training data is populated. Training reads a single DMO, so the preparation happens here.

2. Add a predictive model and choose to create a model from scratch. This is the clicks, not code path; the bring your own model path starts from the same AI Models tab when the model already exists elsewhere.

3. Select the training data, then choose the field to predict and whether the model should maximize or minimize that outcome. Pick an outcome that expresses a real business result and a direction that matches what the business wants.

4. Select the training attributes, removing fields with no predictive value, and either let the platform choose the algorithm or select one, setting Autopilot as the scenario requires. Drop fields that would not be knowable before the outcome.

5. Save and train the model, then after training completes open the training metrics such as the AUC score and ROC curve to assess the model. Treat a too high accuracy as a leakage warning, not a success.

6. Run a prediction job or add a batch data transform to write predictions to a Data 360 object, activate the model, and confirm the scored field is available to segments and Flow. This is the step that turns a trained model into a usable one.

## Exam Traps

Three traps dominate this objective.

The first trap is assuming a trained model scores by itself. Predictions are produced when a prediction job or batch data transform runs and writes results to a Data 360 object; training alone changes nothing downstream. If a segment cannot filter on the prediction, the job to write it has not run.

The second trap is choosing a target field that does not express the business outcome, or including features that leak the label. Both produce metrics that look strong and a model that fails in production. The outcome must be something the business acts on, and the features must be knowable before the outcome happens.

The third trap is expecting segments to consume predictions without an object to hold them. Predictions land in a Data 360 object, which is what lets a segment filter on the scored field. There is no shortcut where a segment reads the model directly; it reads the object the model wrote.

## The decision rules to walk away with

Remember the chain train, score, then consume. A model has no business effect until its predictions are written to a Data 360 object and used in a segment, activation, or Flow. Choose the native from scratch path when you want clicks, not code, and the bring your own model path when a data science team owns the model in SageMaker, Vertex AI, or Databricks. Evaluate with the AUC score, the ROC curve, top predictors, and the distribution before you activate.

Before you present a model, answer four questions: what decision does it support, what object and outcome field does it learn from, how are predictions refreshed, and where is the output consumed. Confirm data usage and access under the Einstein Trust Layer before training on customer data. Name the three failure modes, a weak outcome field, leaky features, and predictions that never get wired, and you will have covered the ways a predictive model quietly does nothing.
