# Lesson 44: Designing a Segment for a Business Goal: Audience, Criteria, and Testing Before Publish

A team designs a segment by opening the data model object with the most attributes and filtering until the count looks reasonable. The segment runs, the population is healthy, and it ships. Three weeks later nobody can explain what the audience is for, and the campaign team is still trying to sell to a list that maps to no offer. The segment was technically valid and commercially useless, because it was designed from the available data instead of from the goal.

Requirement ac-1b, in the Data Activations and Utilization domain worth 20 percent of the Salesforce Data 360 Consultant exam, asks you to define basic concepts of segmentation and use cases, focused on turning a business goal into a designed segment by choosing the audience, selecting criteria, and testing before it ships. Where lesson 43 explained what a segment is, this lesson is the method: how a consultant goes from a one-sentence business goal to a validated audience, in that order, without the rework that comes from starting at the canvas.

## Deep Dive

### Design begins with the business goal, stated as audience, condition, and action

Every segment should begin with a business goal stated as an outcome, not as a filter. Re-engage customers who have not purchased in 90 days is a goal. Suppress recent buyers from an ad audience is a goal. Both name who the audience is, what condition qualifies them, and what happens when they land. Only after the goal is clear do you choose the data model object and the attributes. Designing from the data first produces technically valid segments nobody uses, because a segment built from whatever fields were handy usually maps to no decision the business is trying to make.

The discipline that makes this repeatable is to write the goal as a sentence with an audience, a condition, and an action, then map each part to the pieces the platform expects. State the business goal as audience, condition, and action, then map each part to an object, criteria, and a destination. The audience maps to the Segment On object, the condition maps to one or more criteria, and the action maps to an activation target. If any of those three parts has no match, the goal is not yet a segment. If all three do, the design work is mostly done before you open a browser tab.

This ordering also survives review. When a stakeholder asks why a segment exists, you answer with the goal sentence and the three mappings. When someone asks whether the segment is working, you check whether the destination produced the action the goal intended. A segment that cannot produce that sentence is a segment that will drift into disuse the moment its author leaves.

### The Segment On object fixes the grain of the audience

The audience starts with the Segment On object, which is the data model object the segment is built on. Data 360 lets you build a segment on a data model object marked as Profile or Engagement during ingestion, such as Unified Individual, Unified Household, Account, Reservation, or Sales Order.[^1] The choice fixes the grain of the audience: segmenting on Unified Individual yields people, while segmenting on Sales Order yields orders. That distinction decides what the destination receives, because the destination gets one record per membership entity, and the membership entity is the Segment On object.

If the goal is to reach people, build on the individual and reach the order data through relationships. A consultant who needs an audience of people whose criteria rely on recent order behavior has to remember this rule. Segment on Unified Individual and reach order attributes through relationships, rather than segmenting on Sales Order and turning each order into a member. Both produce a result, and only one produces the list of people the campaign wants. When the order object becomes the Segment On object, a customer with four orders becomes four members, which is fine for order analysis and wrong for a one-email-per-person send.

![One customer with four orders carried onto two different Segment On objects. On Unified Individual the customer is one member, and on Sales Order the same customer is four members. The figure lists the Segment On candidates: Unified Individual for people, Unified Household for households and family promotions, Account and Unified Account for B2B targeting on revenue, industry, or related data, Individual without identity resolution which can duplicate or skip entities, and Sales Order or Reservation for transactional grains. Pick the object whose rows match what the destination expects to receive.](</diagrams/day-44-ac-1b-designing-a-segment-for-a-business-goal-audience-c/2-segment-on-grain.png>)

Pick the object whose rows match what the destination expects to receive. That single test resolves most Segment On decisions. An email or journey destination expects people, so segment on the individual. An analytics destination that wants order-level rows can legitimately segment on Sales Order. A household promotion expects households, so segment on Unified Household and let the household logic do the grouping. When you segment on Unified Individual, individual identifiers such as the individual ID and email are the natural members.

There is a broader set of Segment On candidates than the individual alone, and each maps to a different business question. Unified Household groups unified individuals into households, which is the right grain for a family promotion where one mailer goes to a home. Account serves B2B targeting by revenue, industry, or related data, and Unified Account does the same at the account level with identity resolution applied, so one account appears once across sources. Individual, without identity resolution, works but can duplicate or skip entities because nothing has reconciled the profiles. A household promotion pairs with Unified Household, and a B2B account target pairs with Account or Unified Account, because in every case the grain of the object has to match the grain of the decision.

### Criteria come from direct attributes, related attributes, and stored patterns

Criteria can be direct attributes on the segment object itself, or related attributes reached through relationships the segment canvas understands. Direct attributes are single data points attached to the entity you are segmenting, such as postal code, first name, or birthday, and they show up on the canvas as standalone rules. Related attributes are collections of data points, such as purchase history, product numbers, or email interactions, and they land inside containers because one profile can hold many of them.[^2] The library displays a field only if it is mapped and associated with the selected segment target, so an unmapped field is invisible to criteria no matter how useful it would be.

Three criteria patterns cover most requests. A value filter compares an attribute to a threshold, such as lifetime value above a given amount. A behavioral filter looks for a purchase or an email engagement inside a window, such as at least one order in the last 30 days. A membership filter includes or excludes people who are already in another segment, which is how you reuse a well-built audience as a building block. Mixing these patterns inside one container with an AND or OR relationship changes the meaning, so place attributes deliberately rather than stacking them.

Criteria pack into containers, and containers allow both inclusion and exclusion. When you drag a related attribute onto the canvas, a container is added automatically with the related attribute's data model object as the container object, and you can hold up to 20 filters inside a single container.[^3] Attributes in the same container are evaluated together, and attributes in separate containers are independent. That is the difference between at least one purchase that was both a scarf and yellow and a scarf purchase and a separate yellow purchase. When the criteria are likely to be reused, store the pattern in the attribute library so other builders can apply it without rebuilding the logic and without introducing a variation that quietly changes the audience.[^4]

### Preview is the test that prevents a bad send

The segment preview shows the audience the current rules would produce, so you can sanity check size and spot missing members before publishing. Preview is available to users who can view or edit segments and who have access to the underlying data in Data Explorer, so the test is permission-gated and worth confirming before you promise a preview demo to a stakeholder. Run it every time you change criteria, because a small edit to a container window or a relationship path can move the count by orders of magnitude.

When the preview does not look right, the usual culprits are a short list and each has a fix. The lookback window is the most common cause of a count that comes in low. The lookback window or a container whose criteria override it narrowed the data range, which drops members you expected to see without raising any error. A container whose criteria override the segment level window shrinks the range for that criterion specifically, so a 90-day segment with a 60-day order container only counts purchases from the last 60 days. The third culprit is a relationship that did not resolve because the fields were not mapped, which leaves a related attribute silently empty.

Testing before publish is cheaper than apologizing after a bad send. A campaign that reaches the wrong people costs credibility, a suppression that fails costs money, and both are trivially avoidable by previewing the audience and reading the count against the expectation. Wire the expected count into the segment documentation so the next person can tell immediately when an edit has broken it.

Treat the preview number as a test assertion rather than a curiosity. Write down what you expect the audience to be before you run the preview, then compare. If the preview matches your expectation, save and move on. If it does not, the direction of the gap narrows the search: a count far above expectation usually means a relationship fanned out and multiplied rows, while a count far below usually means a window that excluded data or a relationship that did not resolve. Debugging is faster when you framed the expected result first, because you are testing a hypothesis instead of staring at a number and guessing.

### Exclusions are usually where the value is

Many real use cases are about who to leave out. Exclusion criteria keep a person out of a segment even when they match the positive rules, and they are best modeled with containers so the intent is visible on the canvas. A suppression list of recent purchasers prevents a wasted send. Excluding customers in an active service escalation prevents an offer landing on top of a support problem. Both look like an audience and both are defined by who is absent from it.

The canvas supports exclusion as a first-class tab, so you do not have to invert your logic by hand. You place the attributes that should remove a person onto the Exclude side and let the platform subtract them from the included set. A waterfall segment generalizes the exclusion idea by ordering whole segments so each person lands only in the highest priority segment they match, producing clean mutually exclusive audiences without writing a single exclusion rule.[^5] That is the right tool when the goal is one offer per person rather than a specific list to suppress.

Design exclusions deliberately; they are usually the difference between a good and a costly campaign. The fastest way to lose trust in segmentation is to send an offer to someone who just bought, or a win-back to someone who is mid-complaint. Those failures are almost never caused by missing positive criteria. They are caused by an exclusion that was never modeled because the team was focused on who to include.

### Document the design so it survives you

A segment that ships without documentation becomes unmaintainable. Record the business goal, the Segment On object, the lookback window, each criterion and its source attribute, and the activation target that consumes it. This record is what lets a later consultant debug a count change without reverse-engineering the canvas, prove that a decision was consent aware, and avoid editing a segment that other teams depend on.

Include the expected count from preview and the publish schedule in the same record. A count is only meaningful next to the design that produced it and the date it was produced. When the count moves, the note tells the next reader whether the change came from data, from a criteria edit, or from a schedule change. Without the note, the only available answer is that the number is different now, which helps nobody.

Naming conventions should be specific and searchable so the segment is findable months later. A name that encodes the purpose, the audience, and the cadence beats a clever one every time, because the person searching for it in six months is searching for purpose and audience, not for wit. Combined with the design record, a disciplined name turns a segment from a personal artifact into a team asset.

### Walking the build: from goal to validated audience

The build below follows the current Data 360 interface and the design order this lesson recommends.

1. In the Data 360 app, open Segments, click New, and choose Use a Visual Builder to open the segment setup.
2. Write the business goal as audience, condition, and action, then pick the Segment On data model object that matches the grain you need.
3. Set the Lookback Window for the initial range, then in the segment canvas add containers, choose direct and related attributes, and build inclusion and exclusion criteria.
4. Reuse stored attribute library patterns where they fit, and add calculated insights when the condition depends on a metric.
5. Click Preview to review the audience size and members, and investigate anomalies against the lookback window and object relationships before saving.
6. Save the segment with a specific name, record the expected count and criteria, and continue to an activation when the design is validated.

Notice that the goal sentence is written before the object is chosen, and the count is recorded before the activation is built. That order is the lesson. Reverse it and you will design a segment around whichever object you happened to open, then spend the campaign explanation justifying an audience the business never asked for.

## Exam Traps

The first trap is designing from available data instead of from the goal, which produces valid segments nobody uses. The fix is to start with the audience, the condition, and the action, then map each to an object, criteria, and a destination. If you cannot name the destination, the segment is not ready to build.

The second trap is building on the wrong grain. Segmenting on a transactional object such as Sales Order yields orders, not people. If the destination expects people, build on Unified Individual and reach the transaction by relationship, so each customer appears once rather than once per order.

The third trap is skipping preview and discovering problems after publish. Preview reveals missing members and unexpected size, and the usual cause is the lookback window, a container override, or a relationship that did not resolve. A design that is never tested is a guess wearing a schedule.

## The Decision Rules

Carry four rules forward. Start from the goal, stated as audience, condition, and action, and map each part to an object, criteria, and a destination before you open the builder. Choose the Segment On object by the grain the destination expects, and reach transaction data through relationships rather than segmenting on it. Use containers to keep inclusion and exclusion logic readable, because exclusions are where most campaign value and most campaign risk live. And preview every change against a documented expected count, because the lookback window or a container whose criteria override it narrowed the data range is the first thing to check when the number surprises you.
