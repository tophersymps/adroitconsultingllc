# Lesson 80: AI Regulation for Builders: The EU AI Act, US Frameworks, and Industry Rules

Lesson 79 ended with a sentence worth repeating: oversight that depends on the agent cooperating is not oversight. The same logic applies to regulation. A rule that only exists in a slide deck is not a control, and a control you cannot evidence is not compliance.

This lesson is about the rules that will judge the systems you are building, and the artifacts they will ask you to produce. Not as legal advice, and not as a tour of acronyms. As an engineering problem: what must exist in the repository, in the logs, and on the approval screen, and by when.

Start with the three questions every regime asks. They are the same three everywhere.

1. **Scope and role.** Is this an AI system under the definition, and are you the provider, the deployer, or both?
2. **Tier.** What kind of harm is the system capable of, and which category does the law put it in?
3. **Evidence.** What document, log, or assessment can you hand over on request, and how long must you keep it?

Everything else is jurisdiction-specific detail layered on top of those three answers. Get the three right and the detail becomes a checklist. Get the role wrong and you can comply perfectly with the wrong set of duties.

## The EU AI Act: four tiers and a calendar that moved

Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 entered into force on 1 August 2024 and applies in stages rather than all at once. [^1] It sorts systems into four levels: unacceptable risk, high risk, transparency risk, and minimal or no risk, and attaches duties to each level. [^2]

**Unacceptable risk.** Nine practices are banned outright, among them harmful manipulation, social scoring, untargeted scraping of faces to build recognition databases, emotion recognition in workplaces and schools, and real-time remote biometric identification for law enforcement in public spaces. The first eight prohibitions have applied since 2 February 2025, the same date the AI literacy duty in Article 4 started. A ninth prohibition, covering systems that generate non-consensual intimate imagery or child sexual abuse material, arrives in December 2026 and came in through the AI Omnibus. [^2]

**High risk.** These are systems where a failure could harm health, safety, or fundamental rights, and the Act names the categories rather than the technologies: safety components in critical infrastructure, education admissions and grading, employment and worker management, credit and access to essential services, biometrics, law enforcement, migration and border control, and the administration of justice. [^2] The obligations are familiar if you have read the last few lessons: risk management, data governance, activity logging, technical documentation, instructions for use, human oversight, and adequate accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity.

**Transparency risk.** Article 50 duties for interactive and generative systems: tell people they are talking to a machine, mark AI-generated content in a machine-readable way, and label deepfakes and AI-generated text published on matters of public interest. These apply from 2 August 2026. Systems placed on the market before that date get a limited grace period on the marking duty alone, running to 2 December 2026, and content generated before 2 August 2026 does not need retroactive labelling. [^3]

**General-purpose models.** Duties for providers of foundation models started on 2 August 2025, covering transparency, copyright policy, and additional assessment and mitigation for models with systemic risk. The Commission published a voluntary code of practice alongside the obligations in July 2025. [^2]

Now the part most summaries get wrong. The high-risk rules were scheduled for 2 August 2026. They are not applying then. The Commission proposed the Digital Omnibus on AI on 19 November 2025, the co-legislators agreed a text in May 2026, and the act entered into force on 27 July 2026. High-risk obligations now start on 2 December 2027 for stand-alone systems and 2 August 2028 for AI embedded in regulated products such as medical devices and lifts. [^4] The stated reason was preparation, not policy retreat: the harmonised standards that give the rules legal certainty were not ready, and the Commission's own FAQ says the delay put the original date in jeopardy. [^4]

![Four stacked tiers of EU AI Act risk with the current application dates beside each: prohibited practices from February 2025, general-purpose model duties from August 2025, transparency duties from August 2026, and high-risk duties moved to December 2027](</diagrams/regulatory-landscape-eu-us-ai/1-four-tiers.png>)

Penalties scale with the tier. Breaching a prohibited practice carries administrative fines of up to 35 million euros or 7 percent of total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher. Breaching provider obligations, deployer obligations, or the Article 50 transparency duties carries up to 15 million euros or 3 percent. [^5] For a mid-sized company, the percentage is the number that matters, and it applies to the group, not the subsidiary that ran the experiment.

### The role question, which is where most teams get it wrong

The Act splits duties between the **provider**, who develops a system or has one developed and places it on the market or puts it into service under its own name or trademark, and the **deployer**, who uses a system under its own authority. [^6] Those are not labels you choose for convenience. If you put your name or trademark on a high-risk system that someone else built, Article 25 makes you its provider for the purposes of the Act, with the full provider duty set. [^7]

So a bank that licenses a vendor's credit-scoring engine, wraps it in its own onboarding portal, and calls it "our decisioning platform" has likely moved from deployer to provider for that component. A staffing firm that runs the vendor's engine under the vendor's name stays a deployer. Same technology, different duty set, decided by branding and control, which is exactly the kind of detail that gets missed until diligence.

## The United States: no statute, three layers, one live fight

The US has no comprehensive federal AI statute. What it has is three layers that interact badly if you model them as one.

**The voluntary layer.** NIST's AI Risk Management Framework 1.0, released in January 2023, organises AI risk work into four functions: govern, map, measure, manage. The Generative AI Profile, NIST AI 600-1 from July 2024, extends it with the risks that generative systems make new or worse, and suggests actions per function. [^8] The framework is voluntary, and it is being revised under the White House AI Action Plan, with a concept note for a critical infrastructure profile published in April 2026. [^9] Voluntary does not mean optional in practice: it is the vocabulary enterprise procurement teams, insurers and external auditors already use, and it maps almost line for line onto the ISO management system standard below.

**The federal executive layer.** Executive Order 14365, signed 11 December 2025, set a policy of a minimally burdensome national framework and created an AI Litigation Task Force inside the Department of Justice to challenge state AI laws on commerce clause and preemption grounds. It also directs the Commerce Department to evaluate state AI laws and conditions some broadband funding on state compliance. [^10]

Read that carefully, because a lot of commentary does not. An executive order cannot preempt a state statute. Preemption comes from Congress or from a valid federal regulation, and courts decide whether a particular state law falls. Until one of those happens, the state statutes are the binding layer, and the task force is a litigation strategy rather than a compliance exemption.

**The state layer, which is what you actually build against.** As of today:

- **Texas HB 149**, the Texas Responsible AI Governance Act, took effect on 1 January 2026 and regulates use of AI systems in the state with civil penalties attached. [^11]
- **California SB 53**, the Transparency in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Act, was chaptered on 29 September 2025. It applies to developers of frontier models trained with more than 10^26 integer or floating-point operations, with the heaviest duties for large developers earning over $500 million, and it requires a published frontier AI framework, transparency reports and reporting of critical safety incidents. Civil penalties reach $1 million per violation and only the Attorney General can bring them. [^12]
- **Colorado SB 26-189**, signed 14 May 2026, repeals and reenacts the state's 2024 AI Act. From 1 January 2027 it requires developers of automated decision-making technology used in consequential decisions to hand deployers technical documentation covering intended uses, training data categories and known limitations, requires deployers to give consumers notice at the point of interaction and a plain-language explanation within 30 days of an adverse outcome, and grants consumers rights to their data, to correction, and to meaningful human review. Records must be kept three years. The Attorney General enforces it through the consumer protection act, with a 60-day cure period until 1 January 2030 and no new private right of action. [^13]
- **Illinois Public Act 103-0804**, effective 1 January 2026, amends the Illinois Human Rights Act so that using AI with a discriminatory effect in recruitment, hiring, promotion, training selection, discharge, discipline or tenure is unlawful, explicitly treating zip code as a possible proxy for a protected class, and it requires employers to notify employees. [^14]
- **New York City Local Law 144** has been enforced since 5 July 2023: employers and agencies using an automated employment decision tool must have a bias audit done within the year before use, publish a summary of the results, and give the required notices, which the city's guidance sets at 10 business days before use. [^15]

The pattern to notice is instability, not stringency. Colorado passed a comprehensive AI law in 2024 and replaced it with a narrower disclosure regime in 2026. California regulates model developers while Illinois regulates employer conduct and New York City regulates hiring tools. There is no single posture to adopt, only a register you keep current.

## Industry rules and the standards layer

Two standards do most of the commercial work.

**ISO/IEC 42001:2023** specifies requirements for an AI management system: how an organisation establishes, operates and improves the governance around its AI work. It is certifiable, which is why it shows up in procurement questionnaires. [^16] **ISO/IEC 42006:2025**, published in July 2025, sets the requirements for the bodies that audit and certify those management systems, building on ISO/IEC 17021-1 so that a certificate has an accreditation chain behind it. [^17]

The distinction to hold on to: a management system certificate proves you have a repeatable process for identifying and controlling AI risk. It does not prove conformity with any specific law. What it buys you is that the per-law evidence is produced by a process rather than rebuilt from scratch for each audit.

One honest state-of-play note. The EU's harmonised standards, the ones that would give high-risk compliance a presumption of conformity, are still in development. The Commission's FAQ records that CEN and CENELEC could not deliver on the original timetable and that standardisation work continues. [^4] That is part of why the high-risk dates moved. If you need a defensible position before the standards land, ISO 42001 plus the AI RMF plus your own evidence trail is the practical substitute.

## Worked example: one hiring agent, five rulebooks

Meridian Staffing places warehouse and clinical staff. It has about 400 employees and operations in Illinois, Texas, Colorado, New York City and Dublin. Last quarter it shipped **Triage**, an agent that reads each application, scores fit against the requisition, drafts candidate outreach, proposes an interview slate, and flags any candidate it recommends rejecting.

Volume is 4,000 applications a month. A human recruiter reviews every slate before anything is sent.

### Step 1: name the role

Meridian buys a commercial foundation model as a service, so it is a deployer of that model. It built the ranking logic, the prompts, the tool calls and the candidate-facing workflow itself, and it runs the assembled system under its own name. So it is the provider of the Triage system. Both roles carry duties, and they differ: provider duties concern the system's design and documentation, deployer duties concern how it is used and overseen. [^6]

If Meridian had instead white-labelled a vendor's ranking engine and put its own brand on it, Article 25 would make it the provider of that component too. [^7]

### Step 2: classify the tier

Employment is one of the high-risk categories in the EU AI Act, since the system materially influences access to work. [^2] In Colorado, hiring is a consequential decision, so Triage is covered automated decision-making technology from 1 January 2027. [^13] In Illinois, it is AI used in hiring, which brings the Human Rights Act's discriminatory effect standard and the notice duty. [^14] In New York City, it is an automated employment decision tool, which brings the bias audit. [^15] In Texas it is a use of an AI system regulated under HB 149, and the disclosure and prohibited-use provisions are the ones to read. [^11]

One system. Five regimes. None of them asking the same question.

### Step 3: map obligations to artifacts

Here is the table that turns five rulebooks into a work plan. Each row is something that has to exist, not something that has to be believed.

| Artifact | What it contains | Which rules it answers |
|---|---|---|
| System card and technical documentation | Intended purpose, model and data categories, known limitations, human review instructions, version history | EU high-risk documentation and instructions for use; Colorado developer documentation |
| Risk classification record | Why the system is high risk, which Annex III category applies, who signed off, review date | EU conformity work; internal governance |
| Oversight design | Who reviews which tier of decision, with what authority and training, plus the escalation path | EU deployer oversight duty; Colorado human review right; NYC notice |
| Trace logs and retention | Per-run record of inputs, tool calls, outputs, reviewer identity and timestamps | EU logging duty; Colorado three-year records; incident forensics |
| Evaluation and bias evidence | Test sets, disparate impact measures, accuracy by group, re-run dates | NYC bias audit; Illinois defense; EU accuracy and robustness duties |
| Notices and appeal path | Candidate notice, adverse-action explanation within 30 days, appeal route | Colorado notices; Illinois employee notice; NYC 10-day notice; EU chatbot disclosure |
| Incident register and change log | What broke, when, blast radius, fix, and every material model or prompt change | EU serious incident reporting; California incident reporting; Colorado material update notices |

Seven artifacts. Every one of them is something the last four lessons already told you to build: the trace log is lesson 76, the oversight tiers are lesson 79, the incident process is lesson 75, the evaluation suite is lesson 72.

### Step 4: put dates on it

A compliance plan without dates is a reading list. Meridian's register, ordered by what bites first:

1. **Now.** Article 50 transparency is live. If Triage drafts candidate-facing messages, the disclosure and marking duties for AI-generated content already apply. [^3]
2. **Now.** Illinois notice obligations and New York City bias audit obligations are in force. If Meridian has never run the audit, that is the first gap to close. [^14] [^15]
3. **December 2026.** The watermarking grace period ends for generative systems already on the market. [^3]
4. **1 January 2027.** Colorado's disclosure, explanation and human review duties start. The 30-day adverse-outcome explanation is the one that needs a reporting path, not just a policy. [^13]
5. **2 December 2027.** EU high-risk obligations for stand-alone systems, which is when the seven artifacts above stop being good practice and become the minimum. [^4]

### Step 5: find the gaps you cannot paper over

Two show up in almost every version of this exercise.

The first is the adverse-action explanation. Colorado requires a plain-language description of the tool's role within 30 days of an adverse decision. [^13] Most agents produce a score and a rationale at decision time, then lose the retrieval context by the time anyone asks. If your trace retention is 24 hours, you cannot answer this in week three, let alone month three.

The second is the change log. Colorado requires developers to notify deployers of material updates, and in the EU a substantial modification can reset conformity work. If your prompts are edited in a console with no versioning, you have no way to say what the system was on the day of a disputed decision. Version the prompt like you version the code.

## The pattern: build the control once, map it many times

The instinct when a second jurisdiction arrives is to build a second compliance program. That instinct is expensive and it fails, because the two programs drift and then disagree about the same system.

Build one evidence set and one obligation register. The register is a table with four columns: obligation, regime, artifact that satisfies it, owner with a date. The evidence set is the seven artifacts above. When a new state passes a law, you add rows and check whether existing artifacts cover them. Most of the time they do, with an amendment.

![A control plane diagram showing a single evidence set feeding an obligation register that fans out to five separate regulatory regimes](</diagrams/regulatory-landscape-eu-us-ai/4-control-plane.png>)

Two warnings from teams that have run this.

Do not let the register live in a spreadsheet owned by one person in legal. It goes stale in a quarter. Put it in the repository next to the system, with dates and owners, and review it when you ship a change to the system, not on a calendar reminder that everyone snoozes.

Do not treat an artifact as done because it exists. A bias audit that tested a model version you retired in March is evidence of diligence in March. In an enforcement conversation, it is evidence that your process stopped.

## Try it

1. **Write your role sentence.** "We are the provider of [system name] because we put it into service under our name, and the deployer of [model] because we use it under our authority." If you cannot finish it, the ambiguity is the finding.
2. **Classify against the EU Annex III list.** Name the category, or write down why none applies. Keep the answer, with the date and the person who signed it.
3. **List the states you actually operate in.** Not where you are headquartered. Where the candidates, customers or employees are. Then check each one against the state list in this lesson.
4. **Build the seven artifacts.** Start with the trace retention policy, because it is the one that cannot be retrofitted. Decide today how long you keep a run, and whether you can reconstruct decision-time context ninety days later.
5. **Run the adverse-action test.** Pick a rejection from six weeks ago. Can you produce the inputs, the model version, the prompt version, and a plain-language explanation of the system's role? Time how long it takes. That number is your real readiness score.
6. **Put the dates in the repo.** Five rows, five dates, one owner each. Review it when the system changes.

## Summary

Regulation asks three things of an agent system: what role you occupy, how much harm it can do, and what you can prove.

The EU AI Act answers with tiers and a calendar. Prohibitions have applied since February 2025, general-purpose model duties since August 2025, transparency since August 2026, and high-risk duties from 2 December 2027 after the Digital Omnibus moved them. Role matters as much as tier, and Article 25 can move provider duties onto a deployer that puts its name on a high-risk system.

The US has no federal statute. NIST's framework supplies the vocabulary, executive action supplies litigation and funding pressure, and state and city statutes supply the binding obligations, from Texas and Illinois to California, Colorado and New York City. Preemption is a live political fight, not a compliance fact.

Industry standards do the connective work. ISO/IEC 42001 gives you a certifiable management system, ISO/IEC 42006 gives the certificates an accreditation chain, and neither one substitutes for the evidence a specific law demands.

The work itself is smaller than it sounds: seven artifacts, one register, dates attached. The teams that struggle are not the ones with the most obligations. They are the ones that built a separate program for each one.
