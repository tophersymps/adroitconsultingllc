# Lesson 48: Governance: AI Policies, Review Boards, and Model Risk Management

Lesson 47 made agent quality measurable: a harness, regression suites, and a gate that converts a score drop into a decision. Measurement reaches its limit when nobody is accountable for acting on it. That is where governance begins.

The one idea to carry out of this lesson: **governance is the answer to three questions that measurement cannot answer for you. Who is allowed to change this agent, the model it runs, or the data it touches? Who gets to accept the risk when we ship it anyway? And how do we prove, months later, that those decisions were made deliberately and by someone with the authority to make them?** Governance has three building blocks that answer those questions. Policies set the standing rules. A review board makes the hard, case-by-case calls that no written rule can settle and records them. Model risk management, a discipline that banks have been running since 2011, gives you the inventory, tiering, validation, and monitoring that turn governance from a binder on a shelf into a system an examiner can walk through.

## What governance means for an agent

Learning how to run one agent is a technology problem. Learning how an organization decides which agents to run, who may change them, and who is answerable for their output is a governance problem. Post the same support agent in a company with a pilot's mentality and nobody owns the prompt that slipped. Put the same agent under a proper governance structure and a named person can tell you what it was built to do, who approved the change, and what evidence cleared it.

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0, released 2023) organizes AI risk management into four functions: Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage. Govern is the foundation, and the framework treats it as cross-cutting, meaning it infuses the other three. [^1] [^2] Table 1 of the AI RMF Core lists six Govern categories. Govern 1 covers policies, processes, and practices. Govern 2 covers accountability structures: roles, responsibilities, lines of communication, and the requirement that executive leadership take responsibility for deployment decisions. Govern 1.6 requires mechanisms to inventory AI systems. Govern 1.7 requires processes for decommissioning systems safely. [^2] Read that list as the table of contents for this lesson and the next several lessons of a mature program.

The EU AI Act makes the same demand legally binding, but only for high-risk systems. Article 9 requires a provider to establish, implement, document, and maintain a risk management system, defined as a continuous iterative process planned and run throughout the entire lifecycle of the system. [^3] The act names the steps: identify and analyze known and reasonably foreseeable risks, estimate and evaluate the risks that emerge in intended use and reasonably foreseeable misuse, evaluate other risks from post-market monitoring data, and adopt targeted risk management measures that bring residual risk down to a level judged acceptable. [^3] Where NIST offers voluntary architecture you can adopt in any order, the AI Act is a required sequence with a documented trail.

## AI policies: the standing rules

An AI policy is the set of rules that apply before anyone makes a decision. It covers which tools employees may use, what information must not be entered into a model, when human review is required, and what evidence a deployment must produce before it is approved. [^4] A good policy saves a review board from drowning in trivia. If the policy already says that customer data never goes to a public model endpoint and that every external-facing bot needs a human-in-the-loop step for high-impact actions, those are preapproved controls. Nobody needs to convene a committee to rediscover them.

The limit is the thing worth understanding. A policy cannot resolve every real-world disagreement. Software will always present a case that sits outside the tidy boxes: a customer-facing chatbot where the team argues about how much testing is enough, a hiring model whose bias risk is genuinely disputed, a vendor that will not hand over documentation. [^4] Those are exactly the cases no written rule can settle, because they involve competing goals and more than one kind of risk. That is why you have both a policy and a board. The policy handles the 90 percent of uses that are routine and preapproved. The board handles the residual that needs a person to weigh the trade-offs and write down the answer.

Write policies so they delegate rather than accumulate. A policy that makes every model change a board decision becomes a bottleneck fast, and a bottleneck's real cost is that teams learn to route around it and ship shadow AI that no one governs. [^4] The policy should establish the risk-classification method and the authority attached to each tier, then let low-risk uses flow through an approved process so the board is free to concentrate where it matters.

## Review boards: the human body that decides

A review board, often called an AI governance committee, is a cross-functional body that decides who may accept which AI risks, under what conditions, and with what evidence. [^4] The name varies across organizations: AI steering committee, responsible AI council, AI risk committee, AI oversight committee. What matters is not the label but the authority behind it. [^4] The board needs a written mandate, defined membership, clear decision rights, and a direct escalation path to executive leadership and, where appropriate, the board of directors. [^4]

The membership is where most organizations get it wrong. A board made only of lawyers sees legal risk. A board made only of technologists misses the regulatory exposure. The effective version is cross-functional: an executive sponsor or chair, business or program leadership who own the outcome, legal, a risk and compliance function, privacy and data governance, cybersecurity, and technology or AI leadership. [^4] NIST makes the same point in Govern 3: decision-making is informed by a diverse team. A committee assembled from a single discipline will see only part of the problem. [^2] [^4]

Three disciplines keep a board from becoming theater. First, it should not approve every AI use. A board that decides everything is a bottleneck; a board that always approves whatever arrives is a rubber stamp, and both are failures. [^4] Second, it should not replace system owners. A named individual remains accountable for each system, its risk classification, its monitoring, and its retirement. A board can approve a use case, but only a named person can be expected to notice drift six months after launch. [^4] Third, a useful meeting produces decisions, owners, deadlines, and records. A committee that only discusses risk is not governing it. [^4] The decision record, who accepted what risk and why, is the evidence an examiner asks for later, and it rarely exists if the board does not write it down.

## Model risk management: the bank's discipline

Model risk management is where governance gets teeth, and the strongest template comes from banking. The Federal Reserve and OCC issued interagency model risk management guidance in 2011, known as SR 11-7 and OCC Bulletin 2011-12. [^5] The core discipline it popularized has survived for fifteen years: maintain a firmwide inventory of models, validate models independently of the people who built them, and subject them to ongoing monitoring and effective challenge. [^5]

The reference point matters to AI because AI systems are, by the definitions banks use, models. Whatever your agent does, pattern matching in the service of a decision, it is a quantitative method that turns input into a decision. Regulators treated it that way well before the generative wave, which is why a bank's first question to a team running an AI tool was often the one in the story every practitioner has heard: what is the model inventory entry, where is the validation, and who owns it? [^5]

A caution worth stating plainly: the landscape changed in April 2026. The Federal Reserve rescinded SR 11-7 on April 17, 2026, and replaced it with SR 26-2, issued alongside OCC Bulletin 2026-13. [^5] The new guidance is risk-based and tiered by materiality rather than applying uniform rigor to every model, it explicitly says it sets no enforceable standards, and, in a detail that matters for this course, it carves generative AI and agentic AI out of scope entirely, calling them novel and rapidly evolving and promising separate guidance in the near future. [^5] That carve-out will not hold forever, and it is not permission to skip the discipline. It means, for now, the hardest class of model is the one you have to govern with judgment in the absence of a prescriptive rule, which is precisely the scenario this lesson is about.

The transferable core stands regardless of which supervisory text is current. Inventory every AI system. Tier each one by the damage it can do, not by the cost to build it. Validate high-risk systems independently, meaning by people who did not write them and cannot be leaned on by the people who did. Monitor each one after deployment, because a model is not finished when it ships, it begins shipping risk. And put someone's name on each one so the accountability is never diffuse.

## Worked example: governance inside a running agent operation

This is not a lecture the Fortress only reads about. The operation this course runs on has its own working version of governance built from the same pieces, and it is small enough to inspect, which makes it a useful example.

The inventory is the course itself. Every lesson in the curriculum is a numbered unit with a fixed topic, a slug, and a quiz sidecar, and they are counted against a hard limit. That count is the model inventory, and it exists because a `TRACK_COMPLETE` signal, not a floor on creativity, controls when publishing stops. Nothing is added to it by accident, because the scheduled task compares its directive against the full known sequence before writing anything.

The validation is the verify gate. Before any lesson can commit, `verify-lessons.py` runs a deterministic grader that hard-fails on em-dashes and clear AI-slop phrases, refuses to publish a lesson lacking its three-question quiz sidecar, and enforces a depth floor measured against the substance of the topic. [^6] That gate is independent in the way model validation is independent: it does not reason about whether the content is good, it checks the content against rules it did not help write. An author who knows the gate is coming still has to pass it, which is the point of independent validation.

The monitoring is the regression bank. `generate-course-tiers.py` regenerates every lesson's knowledge checks and the final exam from the complete sidecar set each time the course grows, so the exam continuously covers whatever the inventory currently contains. [^6] That is ongoing monitoring in the operational sense: the system is re-tested against its own up-to-date standard on a schedule, not validated once at launch and trusted forever.

None of this needs a governance committee of eight, because the scope is small and the rules are already written. That is the real lesson in miniature. The governance artifacts scale: a one-line command that owns the inventory, a deterministic gate that validates, a regeneration script that monitors. The same three building blocks, inventory, independent validation, monitoring, are what a bank decked out at a billion dollars in models runs, just with more meetings around them.

## The pitfalls to avoid

The first is governance theater. A weekly committee that approves everything, produces no written decisions, and never revisits a shipped system is worse than no committee, because it lets the organization believe the risk is managed when it is actually unowned. [^4] A governance program is judged by whether its decisions change what the organization does, not by the thickness of its policy binder. [^4]

The second is the inventory gap. Almost every maturing AI program fails on the same two gaps: an incomplete inventory of systems and an accountability structure that does not match who actually owns the models in production. [^5] You cannot govern an agent you do not know exists, and shadow AI, deployed quietly outside the review process, is the single most reliable way to accumulate ungoverned risk. The inventory is not paperwork; it is the map that tells you what needs oversight in the first place.

The third is confusing compliance with governance. Saying no to everything is a viable way to avoid regulatory attention and a disastrous way to run an AI program, because you give up the value the tools exist to capture. Good governance is an allocation of risk tolerance, a decision, made deliberately, about how much risk the organization is willing to hold and who signs for it. A framework that cannot say "this risk is acceptable, here is who accepted it, and here is the evidence" is not managing risk, it is avoiding decisions about it. [^4]

The fourth is treating governance as a one-time launch gate. The rule to carry: a model is not a project, it is an ongoing obligation. The EU AI Act's risk management system is defined as continuous and iterative across the lifecycle, with regular systematic review and updating. [^3] Validate before you ship, and keep monitoring after, because the risk profile of the input changes as the world the agent operates in changes.

## Try it

1. **Draft the policy for one agent.** Take an agent you have running and write the rules that would have governed it from the start: approved tools, data that must never be entered, human review requirements, and the evidence a deployment must produce before approval. List which third of those rules a review board would need to decide case by case. [^4]

2. **Build the inventory entry.** For the same agent, write the one-page record that makes it governable: the intended purpose, the named system owner, the risk tier and why, the validation that cleared it, and the monitoring threshold that would trigger a review. If a gap shows up, that gap is your shadow risk, and it is precisely the gap a regulator or a board would probe. [^5]

3. **Walk one decision through your governance.** Pick the last risky thing you changed, a new tool, a model swap, a class of data. Write down who approved it, who accepted the residual risk, and what evidence backed the call. If you cannot write the answer, you have found exactly the missing decision right that governance exists to fill. [^4]
