# Lesson 90: Final Synthesis: The Enterprise AI Architect's Toolkit, Complete

Lesson 89 ended with a calendar and one hard warning: choose the workflow where you can see the labelled history, because a wrong choice costs a quarter instead of a program. That lesson was about sequence. This one is about the inventory you sequence, and it is the last lesson in the series.

Ninety lessons is more than anyone holds in their head at once, and that is the problem this lesson solves. Nobody needs to recall lesson 43 during a design review. You need to answer a hostile question in one sentence, name the artifact that backs the answer, and know which shelf it came from.

The difference shows up in the room. A reviewer asks whether the agent can issue a refund without a human. An architect who has not done this sorting starts talking about the model and the prompt. An architect who has done it answers with the rung, the cap, the credential the tool uses, and the trace that proves it ran that way. Same system, same review, different credibility.

What follows is the sorting: four shelves that hold the whole course, the five decisions that survived every model release in it, one afternoon audit you can run against a live system, and the rule for choosing the two areas you go deep on next.

## Define the terms

**Toolkit.** The decisions and artifacts you reuse across projects, not the software you install. A framework is a dependency you can drop. A toolkit is what you know how to do when the framework is gone.

**Shelf.** A group of toolkit pieces that answer the same question. There are four: can it do the work, does it keep working at a cost you forecast, what may it never do, and why does it deserve to exist.

**Capability and control.** Capability is what a system can do. A control is what it cannot do regardless of what the model decides on a given run. Writing "never email the customer directly" into a system prompt is a capability limit. A deny rule that blocks the send tool is a control. The gap between those two sentences is where incident reviews live.

**Invariant.** A decision that stayed true across the whole course. Model releases changed prices, context windows and tool formats. They did not change the invariants, which is why the five in the next section are worth memorizing and the version numbers are not.

**Seam.** The place where two shelves meet, and where most production failures actually happen. The evaluation set meeting the retrieval index is a seam. The autonomy decision meeting the service account scope is a seam.

**Audit item.** One shelf question with a yes or no answer and a named owner. "Do we have a held-out set with recorded outcomes, and who owns it" is an audit item. "Are we doing evaluation" is a conversation that ends in agreement and no artifact.

**Deep area.** Two parts of the toolkit you can defend under hostile questioning, chosen by exposure rather than by novelty.

## The four shelves

Every lesson in this course lands on one of four shelves. This table is the contents page for everything that came before it.

| Shelf | What sits on it | Where it lives in the course | The question it answers | What its absence looks like |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Build | The loop and its patterns, tool schemas, protocols, retrieval, memory, model choice, prompt versioning | Loop and patterns (3, 23), tools and protocols (7, 8, 30, 31), retrieval and memory (10 to 15, 61 to 62) | Can the system do the work at all? | A demo that cannot be scored |
| Operate | Evaluation, tracing, reliability, latency budgets, cost per unit, scaling | Evals and observability (19, 20, 36 to 39), reliability and performance (53 to 55), cost (73), audit and replay (76) | Does it keep working, and at what cost? | A pass rate nobody can reproduce and a bill nobody forecast |
| Control | Identity, guardrails, human in the loop, audit trail, governance, regulation | Guardrails (17, 18), oversight (25, 79), identity and policy (48 to 52), security review (74), regulation (80) | What may it never do? | An incident report where a control should have been |
| Justify | Business case, baseline, adoption, review, roadmap, handoff | Case studies (40 to 43), business case and adoption (81, 82), review (83), capstone and roadmap (84 to 90) | Why should this exist, and who owns it? | A working system nobody approved and nobody funds |

Two properties of that table matter more than its contents.

The shelves are not phases. They are a maintenance surface, and a system in production has open items on all four at once. The control shelf moves when your threat model or a regulation moves. The build shelf moves when a model release changes what is cheap. The operate shelf moves the week a metric drifts. Treat the table as a project plan that finishes and you will ship something that was complete on the day it deployed and has been quietly aging ever since.

The shelves also have different owners in anything larger than a team of one. Microsoft's AI agent adoption guidance is useful here because it names components rather than phases: a generative model that reasons, instructions that set scope and boundaries, retrieval that grounds answers, actions that let the agent change systems, and memory that carries state across turns.[^1] Four of those five sit on the build shelf. Memory sits across build and control, because what an agent retains is a retention decision with a legal owner rather than an implementation detail to settle later.

That guidance also gives the reason the operate and control shelves exist at all. It splits agents into productivity agents that retrieve and synthesize, action agents that perform specific tasks inside defined workflows, and automation agents that run multi-step processes with minimal oversight, then states that the generative decision-making at the center of all three introduces nondeterministic behavior, which requires testing and governance.[^1] A deterministic job that fails returns the same wrong answer every time. An agent that fails returns a different one, which is why monitoring and policy belong in the design rather than the epilogue.

Protocols sit on the build shelf beside tools, and they deserve a paragraph because they outlive whatever framework you wrap around them. The Model Context Protocol specification defines an open protocol over JSON-RPC 2.0 where a host application connects through clients to servers, servers offer resources, prompts and tools, clients may offer elicitation back to servers, capability negotiation happens per request, and optional extensions such as long-running tasks are negotiated at initialization.[^2] Plumbing decides how fast you can change what an agent touches. It does not decide whether the agent should touch anything, and that question is item one on the control shelf.

## Five decisions that did not change

If you keep one page from ninety lessons, keep this one. Each decision is written the way it survived, with its evidence requirement attached, because every one of them has a version that reads fine on a slide and collapses in production.

**1. Build the simplest construction that works, and climb only when evidence says you must.** Anthropic's guidance on building agents is explicit about this: find the simplest solution possible and increase complexity only when it is needed, because agentic systems trade latency and cost for task performance rather than winning on merit against a simpler design.[^3] The same page warns that frameworks add abstraction layers which can obscure the underlying prompts and responses and make them harder to debug.[^3] In review language that becomes: name the simpler level you rejected and why it was not enough. If you cannot name it, you have a preference, not a design decision.

**2. The loop is a loop, and every loop needs budgets.** Perceive, reason, act, observe, then run it again with limits attached: turns, tokens, wall clock, dollars per unit. Every framework from lesson 21 onward and every platform from lesson 71 onward is a wrapper around those four steps. Budgets are the part that gets skipped, and an unbounded loop is not an agent. It is an outage waiting for its trigger.

**3. Measurement comes before architecture.** A held-out set with the real outcome recorded beside each case, thresholds written as numbers with a consequence attached, and a grading order that starts with the cheapest reliable check. The entire operate shelf rests on this invariant, and it is also the one architects most often inherit instead of building, which is why so many teams can describe their system in detail and cannot score it.

**4. Autonomy belongs to actions, not to agents.** One agent can sit at four different rungs depending on which tool it is reaching for. The rung, the evidence that permitted it, the cap on blast radius and the date of the scheduled descent are four separate written decisions. "Semi-autonomous" tells an operator nothing about what the system does at 2am, and the operator is the person who has to explain it afterward.

**5. The human boundary is a design artifact.** A named owner, an escalation path, a kill switch that somebody has pulled in a drill, and the rules with no exceptions enforced outside the model by the runtime rather than inside the prompt. Lesson 89 drew the deterministic line. This is the same line seen from the review side: a control the model can decline is not a control.

Read the five again and notice what they have in common. Each one is a statement about evidence or about boundaries. None of them is a statement about a model, a vendor or a framework, which is why a release cycle that changes three of those things every quarter has not moved any of them.

## An afternoon audit of a live system

Dana is an architect at a 400-person freight brokerage. Six thousand carrier exception emails arrive each month, an agent triages them and drafts a reply for a human to send, and the system has been running for five months. She has one afternoon and a design review next week.

She runs the shelf audit with four items per shelf, sixteen in total. Every item gets a yes, a no and a name beside it.

| Shelf | Audit item | Dana's answer | The failure it predicts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Build | Can a new engineer start the loop from the repository without asking anyone? | Yes | Onboarding debt rather than an outage |
| Build | Is every tool schema versioned with the prompt that depends on it? | Yes | Nothing yet, and nothing for a while |
| Build | Is there a written out-list of actions the agent may never take? | No | Scope creep into a high-consequence action |
| Build | Does every write tool carry an idempotency key? | Yes | Duplicate writes after a retry at peak |
| Operate | Is there a held-out set with recorded outcomes, and a re-label date? | No | A pass rate that drifts with no code change and no explanation |
| Operate | Does the evaluation suite run on a schedule, whether or not anyone ships? | No | A regression nobody detects until a user reports it |
| Operate | Is cost per unit measured, including retries and evaluation calls? | Yes | A finance conversation in month seven |
| Operate | Is p95 latency measured at the point where the human is waiting? | No | A queue that grows invisibly during the morning peak |
| Control | Does the send action require a human click in the interface? | Yes | The worst case in the design becomes reachable |
| Control | Is the service account scoped to the tools the agent actually calls? | No | A credential that outranks the autonomy decision |
| Control | Can a tool call be replayed from the trace with its arguments? | Yes | An incident nobody can reconstruct |
| Control | Is there a named on-call owner and a kill switch path? | Yes | Nobody to call at 2am |
| Justify | Is there a decision record for the model tier? | No | A choice that gets relitigated every quarter |
| Justify | Is the baseline dated, and is the baseline number written down? | Yes | A business case nobody can defend in a budget cycle |
| Justify | Is there an approval with conditions, in writing, from a named approver? | No | A system that exists on enthusiasm rather than authority |
| Justify | Is there a runbook somebody else has used for a week? | No | A system that leaves when Dana does |

Eight yes, eight no. That is the whole audit. Dana does not have a partially evaluated system and a vaguely governed one. She has four shelves, eight items that hold and eight that do not, each with a name and a predicted failure.

Now the part that decides whether the audit was worth the afternoon. She orders the eight gaps by the cost of the failure they predict, not by the effort of fixing them.

1. The service account scope, because a credential that outranks the autonomy decision makes every other control conditional.
2. The re-label date on the held-out set, because a stale evaluation number is more dangerous than no number at all. It gets quoted in meetings.
3. The scheduled evaluation run, which is the cheapest of the six and the only one that catches regressions.
4. p95 latency at the human wait point, because a drafting agent that is slow stops being used and a tool nobody uses reports no failures.
5. The out-list and the approval, both of which are an hour of writing and both of which belong in the packet rather than in a conversation.
6. The runbook and the decision record, which are the two that Dana cannot delegate and the two most likely to be postponed again.

She takes eight of sixteen and a fix order to the review instead of a status of "on track". One of those two answers survives contact with a skeptical reviewer, and it is not the second one.

## Read the seams

A shelf audit tells you which pieces are missing. The seams tell you what will actually break, because the failures that reach production come from the joins between shelves rather than the gaps inside them. Four seams cover most of what goes wrong after go-live.

1. **The evaluation set against retrieval freshness.** The held-out cases carry outcomes that were true when they were labelled. The index behind them gets rebuilt, the source documents get edited, and the answers change without a line of code changing. The detection is a re-label date on the set and a canary case whose expected answer you can check by hand. The fix is a cadence, not a project.
2. **The cost envelope against the evaluation suite.** Run cost has four lines, and the nightly evaluation run is the one teams forget, because it does not appear in user traffic. A budget built from per-request token math is wrong by the amount the evaluation program spends, plus retries, plus retrieval, plus the human minutes spent sampling decisions.
3. **The governance policy against the enforced control.** A policy on a slide is an instruction, and an instruction that the model can decline is a capability limit at best. The enforced version is a deny rule, a scoped credential, a test that fails the build, or an approval that cannot be bypassed in the interface. If the same rule has been rewritten three times to make it stick, it belongs on the enforced side.
4. **The autonomy rung against the credential scope.** This is the seam that turns a bounded design into an unbounded one. An action at the draft-only rung is still an action if the credential behind it can write. The scope is the real permission, and it is set in a console that the design document never mentions.

Every one of those four is a join. If you fix the shelves and ignore the seams, you get a system whose every component is defensible and whose behavior is not.

## Where the field stands

Three external references frame how young this practice still is, and each one maps onto a shelf.

Governance first. NIST's AI Risk Management Framework organizes the work into four functions, GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE and MANAGE, with governance as a cross-cutting function that informs and runs through the other three rather than sitting beside them.[^4] That is the control shelf and the operate shelf described as one system, and it is also the reason a policy that nobody enforces fails its own framework test.

Security second. The OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications 2025 orders the risks an agent exposes: prompt injection, sensitive information disclosure, supply chain, data and model poisoning, improper output handling, excessive agency, system prompt leakage, vector and embedding weaknesses, misinformation, and unbounded consumption.[^5] Part of that list is older security work wearing new nouns, which is reassuring. Excessive agency and unbounded consumption are genuinely new, and both are control shelf items with a number attached.

Adoption third, and this is the number worth carrying into your next planning conversation. In the 2025 Stack Overflow developer survey, 84 percent of respondents said they use or plan to use AI tools in their development process, 51 percent of professional developers said they use them daily, and 8,323 respondents, 17 percent of the sample, said they use or develop agents as part of their development work.[^6] Among the respondents working with agents, ChatGPT appeared at 82 percent, GitHub Copilot at 68 percent and Claude Code at 40.8 percent.[^6] Tools are close to universal. Agent development is still a minority activity, measured on the largest sample we have.

Put those three side by side and the reading is not complicated. The tooling curve is steep, the risk list is already published and ordered, and the part of the toolkit that decides whether a system survives its second year is the part the field is thinnest on. That is the operate shelf and the control shelf, and it is where an architect's work is hardest to reuse from a vendor blog post.

## Choosing the two deep areas

Lesson 89 closed with a question: which two parts of the toolkit are you going to go deep on next. Here is the rule for answering it, and it is deliberately not about what is interesting.

Score each shelf twice, from one to five. **Exposure** is how close the nearest failure you have personally seen is to that shelf. **Visibility** is how much your organization judges you by the number that lives there. Then apply one constraint: if you are the accountable architect for a system in production, one of your two areas must come from the control shelf, because that is the shelf where being a generalist gets expensive.

| If you are | Your two deep areas | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A platform or infrastructure architect | Operate and build | Your number is cost per unit and p95 latency, and both are instrumented |
| A risk, security or GRC architect | Control and justify | Your number is findings closed and approvals granted, and the artifacts live in both shelves |
| A solo architect on one system | Build and operate | You are the only person who can keep the loop and the eval set honest |
| A product owner or delivery lead | Justify and operate | Your number is adoption and unit economics, and neither survives without measurement |

The test for a real deep area is one sentence with a number in it. For the operate shelf: "Our held-out pass rate is 87 percent, it is re-labelled quarterly, and a three-point drop halts new intents." For the control shelf: "Every write tool is scoped to one service account, the send action requires a human click, and we have drilled the kill switch twice." If you cannot produce the sentence, that shelf is working knowledge for now, and saying so out loud is better than pretending.

Then write the two down in the roadmap from lesson 89, with the artifact each one produces in thirty days. A deep area without an artifact is a reading list.

## Exam traps

**Treating the toolkit as a software list.** The version of the toolkit that fails is a list of frameworks. The version that survives is a list of artifacts and decisions, which is why the shelf table above names tools and protocols in one row out of four.

**Confusing capability with control.** A prompt that says never is a preference. A deny rule, a scoped credential or a blocking test is a control. Exam answers that treat the two as interchangeable are wrong for the same reason the production systems that confuse them are wrong.

**Auditing only the shelves you enjoy.** Most architects audit build and operate and report on control and justify from memory. The assembly is only worth doing if the shelves you are least comfortable on get the same four questions as the rest.

**Calling a shelf complete because a document exists.** A policy document on the control shelf is evidence that somebody wrote a policy. The audit item is whether the rule is enforced by something that does not ask the model for permission.

**Choosing a deep area by novelty.** Depth in the newest protocol is worth less than depth in the shelf where your nearest failure lives, because the second one is the shelf that will page you.

**Reading a synthesis as permission to skip the cheap items.** The eight gaps in the audit above cost less than a week combined, and four of them are writing. Synthesis is not a reason to defer them.

**Promising a lesson that does not exist.** This course ends here. Anything that promises a next lesson in this series is describing the restart, not a continuation.

## Try it

One afternoon, and you will need the system in front of you rather than a document about it.

1. Work through the sixteen audit items. Answer each one with a yes, a no and a name. Where your own system needs different items, swap them in, but keep four per shelf.
2. Score each shelf out of four. Write the four scores at the top of the page, because the shape of the four numbers tells you more than the total does.
3. Order every gap by the cost of the failure it predicts, not by how easy or interesting the fix looks.
4. Run the four seam checks by hand: the re-label date, the evaluation suite line in the cost model, one governance rule traced to its enforced form, and one tool credential compared against its autonomy rung.
5. Write the two deep areas and the artifact each one produces in thirty days. Put both in the roadmap calendar from lesson 89.
6. Take the page to one person who did not build the system and ask them to attack the eight gaps in your ordering. Their objection is the review you have not had yet.

The step people skip is number three, and it is the step that turns a list of gaps into a plan somebody will actually fund.

## Summary

- Ninety lessons sort onto four shelves: build, operate, control and justify. They are a maintenance surface rather than phases, and they have different owners.
- The components of an agent are a model, instructions, retrieval, actions and memory, and the nondeterminism in the middle is why testing and governance are permanent requirements rather than later phases.[^1]
- Five decisions survived every release in the course: simplest construction that works, loop budgets, measurement before architecture, autonomy per action with evidence, and the human boundary as a design artifact.
- The seams between shelves cause more production failures than the gaps inside them, and the four that matter are evaluation freshness, cost envelope, enforced control and credential scope.
- Run the sixteen-item audit against a live system in an afternoon, order the gaps by the cost of the failure they predict, and take the page into the review.
- The field is early on agent development even where tool adoption is near universal, and the shelves that decide whether a system survives year two are the least crowded.[^6]
- Pick two deep areas by exposure and visibility, with one from the control shelf if you are accountable, and give each one an artifact within thirty days.
