# Lesson 81: ROI: Building the Business Case for Enterprise AI

Lesson 80 ended with seven compliance artifacts, each of which costs engineering time, review capacity and storage. Somebody senior is going to ask what the return is. Most teams answer that question badly, and they answer it badly in two directions at once.

The first bad answer is a vendor deck with a number on it. "Forty percent productivity gain" or "three hundred hours saved per month," presented as if the figure came from anywhere but a competitor's case study. The second bad answer is no number at all, just a confident claim that the pilot is going well because people seem to like it. Both end the same way: a budget review where nobody can defend the spend, and the program gets cut or quietly starved.

A business case is not a spreadsheet you fill in after the decision. It is the artifact that tells you whether to make the decision, and then keeps telling you whether to keep going. That second job is the one people forget.

## Four things people mean when they say ROI

The word gets used for four different measurements, and most arguments about AI value are actually arguments about which one is on the table.

Return on investment is the ratio itself: net benefit divided by total cost. Spend $180,000 to net $390,000 and the ROI is 117 percent. It is useful for ranking two options competing for the same budget, and close to useless for anything else, because it hides timing entirely.

Payback period is how long until cumulative cash returns the cash you put in. This is the number a CFO reaches for when capital is constrained, and the number that gets cut from board slides most often. A 4.5 month payback and a 4.5 year payback can carry identical ROI figures.

Net present value discounts every future dollar back to today and sums them. It is the only one of the four that handles a three-year horizon honestly, because a dollar saved in month 34 is worth less than a dollar saved in month 3. If your case runs longer than a year, NPV is the primary number and the other three are supporting detail.

Total cost of ownership is the cost side alone, fully loaded. It is not a return measurement at all, but it is the number that kills more AI programs than any benefit shortfall, because TCO is where the surprises live.

Underneath all four sits the unit that makes them computable: **cost per accepted output**. Not cost per token, not cost per seat, not cost per query. Cost per unit of work that a human or a downstream system accepts without redoing it.

That distinction carries most of this lesson. Get the denominator wrong and every number above it is wrong too.

All four are computable only on cost per accepted output.](</diagrams/roi-business-case-enterprise-ai/1-four-roi-measures.png>)

## Three numbers that get quoted, and what each one actually measures

Before building anything, it helps to know what the published evidence says, because someone in the room will quote a statistic at you. Three dominate, and they are routinely stitched into a single story that none of them supports.

Worldwide AI spending is forecast to hit $2.670 trillion in 2026, up 49.5 percent year over year, according to Gartner's September 2026 forecast. Within that, spending on AI agents and assistants is forecast at $29.2 billion for 2026, up from $16.5 billion in 2025. Gartner also places generative AI in the Trough of Disillusionment for 2026, and notes that enterprises are leaning on AI features embedded in software they already own, while asking providers to help them track costs and evaluate success inside the workflow.[^1] That last clause is the whole lesson in miniature.

The expectations gap is measurable. Gartner research published on October 26, 2026 found that 77 percent of board members expect AI to deliver value through productivity and cost savings, while 97 percent of organizations cannot achieve their desired cost savings through productivity alone. Thirty-eight percent of companies are over budget on AI, and 85 percent plan to spend more next year. In the same research, a July 2026 survey of 297 CHROs found 78 percent of leaders have no alignment on where AI creates value, and a 2Q26 survey of 1,865 employees found only 21 percent of the time freed up by AI was redirected into more innovative work.[^2]

McKinsey's 2025 global survey found 88 percent of respondents report regular AI use in at least one business function, up from 78 percent a year earlier. Roughly two thirds had not yet begun scaling AI across the enterprise. Thirty-nine percent attributed any enterprise-level EBIT impact to AI, and only about 6 percent qualified as high performers: those attributing at least 5 percent of EBIT to AI and describing the impact as significant.[^3]

MIT's NANDA initiative, in *The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025*, analyzed 300 public AI deployments, surveyed 350 employees and interviewed 150 leaders, and concluded that 95 percent of enterprise generative AI pilots produced no measurable P&L impact. The report also found the largest returns in back-office automation rather than the sales and marketing tools that absorbed more than half of budgets, and that buying from specialized vendors succeeded about 67 percent of the time while internal builds succeeded roughly one third as often.[^4]

Read those three together and a temptation appears: 95 percent fail, 39 percent see EBIT impact, 77 percent of boards expect savings. It would be easy to write "fewer than one in twenty programs deliver, yet most boards expect returns." That sentence would be a fabrication, because the three figures do not share a denominator.

The 95 percent is a share of analyzed deployments that failed to produce measurable P&L impact. The 39 percent is a share of survey respondents whose organizations attribute some enterprise EBIT impact to AI, which is a far weaker claim than "the deployment paid for itself." The 77 percent is a board sentiment figure with no performance content at all. Stacking them into one arc is exactly the failure mode this course keeps warning about: a well-formatted composite that no source supports.

![Three quoted statistics side by side: 95 percent of analyzed deployments showed no measurable profit and loss impact, 39 percent of McKinsey survey respondents attribute some enterprise EBIT impact to AI, and 77 percent of board members expect savings. Each has a different denominator, so none of them can be combined.](</diagrams/roi-business-case-enterprise-ai/2-stat-denominators.png>)

What the numbers do support is narrower and more useful. Most programs do not produce a defensible return, the gap is organizational more than technical, and the returns that do appear cluster in unglamorous back-office work. Your business case has to survive in that environment.

## Build the cost side first

Benefits are negotiable. Costs are not. Build the cost model before you write a single sentence about value, because the cost model is what tells you whether the benefit question is even interesting.

McKinsey's July 2026 interview with David Tepper, who ran internal generative AI consumption for Azure, lays out six cost drivers that enterprise spreadsheets usually miss.[^5]

Long-lived context. Agentic tasks can consume roughly 1,000 times more tokens than single-turn reasoning or chat. Every tool call appends to a context that gets re-sent on the next turn, so cost grows with the length of the trajectory rather than the size of the task.

Refinement is the sink. In the tokenomics research the article cites, about 60 percent of an agentic task's cost is tied to checking, repairing and re-verifying answers, not to producing the first one. The expensive part of an agent is the part that looks like quality control.

Autonomy creates variance. Two runs of the same task can take different paths, call different tools and retry different amounts. Tepper reports up to 30 times variance on outcomes from the same prompt. Cost behaves as a distribution, not a unit price, which means a business case built on the mean will be wrong in both directions: too optimistic in the tail, too pessimistic for the common case.

Reasoning is wasted on easy work. Extended thinking pays for itself on hard tasks and is pure overhead on easy ones. An agent that always thinks hard is paying a premium on the 80 percent of cases that did not need it.

Orchestration compounds. How work is decomposed and handed between models changes cost by an order of magnitude without changing the outcome. A three-agent pipeline that re-sends full context at every hop costs several times what a single agent with one tool loop costs for the same answer.

Information structure matters. Prompt design, context length, formatting and language all move the token count. Non-English text fragments into more tokens per unit of meaning, so the same conversation costs more in some languages than others.

Those six describe the variable layer. The enterprise layer sits on top of it, and it is usually larger:

- Integration work against the systems of record (ERP, CRM, ticketing), which is where most failed pilots actually died
- The evaluation set: building it, labeling it, and keeping it current as the workflow changes
- Human review capacity, which does not disappear when the agent improves, it relocates
- Platform, observability and cost attribution, so you can tell which of your agents is the expensive one
- Security, access control and audit, which lesson 79 treated as a design input and which shows up here as a line item
- Change management, which lesson 82 covers and which every honest cost model includes
- Exit cost: the work of getting your prompts, evals and data out of a vendor you want to leave

Model the cost twice, at the median and at the 90th percentile. If the case only clears at p50, it does not clear.

## The denominator problem

Cost per token is not a business metric. Tepper's formulation is blunt and correct: tokens are not value, tokens are the bill. The bill tells you what you spent. It does not tell you whether you should have spent it.

Cost per seat is not a business metric either, though it is how every SaaS contract is priced, which is precisely why AI procurement keeps going wrong. Consumption-based systems do not scale with users. Ten people running one agent hard can outspend a thousand people running it casually.

The denominator that works is accepted outputs:

```
cost per accepted output =
  (model + infrastructure + human review + platform + amortized build)
  ÷ (volume × first-pass acceptance rate)
```

First-pass acceptance matters because rework is the hidden multiplier. An agent that produces 1,000 outputs a month at 70 percent acceptance with 8 minutes of rework on the failures has a real cost of 1,000 units of model spend plus 240 units of human correction, divided by 700 usable outputs. The number that comes out is often double the naive figure.

![The denominator problem: cost per token, cost per seat and cost per query all fail, replaced by cost per accepted output. A worked rework example shows 1,000 outputs at 70 percent first-pass acceptance, with 300 failures corrected at eight minutes each adding 240 units of human correction over 700 usable outputs.](</diagrams/roi-business-case-enterprise-ai/3-denominator-rework.png>)

Once you have that number, three questions become answerable. Is it below the human cost of the same output? How much can it degrade before it stops being below? And which of the fourteen things we are running is the one to kill?

## Worked example: the invoice exception queue

A mid-market company processes 24,000 supplier invoices a month. Roughly one in eight arrives with a problem: a quantity mismatch against the purchase order, a missing PO, a bank detail that does not match the vendor master, or a suspected duplicate. That is 3,000 exceptions a month.

This example is constructed, and every number in it is labeled so you can substitute your own. The method is the point.

### Step 1: measure the baseline

An AP analyst works an exception in a median of 22 minutes: open the invoice, find the PO, check the receiving record, email the business owner, get a reply, post the decision. Three weeks of timestamp data gives a median of 22 minutes and a 90th percentile of 41. Use the median for the case and quote the tail separately, because the tail is where the overtime lives.

Baseline hours: 3,000 × 22 ÷ 60 = **1,100 hours a month**.

Loaded analyst cost (salary, benefits, overhead, tooling): **$62 an hour**.

Baseline cost: 1,100 × $62 = **$68,200 a month**, or $818,400 a year.

Two warnings on baselines. Do not compare against your worst quarter, and do not count hours that were never going to be worked. If analysts absorb the queue inside salaried time with slack in it, you have not saved cash, you have freed capacity. Those are different things, and the next section is where that distinction either holds or collapses.

### Step 2: design the change and bound it

This is a workflow with one agentic step inside it, using the taxonomy from lesson 78. Retrieval and the three deterministic checks are code. The agent's job is bounded: when a check fails, gather the evidence from the invoice, the PO, the receiving record and prior correspondence, then propose a resolution with citations.

A validator gate decides what happens next. It passes the proposal if the schema is valid, the cited evidence actually exists in the retrieved set, the proposed action is inside a policy allowlist, and confidence clears a threshold. Otherwise the exception goes to a human with the evidence packet already assembled.

Design assumption for the base case: the gate auto-clears 40 percent of exceptions. The remaining 60 percent go to an analyst, who now spends 12 minutes instead of 22 because the lookup work is already done. Analysts sample 10 percent of the auto-cleared cases for four minutes each.

Note what carries the value in this design. It is not the 40 percent autonomy. It is the packet preparation that shortens every remaining human decision, which is the pattern most back-office agent work actually follows.

![The invoice exception queue end to end: 24,000 invoices a month produce 3,000 exceptions, a validator gate auto-clears 40 percent and sends the other 60 percent to an analyst who now needs 12 minutes instead of 22. Baseline 1,100 hours a month drops to 368, freeing 732 hours worth 45,384 dollars against a 13,053 dollar cost stack, netting 32,331 dollars a month and a 4.5 month payback on 180,000 dollars.](</diagrams/roi-business-case-enterprise-ai/4-invoice-workflow-economics.png>)

### Step 3: the monthly cost stack

| Line | Basis | Monthly |
|---|---|---|
| Inference | 51,000 blended tokens per attempt, $4 per million tokens, 1.4 attempts per exception | $857 |
| Retrieval and hosting | vector index, orchestration, logging | $1,200 |
| Sample review of auto-cleared | 120 cases × 4 minutes × $62 | $496 |
| Build amortization | $180,000 over 24 months | $7,500 |
| Run and eval upkeep | 0.25 FTE equivalent | $3,000 |
| **Total** | | **$13,053** |

The $4 per million blended token figure is a planning assumption, not a market quote. Replace it with your provider's current rate card, then recompute, because that line moved a lot between 2024 and 2026 and will move again.

The build cost is the one people fudge. Integration against an ERP, a labeled evaluation set of a few hundred real exceptions, a policy allowlist, a review UI and a security review is not a two-week job. $180,000 is deliberately unglamorous, and if your estimate is a fifth of that, the missing work is somewhere in the list from the previous section.

### Step 4: benefits, net, and the three return numbers

Hours after: 1,800 exceptions × 12 minutes ÷ 60 = 360 hours, plus 8 hours of sample review = **368 hours a month**.

Hours freed: 1,100 minus 368 = **732 hours a month**.

At $62 an hour: **$45,384 a month**.

Whether that is cash or capacity depends on what the company does next. If two of the six analysts are redeployed to a backlog that was previously outsourced, the $45,384 contains real cash. If they simply have less to do and headcount stays flat, you have bought capacity worth $45,384 and banked $0. The business case should say which one, in writing, with a name attached.

Net monthly benefit, treating the hours as cash: $45,384 minus $13,053 = **$32,331**.

Cash flow, which excludes the amortization line and puts the build cost at month zero: $45,384 minus $5,553 = **$39,831 a month**.

Payback on $180,000: 180,000 ÷ 39,831 = **4.5 months**.

Thirty-six month NPV at a 10 percent discount rate, build at month zero, no amortization inside the cost line: roughly **$1.05 million**.

Three figures, three different stories. The ROI ratio (about 330 percent over three years, net benefit divided by total cost with the build counted once) is the one that wins the slide. The 4.5 month payback is the one that survives a capital committee. The $1.05 million NPV is the one an actual finance model will be built from. Present all three, and label which is which, because a reviewer who finds a payback figure masquerading as an ROI will distrust the rest of the document.

### Step 5: sensitivity, and where the case breaks

The 40 percent auto-clear rate is a guess until measured. Vary it, and vary the human time on the tail:

| Auto-clear rate | Tail time | Hours freed | Monthly benefit | Net monthly |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 60% | 11 min | 868 | $53,816 | $40,763 |
| 40% | 12 min | 732 | $45,384 | $32,331 |
| 20% | 14 min | 536 | $33,232 | $20,179 |
| 10% | 15 min | 423 | $26,226 | $13,173 |

The case stays positive all the way down to a 10 percent auto-clear rate, which is worth understanding rather than celebrating. It stays positive because the packet preparation is doing the work, and packet preparation works even when the agent gets the answer wrong. The agent does not have to be right to be useful; it has to make the next human decision faster.

That is also why the case is fragile in one specific way. Remove the validator gate and let the agent's proposal reach the analyst as an untrusted draft, and the analyst re-does the full 22 minutes to check it. Hours freed drops to zero. The system now costs $13,053 a month and saves nothing, which is the exact shape of most failed pilots: a capable model, no gate, and a workflow nobody redesigned.

Add the error line and the picture sharpens further. Suppose 0.2 percent of auto-cleared cases turn out to be wrong in a way that costs money, say a duplicate payment averaging $11,000. That is roughly 2.4 cases a month, or $26,400 a month in expected loss, which turns the best case in the table into a loss. This is why the gate is not a nice-to-have and why "acceptance rate" has to be measured on outcomes, not on whether the output looked plausible.

![Sensitivity of net monthly benefit to the auto-clear rate: 40,763 dollars at 60 percent falls to 13,173 dollars at 10 percent and stays positive. Removing the validator gate drops hours freed to zero while the cost stays at 13,053 dollars a month, and 0.2 percent of auto-cleared cases being wrong at 11,000 dollars each adds 26,400 dollars a month in expected loss.](</diagrams/roi-business-case-enterprise-ai/5-sensitivity-break.png>)

### Step 6: what the spreadsheet still does not contain

Cycle time. Exceptions resolved in two hours instead of four days change the discount capture rate and the supplier relationship, and neither is in the model above.

Capacity quality. Those 732 hours are only worth $62 an hour if the work they get redirected into is at least as valuable. Gartner's finding that only 21 percent of freed time goes to more innovative work is a warning, not a footnote.

Option value. The evals, the policy allowlist and the review UI are reusable for the next four workflows. That is real, and it is also exactly the kind of claim that inflates cases, so cap it and label it.

## The document itself

Seven sections, in the order a CFO reads rather than the order engineers write.

**1. The decision requested.** One sentence: approve $X on date Y to reach outcome Z, reviewed at gate G. If the first page does not contain a decision, the reader stops.

**2. The baseline, measured.** Where the numbers came from, how many weeks of data, median and 90th percentile. State plainly that this is measured, not modeled.

**3. What changes in the workflow.** The before and after, step by step, including who does what. A business case that cannot describe the new workflow in a diagram is not ready.

**4. The cost stack, at p50 and p90.** Every line from the table above, plus the ones that did not make the base case. Show the inference line separately so it can be re-priced without rebuilding the document.

**5. The benefit, with the conversion rule.** Hours to dollars only where a role is redeployed or a vacancy is not backfilled. Capacity gains labeled as capacity. Revenue attributed only with a mechanism you can name.

**6. Sensitivity and break-even.** The table from step 5, plus the one sentence that matters most: at what acceptance rate does this stop paying for itself, and how far is that from the measured rate?

**7. Kill criteria and gates.** Written before launch, with dates and thresholds. If first-pass acceptance is below X at day 60, the program stops. Vague commitment is how zombie pilots survive for three years.

McKinsey's four-step measurement architecture fits inside section 2 and section 7 and is worth adopting wholesale: inventory the agents and their owners, define quantifiable KPIs in business terms and get the business sponsor to sign their name to them before launch, instrument at the workflow and agent-step level rather than only at the bill, and decide explicitly who owns the measurement architecture.[^5] The signature is the load-bearing part. An unsigned KPI gets renegotiated the moment the first result is inconvenient.

One practice from McKinsey's review of more than 50 agentic builds is worth repeating here: agents need onboarding the way a new hire does, with a job description, a feedback loop and continuing evaluation. The article's phrasing is that there is no launch-and-leave.[^6] Budget for that labour in the cost stack or the benefit number will decay after month four.

## Three ways business cases lie

The denominator swap. Reporting cost per token or per seat while the benefit is stated per task. It makes the cost look small and the benefit look large, and the two numbers never have to meet.

Baseline inflation. Comparing against the worst month, or against a hypothetical fully optimized process instead of the one that exists. The tell is a baseline with no measurement window attached.

Benefit double counting. The same hour counted once as cash saved and again as capacity created. It is the most common error in AI business cases and the easiest to catch: sum the benefit lines and ask whether the total implies headcount went down.

There is a fourth, harder to name and more common than the other three: building the case backwards. Someone decides the program is happening, then the document is written to support it. This is detectable from the outside, because a real business case contains at least one line that hurts. If nothing in the document makes the program look worse, it was written after the decision.

## Try it

Pick one workflow you own that has a measurable queue. Do this in a week.

1. Pull two weeks of timestamps for the human version. Compute median and 90th percentile handling time per item, plus the monthly volume.
2. Write down the loaded hourly cost of the role, including overhead.
3. Sketch the workflow with one bounded agent step and name the validator gate that decides whether its output is used automatically.
4. Estimate the token count per item honestly, including retries and re-sent context. Multiply by your provider's current rate card.
5. Compute cost per accepted output at your measured acceptance rate, then at 60 percent of it.
6. Write the seven-section document on one page, with a p50 and a p90 cost line and one kill criterion with a date.
7. Show it to the finance partner who will actually review the spend, and ask one question: which line would you push back on first?

The answer to question 7 is the most valuable input you will get all quarter. It tells you which assumption your case is standing on.

## Summary

- ROI, payback, NPV and TCO answer different questions. Present all four, labeled, or expect the argument to be about which one you meant.
- Published statistics do not stack. The 95 percent MIT figure, the 39 percent McKinsey EBIT figure and the 77 percent board expectation figure measure different things and cannot be combined into one claim.
- Build the cost side first, at p50 and p90. Agentic cost behaves as a distribution, roughly 60 percent of it sits in refinement, and orchestration choices move it by an order of magnitude.
- Cost per accepted output is the denominator that makes the rest computable. Cost per token is a bill, and cost per seat is a SaaS habit.
- In the worked example, a 4.5 month payback depends less on how often the agent is right than on whether the workflow removed human lookup work. Take away the validator gate and the case goes from strongly positive to negative at the same model quality.
- Kill criteria written before launch, with dates and thresholds, are what separate a business case from a wish.
