# Lesson 82: Change Management: Training, Adoption, and Feedback Loops

Lesson 81 ended with a 4.5 month payback and one buried sentence: the case assumes analysts work the new queue the way it was designed. That sentence is where most programs die. A good model, a calibrated validator gate and a cost model that survives a finance committee can still produce a queue that goes unused, or gets used in a way that quietly hands every saved hour back.

Change management has a reputation problem in engineering circles and it earned that reputation. The phrase usually arrives attached to a town hall, a slide deck and a page of instructions, and McKinsey's assessment of that version is that it can create awareness but rarely builds "the trust, habits, and operating discipline required to scale agentic ways of working."[^1] What follows is the working version: the three jobs hidden under one label, what the published evidence actually shows, and a 90-day program for the invoice exception queue that ends in a measurement you can argue with instead of a feeling.

## Three jobs under one label

Training, adoption and reinforcement get used interchangeably in planning documents. They are different problems, they fail differently, and each one needs its own artifact.

**Training is capability.** It answers whether a person can do the new task under the conditions the task actually happens in. Its artifact is a proficiency check that a named person either passes or does not, on real work, before they are counted as ready.

**Adoption is behavior at scale.** It answers whether the new way of working survives a busy Tuesday, after whoever designed it stops watching. Its artifact is telemetry tied to a named behavior, not a login count.

**Reinforcement is the loop.** It answers what happens to the tenth correction a user makes, and whether the system is different because of it. Its artifact is a change to the system: an eval case, a policy line, a tool fix, or a training module.

Prosci's ADKAR model names five individual outcomes and is worth reading as a diagnostic rather than a brand: awareness, desire, knowledge, ability, reinforcement. The failure they describe is specific, and it is the one this lesson spends most of its length on. AI rollouts rarely stall at knowledge. They stall in the gap between knowledge and ability, because "training completion indicates that employees received information, not whether they can apply it independently, under real conditions, in the workflows that matter most."[^2]

That distinction sounds like semantics until you are the one designing the training. A completion rate is a report about the program. A proficiency gate is a claim about the person, and it can be wrong in a way that shows up in production.

## What the published evidence says

Five figures frame this problem, and each one measures something different. They do not stack, so keep them apart.

Gartner surveyed 11,838 employees in the first quarter of 2026 and found that 47 percent of the employees who are expected to use AI in their work report resistance to adopting it. The same research found that employees broadly agree AI is good for their organization while being less convinced that it is good for them.[^3] That split is the adoption problem in one sentence. Resistance in 2026 is rarely a belief that the technology does not work. It is a belief about what the technology does to the person using it.

Gartner's 2027 CIO and Technology Executive Agenda survey of 2,002 CIOs and technology executives, run from May to July 2026, found that only 13 percent of CIOs report their enterprise has realized significant or transformational value from AI, and that the likelihood of any given use case succeeding remains about one in five.[^4] Spending is not the constraint. Attention is being spent on technology while capability building is treated as an implementation detail.

Prosci's research puts a multiplier on the people work: projects applying effective change management are seven times more likely to meet their objectives, and projects with extremely effective sponsors were 79 percent likely to meet their objectives against 27 percent where the sponsor was extremely ineffective.[^5] Read that pair carefully, because it has an uncomfortable implication. The sponsor is not a communications channel. The sponsor is the person who can move money, headcount and priorities, and a program without one of those will be starved at the first budget review no matter how good the training is.

Prosci's survey of 1,107 professionals supplies the numbers that sit closest to the work. Sixty-three percent of organizations cite human factors as a primary challenge in AI adoption, and within those factors user proficiency is the single largest challenge, cited by 38 percent of respondents, more than double technical integration issues at 16 percent and organizational adoption challenges at 15 percent.[^2] The same research found trust in AI varies sharply by role, running at +1.09 among executives against +0.33 among frontline workers, which is the 47 percent resistance figure wearing a different hat. None of this is a soft risk that sits behind the technical risk. On the evidence it is the larger one.

## The 1:3:5 pattern

Here is the sentence to take from this lesson if you take nothing else. McKinsey's finding is that successful AI transformations spend roughly one dollar on agentic technology for every three dollars on process redesign and every five dollars on capability building and adoption, and that most companies invert that ratio, putting technology first in attention and money while behavior change is treated as a detail to handle at go-live.[^1]

You do not need to adopt 1:3:5 as a budgeting rule to use it as a diagnostic. Take the number the program has actually spent on the model, the integrations and the platform, then look at what it has spent on redesigning the workflow and on teaching people to run it. If the first number is the largest of the three, the program is buying a capability it has not decided how to absorb. On the invoice queue from lesson 81, a $180,000 build with a $3,000 run-and-eval line and no line for process redesign or capability building is a program that has already chosen its failure mode.

McKinsey also names the sequence that capability building follows, and it is worth internalizing as a checklist because the names are plain. Awareness, so people can describe what is changing and why. Belief, driven by leaders who use the tool in public and by trusted peers who demonstrate it is safe. Commitment, which requires protected time and an honest acknowledgment that a role is being redesigned. Develop, the stage where habits form. Then enforce, where the new behavior is wired into the operating system through role definitions, review rituals and metrics.[^1]

Two of those stages get skipped in almost every rollout. Commitment is skipped because it costs calendar time and looks like hesitation. Enforce is skipped because the pilot ends and everyone moves to the next thing.

The belief stage is where our own publish pipeline offers a small real example. When this site hit a hard cap of 100 production deploys a day, the fix was not a reminder to merge less often. The publish ritual changed shape: content commits queue onto a holding branch and a scheduled job flushes them to main three times a day. Nobody had to believe in the new habit. The tool made the new habit the path of least resistance, which is the only version of a behavior change that holds under deadline pressure.

## Training that survives contact with a Tuesday

Incumbent training for an agent rollout tends to be a two-hour session on how to write a prompt, delivered once, before the workflow changes. McKinsey's assessment of that format is blunt: one-time training is poorly suited to creating work habits for a technology that changes every few weeks, and the organizations doing this well are moving from classroom delivery to coaching embedded in the work itself. Their example is a Fortune 500 technology company that put AI coaches directly into engineering teams at a ratio of one coach for every five to ten employees, with the coaches sitting in sprint ceremonies and retrospectives, troubleshooting real workflow problems and refining review practice rather than teaching prompting in the abstract.[^1]

Two fears drive most of the quiet non-adoption that a completion dashboard cannot see, and both are worth designing against explicitly.

The first is the fear of being found out. Most people do not know where to start with an agentic tool, and admitting that in front of a colleague can feel like announcing you are behind. The observed result is not refusal. It is paralysis dressed up as busyness, where people stay quiet, wait to see what others do, and learn in private or not at all.[^1] This is why open experimentation has to be the leader's visible behavior before it is anyone else's expectation, and why "show me how you used it this week, including where it failed" is a better standing agenda item than "any questions."

The second is the fear of accountability without control. An agent drafts, recommends and occasionally decides, but when a recommendation is wrong and someone acts on it, the mistake lands on the person who acted. That fear is rational, and it cannot be trained away with enthusiasm. It is answered with a review protocol: a written statement of what the agent is allowed to do unsupervised, what a human must check, what happens when the two disagree, and who owns the outcome when the agent is wrong. If nobody can answer those four questions about your workflow, the training has not started yet, because the answer is what people are actually waiting for.

### A proficiency gate instead of an attendance sheet

The replaceable artifact in a training program is the attendance record. The useful one is a signed, repeatable check that a person can run the new workflow on real cases and hold judgment where the workflow requires it.

A gate that works has four parts, and each one is checkable:

- A real evidence set. Twenty historical cases from the actual queue, including the ugly ones: a duplicate, a missing purchase order, a bank detail mismatch, and at least one case where the correct action was to escalate rather than resolve.
- A scored dry run. The person works the twenty cases in the new interface with the agent's packet in front of them, and a reviewer scores the decisions, not the keystrokes.
- A stated threshold. Something like eighteen of twenty decisions matching the reviewer, with no unsafe action taken on any case, and a written note on any case where the person deliberately departed from the packet.
- A retry path with a date. Failing the gate means a second attempt inside a week with the specific gap named, not a general invitation to review the material.

The point of the gate is not gatekeeping. It is that "trained" becomes a claim with an owner, and a team where the claim is false finds out in a dry run instead of in a payment error.

## Adoption: measure behavior, not logins

The default adoption report is a login count, and it is nearly useless. It measures that a browser session happened rather than that the new path was used, and a confused month of clicking around produces the same number as a month of genuine use. Two things have to be true before the number means anything: it has to be tied to a named behavior in the workflow, and it has to be attributable to a team, so the response can be targeted.

### The champion trap

The standard rollout pattern is to find the two most enthusiastic analysts, make them champions, and let their energy carry the team. It works for about six weeks. The failure is structural rather than personal: a champion's enthusiasm is not a system, so when the champion changes teams, takes leave or simply gets busy, adoption reverts to whatever the default path is. If the default path is the old queue, the program is one transfer request away from zero.

The durable version puts the weight on design rather than on people. The agent's packet is the first thing an analyst sees when they open an exception, so working from it takes less effort than rebuilding the context. The old path is still available, but it costs more clicks, not fewer. Adoption then depends on the tool being the easier route, and the champion becomes a source of improvements instead of the load-bearing wall.

A second fixed habit worth building is keeping champions paired. Two champions per team means one absence does not end the program, and it also prevents the single-champion pattern where improvements only flow through one person's opinions.

### The three numbers that describe adoption

Prosci's measurement triad for the individual side of change translates directly into agent telemetry, and it is better than anything invented from scratch: ultimate utilization, speed of adoption and proficiency. Their point is that these human factors drive or constrain the value a change creates, and that leaders routinely assume all three will reach 100 percent on their own once the solution ships.[^5]

| Measure | What it answers | The telemetry that carries it |
|---|---|---|
| Speed of adoption | How long until the new way is the normal way | Median days from access to first unassisted use, by team |
| Ultimate utilization | What share of the eligible work flows through the new path | Exceptions worked from the agent packet divided by exceptions opened |
| Proficiency | How well people apply it, not how often | Sample review pass rate; rate of cases where an analyst rebuilds context the packet already contained |

That last column matters more than the metric names. A login count tells you a browser session happened. The rate at which analysts rebuild context the packet already contained tells you whether the design is being used or merely tolerated, and it moves in response to real fixes.

One thing to refuse in this section: a dashboard that reports adoption as a single percentage. Speed, utilization and proficiency move independently, and a team can sit at high utilization with poor proficiency for months, producing exactly the rework cost that lesson 81's denominator model was built to catch.

## The feedback loop: corrections into eval cases

This is the part that separates a program that improves from one that ages. Every user correction is either a gift or a complaint, and which one it becomes depends on where it goes.

Anthropic's guidance on agent evaluation states the mechanism in a sentence worth memorizing: if the system is already in production, look at the bug tracker and the support queue, because converting user-reported failures into test cases is what makes the suite reflect actual usage, and prioritizing by user impact tells you where the effort belongs.[^6] The compounding runs in both directions. Failures become test cases, and test cases stop the same failure coming back the next time the model, the prompts or a tool change underneath the workflow.

The second habit is the one most teams skip. Triage feedback constantly, sample transcripts to read weekly, and treat reading them as the work rather than as an optional review, because when a task fails the transcript is what tells you whether the agent made a genuine mistake or whether the grader rejected a valid solution.[^6] That second case is the expensive one. A team that never reads transcripts can spend a quarter repairing a system that was right.

Two pieces of plumbing make that routine instead of heroic, and both are design decisions rather than purchases. Runs have to be traceable and addressable, so a complaint can be resolved back to the exact run rather than to a description of it. And the route from a flagged run into the eval set should be an automation rule wherever the trigger is checkable, so the path from correction to test case does not depend on someone remembering to take it.

### The intake schema

Free-text feedback is where loops go to die. A correction that arrives as "the totals were off again" in a chat message becomes an anecdote, and anecdotes do not become tests. Five fields are enough to make feedback routable:

- The trace identifier, so the exact run can be replayed later.
- The workflow step, chosen from a fixed list rather than typed.
- What was wrong, in one of four buckets: retrieval missed evidence, the tool returned the wrong shape, the output was correct but unusable, or the policy was wrong.
- The corrected answer, which is the only field that becomes an eval case directly.
- Whether the decision was unsafe, which routes the item out of the weekly queue and into the incident path immediately.

The fourth field is the load-bearing one. A corrected answer is a labeled example, and a labeled example is the thing an offline eval needs. A complaint with no corrected answer is a bug report, which is useful, and it is a different artifact from a test case.

### The weekly loop

A loop that runs on a schedule beats a loop that runs on enthusiasm. Our own lesson pipeline works this way by construction: every lesson ships with a three-question quiz attached to it, and the course's knowledge checks and final exam are regenerated from the complete set each time a lesson publishes, so the tiers covering the whole course rebuild themselves as the course grows. The machine-checkable output is what keeps it honest. A lesson that published without its quiz file would make the regenerate step fail loudly rather than quietly leaving a gap in the course.

The path to a weekly version is short. Forty-five minutes, once a week, with the following shape:

1. Show the week's routed items, with the count by bucket, so the meeting starts with the distribution instead of the loudest example.
2. Promote at least one corrected answer into the offline eval set, and require it to be named in the notes.
3. Close or escalate each item with an owner and a date. Anything unresolved twice goes to the platform backlog with the reason attached.
4. Read two transcripts end to end, chosen at random rather than chosen because they failed, and note what the agent did that nobody asked for.
5. Report one number: how many corrections turned into a test, a policy line, a tool fix or a training change. If that number is zero for two weeks, the loop is decorative.

That last step is what keeps the meeting from becoming a status update. The output of a feedback loop is a change to the system, and a loop with no changes is a complaint channel with better branding.

## Reinforcement, or decay

Adoption decays, and it decays fastest after the first visible failure. McKinsey describes the mechanism directly: when trust breaks, from a poor decision, a policy reversal or a model failure, employees can slide back from the committed stages toward awareness, and the correct response is to identify where people are on that curve and intervene at the right stage rather than pushing harder on the next one.[^1] You do not fix a lost-trust problem with more training. You fix it by showing what changed in the system.

Two design decisions make decay visible while you can still act on it.

The first is a stopping rule, decided before launch, with a date and a threshold. Gartner's CIO research found that organizations with guidelines for deciding when a pilot should not move to production were 2.3 times more likely to be high performers, those with guidelines for abandoning a low-value initiative already in production were 2.6 times more likely, and only 31 percent and 17 percent respectively had either practice in place.[^4] A program with no stopping rule cannot be defended, because there is no version of the story in which it ends.

The second is a stated proficiency review. Rerun the dry run eight weeks after go-live on twenty fresh cases, with no notice, and compare the pass rate to the launch gate. A drop is not a disciplinary signal. It is the signal that the workflow drifted, the policy changed, or the packets degraded, and it is available weeks before the error rate shows up in the ledger.

## Worked example: 90 days on the invoice queue

Back to lesson 81's queue: 24,000 invoices a month, 3,000 exceptions, six AP analysts at a 22 minute median, a validator gate auto-clearing 40 percent, and a projected 732 hours a month freed. Assume the build is done and the gate is measured. The question is whether the freed hours are real.

### Days 1 to 15: define the change from the role's side

Write the from-to for each role before writing any training material, because training that describes a tool rather than a changed job produces people who can narrate the tool.

| Role | From | To |
|---|---|---|
| AP analyst | Find the PO, check receiving, email the business owner, post the decision | Read the evidence packet, verify the cited records exist, decide and post; write one correction for anything the packet got wrong |
| AP lead | Allocate the queue each morning, absorb escalations | Own the weekly triage, own the sample review, own the stopping rule report |
| Business owner | Answer email chains about quantity mismatches | Answer a structured question with the evidence attached, or be escalated to by name |
| Finance controller | See a monthly exception and error summary | Read a weekly line on auto-clear rate, sample pass rate and unsafe decisions |

The last row is the one programs forget. If the controller does not see the unsafe-decision count weekly, the first sighting comes at month-end, and the response will be to disable the gate.

### Days 16 to 45: train, then gate

Three sessions, all short, all built on the queue's own cases.

| Session | Length | Content | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| Why and what changes | 60 min | The from-to above, the gate's rules, what the agent can and cannot do unsupervised | Written questions collected, answered in writing |
| Working the packet | 90 min | Live cases in the new interface, including two where the packet is wrong | First attempt at the proficiency gate |
| Judgment and escalation | 90 min | The four buckets, when to escalate instead of resolve, how to write a correction | Gate retake and sign-off |

Then two weeks of embedded coaching rather than a follow-up class: the lead sits with each analyst for two sessions, reviews their corrections, and feeds the first routing items into the triage list. Six analysts at two sessions each comes to roughly ten hours of lead time in the fortnight, which is the pattern McKinsey describes as one coach for every five to ten employees, compressed into a short window.[^1]

Set the gate at eighteen of twenty decisions matching the reviewer, no unsafe action on any case, and a written note on deliberate departures. Six analysts, six gates, six signatures with dates.

### Days 46 to 75: run the three numbers

Baseline the telemetry in week one, then read it weekly:

| Week | Speed (median days to first unassisted use) | Utilization (exceptions worked from packet) | Proficiency (sample pass rate) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7 | 3 | 71% | 86% |
| 8 | 3 | 78% | 88% |
| 9 | 2 | 84% | 89% |
| 10 | 2 | 86% | 91% |

These are illustrative figures, and the shape is the point rather than the values. Utilization climbing while proficiency moves slowly is the normal pattern, and it is also the pattern that hides rework: if proficiency sits at 86 percent, roughly one in seven packets is being corrected, and those corrections are the raw material for the weekly loop.

Two telemetry details earn their keep here. Track the rate at which analysts rebuild context the packet already contained, because that number tells you whether the packet is trusted rather than merely present. Track the unsafe-decision count separately from the error rate, because a wrong-but-harmless resolution and a duplicate payment are not the same event and should not share a denominator.

### Days 76 to 90: prove the loop closed

The 90-day review is not a satisfaction survey. It is four questions with countable answers.

1. How many analyst corrections became offline eval cases, and did the pass rate on those cases change after the fix?
2. How many became policy changes, and how many became tool fixes?
3. Did the sample pass rate rise after those changes, with the weeks before and after shown side by side?
4. What is the stopping-rule reading today, against the threshold set before launch?

If the answer to the third question is no, the loop is capturing without closing, which is the most common way a well-run feedback process produces nothing.

## Try it

Pick one workflow where an agent is live or about to go live, and pick a team of fewer than ten people. Do this in three weeks.

1. Write the from-to for every role the workflow touches, including the manager two levels up. If a role has an empty "to" column, the change is not designed yet.
2. Replace your training plan's completion metric with a gate: twenty real cases, a stated threshold, a named reviewer, and a date.
3. Define the three adoption numbers on telemetry you already collect. If you cannot name the query that produces each one, you do not have the metric.
4. Write the five-field intake schema and route it to one person. Do not build a queue before you have a person.
5. Book the 45 minute weekly triage for the next eight weeks, and put "corrections converted" first on the agenda.
6. Write one stopping rule with a date and a threshold, and get the sponsor to say out loud what happens when it triggers.
7. At day 90, ask the four review questions above and write down whichever one has no answer.

Step 7 is the useful one. Whichever question you cannot answer is the part of your change program that exists in principle only.

## Summary

- Training, adoption and reinforcement are three jobs. Each needs a different artifact: a proficiency check, behavior telemetry, and a change to the system.
- The published evidence points the same way. 47 percent of employees expected to use AI report resistance, 38 percent of adoption challenges come from proficiency rather than technology, and change management quality carries a large multiplier on outcomes.
- Successful programs spend roughly one dollar on agentic technology for every three on process redesign and every five on capability building. Most programs invert that.
- One-time classroom training does not build habits for a system that changes every few weeks. Embedded coaching at a low ratio, plus a gate on real cases, does.
- Adoption should be measured as speed, utilization and proficiency, never as logins and never as a single percentage.
- A correction becomes valuable when it becomes an eval case. The weekly loop earns its place by producing changes, not by producing notes.
- Adoption decays after the first visible failure. A stopping rule written before launch and a surprise proficiency retake at week eight are how you see it coming.
