# Lesson 84: Capstone I: Designing an Enterprise Agent End to End

Lesson 83 finished with a review that approved a design with three conditions attached. It assumed somebody already had a design to defend. The capstone sequence is where that design comes from, and this time nobody hands you a workflow at the end of the chapter.

The capstone has two halves. This lesson is the design half. You pick a workflow, run it past a selection screen, and produce a packet that a reviewer could approve or reject on evidence. Lesson 85 is the build half: the thinnest end-to-end implementation of whatever you designed here.

A **design packet** is the set of documents somebody needs in order to approve a system: the business case with a measured baseline, the scope boundary, an explicit decision per action about what the agent may do unsupervised, the evaluation plan with thresholds, the economics, the failure modes with named owners, and the reversal plan. Requirements describe what the system should do. A packet states what it will do, what it will not do, and what a wrong answer costs. That last part is the one first drafts leave out, and it is the part a reviewer reads first.

Everything you need is already in the course. Lessons 71 to 76 covered platform, cost, security, reliability and audit. Lessons 77 to 80 covered orchestration, oversight and regulation. Lessons 81 to 83 covered the business case, adoption and review. The capstone is the assembly step. The work below is seven steps that take roughly half a day, and each one carries a test you can fail honestly.

## Step 1: pick a workflow that can pay for its own build

Most failed agent projects did not fail at the architecture. They failed at the choice of workflow, before anyone wrote code. Five screens filter the choice, and two of them are non-negotiable.

**Volume.** Count the units of work per month, where a unit is one ticket, one invoice, one claim, one contract, one renewal. A per-run improvement only becomes money when it multiplies. A workflow that runs forty times a month cannot carry a build cost, however good the per-run number looks.

**Variance.** Is the decision judgment-shaped or rule-shaped? OpenAI's practical guide to building agents sorts the candidates into three buckets: decisions that involve nuanced judgment or exceptions, rulesets that have grown too brittle to maintain, and work that leans on unstructured data such as documents and free text.[^1] If the work is a field lookup with a stable mapping, a deterministic job wins and the agent is the wrong tool. This screen has an obvious corollary: if you cannot name the specific ambiguity the agent resolves, you have not found the use case yet.

**A measurable baseline.** You need two things here, and the second is the one people forget. First, the numbers for today: volume, median handling time, error or rework rate, cost per unit. Second, somewhere between one hundred and three hundred historical units with the real outcome recorded beside them, so that which tickets were resolved correctly, which invoices were genuinely fraudulent, which claims were paid as filed, all exist in a form you can score against. If nobody wrote the outcome down, you cannot measure the agent, and an agent you cannot measure is a demo with a longer shelf life.

**Error asymmetry.** The question is not whether the workflow is important. It is what one wrong output costs and whether it can be undone. A wrong draft that a human sends costs an apology. A wrong access grant that stands for a week costs a security incident. This screen does not eliminate workflows. It sets the autonomy ceiling you will have to defend in step 3, and it names the actions that need a human in the loop on day one.

**An owner.** One named person in the business who pays for the build, accepts the residual risk, and signs off on the autonomy question. A workflow without an owner fails this screen outright. It is the most common reason a technically excellent candidate never ships, and no amount of architecture repairs it.

Score each screen from one to five. Reject outright on the baseline and on the owner, because no score rescues either one. Then take the highest total rather than the most interesting candidate. Interesting workflows are usually interesting because they are hard, and this capstone is about producing a defensible design, not an impressive one.

## Step 2: write the brief in ten parts

The packet runs six to ten pages and has ten parts. Each part gets one page at most, and if a part will not fit on a page, that part of the design is not decided yet.

1. **Unit of work and baseline.** What one unit is, how many run per month, what each one costs today, and the target the design is aiming at.
2. **Scope boundary.** What version one does, plus an explicit list of what it does not do. The out-list is what keeps a review to 45 minutes, and it is the section reviewers test first.
3. **The autonomy decision per action.** Not a sentence about the agent being semi-autonomous. One row per action with the rung, the evidence that permitted it, and the cap on blast radius. Step 3 has the detail.
4. **Architecture, at the lowest level of complexity that works.** Microsoft's orchestration guidance puts agent systems on a spectrum from a direct model call, through a single agent with tools, to multi-agent orchestration, and advises using the lowest level that reliably meets the requirement, because each level up adds coordination overhead, latency and failure modes of its own.[^2] OpenAI's guide says the same thing from the other direction: start with a single agent and add specialists only when the single agent provably cannot carry the work.[^1] Write down which simpler level you rejected, and why it was not enough.
5. **Tools and permissions**, one row per tool: what it reads, what it writes, which credential it uses, and the damage a wrong call can do before anyone notices.
6. **Context plan.** What the agent reads at run time, where that data lives, how fresh it must be, and what gets retained afterward. Retention is a design decision with a legal owner, not an implementation detail to settle later.
7. **Model choice and cost envelope**, expressed per unit of work rather than per token, because per-token numbers hide retries.
8. **Failure modes with detection signals.** Not a list of fears: the mode, the signal that reveals it, and the person who owns the response.
9. **Oversight and operations.** The named on-call owner, what the dashboard shows, where the kill switch sits, and the escalation path for when the agent refuses or the human disagrees.
10. **Evaluation plan, rollout, rollback, and the decision records.** Thresholds with numbers, the staged rollout, the way back out, and records for the choices that will be argued about later.

The whole thing maps onto a risk frame you may already be required to use. NIST's AI Risk Management Framework organizes AI risk work into four functions, GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE and MANAGE, with governance running across the other three.[^4] The packet is the MAP and MEASURE work in written form, and the owner from step 1 is the GOVERN function with a phone number.

## Step 3: decide autonomy per action, not per agent

"The agent will be semi-autonomous" is not a design decision, because it never tells an operator what the agent may do at 2am. Autonomy belongs to actions. One agent can sit at four different rungs depending on which tool it is reaching for.

| Rung | What the agent may do | Evidence required to reach it | Blast-radius cap |
|---|---|---|---|
| L0 | Retrieve and summarize, no writes | None, read-only by construction | No write surface |
| L1 | Draft the action, a human sends it | A working draft and a human queue | The reviewer's time |
| L2 | Act, then notify a human | Agreement with the human decision on the held-out set clears the threshold in the evaluation plan | Small, reversible, capped per day |
| L3 | Act inside a written bound | The L2 rung held for 30 days with the production override rate inside the threshold set at design time, and the bound written down | Bounded by the rule, not by the day |
| L4 | Act across the whole scope, sampled afterward | The L3 rung held for a quarter with no reversal events | The full scope, under monitoring |

Four rules make the table usable. Set the rung per action, because the permission on a read tool and the permission on a payment endpoint are different decisions. Require measured evidence to climb, so the ladder cannot be climbed by argument. Write the cap, because "reversible" is not a number. Plan the descent, since demotion happens on a schedule set at design time rather than in the week something goes wrong.

AWS's Well-Architected Agentic AI Lens frames the same idea in two phrases worth borrowing outright: bounded autonomy, where an agent operates inside explicitly defined scope boundaries and guardrails that hold regardless of the input it receives, and tiered oversight, where the level of human review matches the risk and reversibility of each action.[^3] The lens also gives the reason autonomy belongs per action rather than per agent: agent behavior is stochastic, the same input can produce different outputs across runs, and reliability therefore comes from monitoring and evaluation rather than from the fact that a path worked once.[^3]

## Step 4: build the evaluation plan before the architecture

Teams that leave evaluation until the end build a system that cannot be measured, and the retrofit is expensive because the eval set shapes the architecture.

Start with the history. Pull 150 units with recorded outcomes and split them: 100 for development, 50 held back and never used to tune prompts. The held-out split is the only honest number you will produce, and it stops being honest the moment somebody tunes against it.[^6] Then add the cases history does not contain, which is where the interesting failures live: a ticket body carrying instructions aimed at the agent, the same request arriving twice inside a minute, a record deleted between the retrieval step and the write step, and a request that is routine except for the account it belongs to.

Six metrics cover most enterprise workflows, and each one needs a threshold and a consequence:

- **Task completion**: did the unit finish without human rescue?
- **Tool-call correctness**: the right tool, the right arguments, the right order. Microsoft Foundry ships this as a built-in agent evaluator alongside task completion, a useful reminder that tool selection is a measurable behavior rather than an implementation detail.[^5]
- **Groundedness**: every claim in the output traces back to a retrieved record.
- **Override rate**: how often the human changed the agent's answer. NIST notes that the frequency and rationale with which humans overrule a system's output is worth collecting and analyzing, which is the case for recording it from day one instead of after the first incident.[^4]
- **Cost per unit**, including retries and evaluation calls.
- **p95 latency**, measured against the point in the workflow where the human is waiting.

Grade in the cheapest order that is reliable: code-based checks first, human grading only where a rule cannot decide, and a model judge only after its agreement with human labels has been measured.[^6] Write thresholds as numbers with consequences attached. "Above 92 percent on the held-out set, and any drop of more than two points pauses the rollout" is a design statement. "High accuracy" is not.

Say each criterion out loud in a form that can fail. Anthropic's evaluation guidance asks for criteria that are specific, measurable, achievable and relevant, and draws the contrast directly: safe outputs is not a criterion, while fewer than 0.1 percent of outputs flagged by the content filter across 10,000 trials is.[^6] Run that test over every line of your evaluation plan before showing it to anybody.

![Building the evaluation plan: 150 recorded units split into 100 development and 50 held back, four adversarial cases history does not contain, then grading in the cheapest reliable order](</diagrams/capstone-i-designing-an-enterprise-agent-end-to-end/3-evaluation-plan.png>)

## Step 5: put the economics on one page

One formula, and every number labelled as measured or estimated.

Monthly net = (minutes per unit today minus minutes per unit after) multiplied by units per month and the loaded cost per minute, minus run cost.

Run cost has four lines that people underestimate. Model calls per unit, multiplied by retries. The nightly evaluation suite, which runs whether or not anyone is using the system. Infrastructure, meaning trace storage, retrieval index, orchestration and monitoring. And oversight sampling, the human minutes spent reviewing a share of the automated decisions, which stays a permanent line item for as long as the ladder sits above L1.

Two habits keep the page credible. Put a date on every measured figure, because a baseline from eighteen months ago describes a different business. And keep one sensitivity line at the bottom: what the payback becomes if the automation share lands at half your estimate, since that is the number a finance reviewer will compute anyway.

## Step 6: the worked example

The IT service desk. Three thousand tickets a month, a median of 18 minutes to resolve, and a loaded analyst cost of $45 an hour.

Two candidates failed the screen before this one. The quarterly board pack summary runs four times a year, so volume removed it. Closing every ticket end to end failed on scope rather than on appeal: the queue contains access grants, procurement requests and terminations, three actions whose blast radius cannot be undone by a rollback. The accepted version is narrower, and the out-list does most of the work.

**Scope.** Version one handles triage and resolution drafting for the six highest-volume intents, which together cover 62 percent of the queue: password resets on managed devices, software installation requests from the approved catalog, VPN and connectivity triage, printer and peripheral faults, license queries, and new-starter account provisioning for standard roles. Everything outside those six routes to a human with the triage summary attached. Access grants above the standard role template, terminations and security incidents sit in the out-list, and the out-list ships in the packet rather than in someone's memory.

**Autonomy rows.**

- Password reset on a managed device, verified user, no privileged group: L2, act and notify, capped at 200 resets a day, because the action is reversible and the evidence is strong.
- Software installation from the approved catalog: L1, draft the fulfillment request for a human to send, since one wrong package is expensive to unwind.
- Standard new-starter provisioning: L1 for version one, with a named path to L2 after 30 days of clean runs, because the ticket history provides a labelled set to measure against.
- Access grant above the standard template: L0. The agent retrieves the policy and prepares the evidence packet, and a human decides.

**Evaluation plan.** One hundred and fifty tickets with recorded outcomes, split 100 and 50, plus the adversarial set: instruction text inside the ticket body, duplicate submissions, a stale device record, and a reset requested for a user in a privileged group. Thresholds: task completion above 90 percent on the held-out split, tool-call correctness above 97 percent, groundedness above 95 percent, override rate below 8 percent for the L2 action during the pilot, p95 latency under 25 seconds, and cost under 40 cents per ticket.

**Economics.** Today the queue consumes 900 analyst hours a month, which is $40,500 at the loaded rate. The design assumes 30 percent of volume auto-resolved at L2, and the rest handled in 11 minutes with the draft and the triage summary in front of the analyst, which comes to 385 hours, or $17,325. Gross monthly reduction: $23,175. Run cost: $150 in model calls at five cents a ticket, $150 for the nightly evaluation suite, $350 for infrastructure, and $135 for sampling 10 percent of the automated decisions at two minutes each. That is $785 a month, which puts the net at roughly $22,400 a month. Against a quoted build of $110,000, the payback lands under five months. The automation share and the 11 minutes are both estimates, and the packet says so on the page.

![The worked example economics: 900 analyst hours today becoming 385, a gross reduction of 23,175 dollars, 785 dollars of monthly run cost across four lines, and a net of 22,400 dollars against a 110,000 dollar build](</diagrams/capstone-i-designing-an-enterprise-agent-end-to-end/4-worked-example-economics.png>)

**Conditions to ship.** The eval set exists before the first line of production code. The password reset action carries an idempotency key and a per-day cap. The out-list routes with a triage summary attached instead of a rejection. And the L2 authority is reviewed at 30 days against the measured override rate, which is the same 30-day check lesson 83 put on its threshold record.

## Step 7: grade the packet before a reviewer does

Score yourself honestly, because the rubric is worth nothing if it is not adversarial. One hundred points across eight rows.

| Row | What full credit requires | Points |
|---|---|---|
| Baseline and economics | A measured baseline with a date, four run-cost lines, one sensitivity line | 15 |
| Scope boundary | An explicit out-list a reviewer could test | 10 |
| Autonomy decisions | A rung per action, with the permitting evidence and a blast-radius cap | 20 |
| Evaluation plan | Held-out split, adversarial cases, six metrics with numeric thresholds | 20 |
| Simplicity | The simpler design you rejected, named, with the reason it was not enough | 10 |
| Failure modes | Mode, detection signal, owner | 10 |
| Reversal plan | What it costs to undo each decision, and who runs the rollback | 10 |
| Oversight | Named on-call owner, dashboard signals, kill switch, escalation path | 5 |

Above 85, the packet is ready for a review. Between 70 and 84, fix the weakest rows and re-score, because a review spent on gaps you already know about is a review wasted. Below 70, the problem usually sits upstream of the writing: the workflow failed the screen in step 1, or the autonomy question has no owner, and no amount of prose repairs either one.

## The five holes in most first drafts

**The packet is written for a demo.** The evidence page carries a path that worked once instead of a pass rate over a held-out split. The fix is cheap: put one honest failing case beside the numbers, because that is what makes the numbers believable.

**The scope has no boundary.** The design covers the whole queue, which means it commits to the high-consequence actions nobody wants to defend. Fix it with the out-list, written before the architecture rather than after the review.

**The eval set is assembled from the happy path.** Every case is one the agent handles. Research into agent failures is blunt about where they actually cluster: an analysis of 150 annotated traces, validated at a kappa of 0.88 and extended into a dataset of more than 1,600 traces across seven frameworks, produced 14 failure modes in three categories covering system design, inter-agent misalignment and task verification.[^7] Two of those three categories are about the design and the verification rather than the model, which is why the adversarial cases belong in the plan at the start.

**No reversal plan.** The packet lists choices but never states what undoing them costs. Without that column every decision looks equally weighty, and the review spends its hour on the cheap ones.

**Nobody owns the 2am page.** The design automates a workflow and names no operator. Oversight without a named human is a sentence, not a control.

## Try it

Half a day, seven steps, one artifact at the end.

1. List three candidate workflows from your own organization. Run each through the five screens, score them one to five, and write one sentence on why the two losers lost. (60 minutes)
2. Write the ten-part brief for the winner, one page per part, and use "unmeasured" wherever you do not have a number. (90 minutes)
3. Fill the autonomy table, one row per action the agent will touch. If two actions share a row, split them. (30 minutes)
4. Build the evaluation plan: the held-out split, the adversarial cases you can construct from history, six metrics with numeric thresholds. (45 minutes)
5. Do the economics on one page: four run-cost lines, one sensitivity line, every figure marked measured or estimated. (30 minutes)
6. Write three decision records for the choices a reviewer will attack, using the five-line format from lesson 83. (30 minutes)
7. Grade the packet against the rubric, fix the lowest row, and re-score. Then take it to a colleague who did not help you write it and ask them to name the weakest row. They will find one you missed. (45 minutes)

If step 4 takes less than 45 minutes, look again at the adversarial cases. The plan is thin, and it will not get thicker under deadline pressure.

## Summary

- The first half of the capstone is a design packet for a workflow you choose, not a coding exercise.
- Choose the workflow with five screens: volume, variance, a measurable baseline with labelled history, error asymmetry, and a named owner. Reject outright on the baseline and the owner.
- The packet has ten parts across six to ten pages: unit and baseline, scope with an out-list, autonomy per action, the lowest workable architecture, tools and permissions, context and retention, model and cost envelope, failure modes with signals, oversight, and the evaluation plan with rollout and rollback.
- Autonomy belongs to actions. Set a rung per action, require measured evidence to climb, write the blast-radius cap, and schedule the descent before you need it.
- Build the evaluation plan before the architecture: held-out split, adversarial cases, six metrics with numeric thresholds, and grading in the cheapest order that is reliable.
- Put the economics on one page with four run-cost lines and one sensitivity line, and label every number measured or estimated.
- Grade the packet with the rubric before a reviewer does. Below 70, the workflow choice is usually the problem rather than the writing.

## What's next

Lesson 85 is Capstone II: implement the MVP. It takes the packet you produced here and cuts it down to the thinnest end-to-end slice that can be run and scored, the first tool, the evaluation suite before the features, and the smallest version of the workflow that produces a real number instead of an opinion. Bring the packet. The build is judged against it, and the decisions you wrote down here are what the review at the end of the sequence will hold you to.
