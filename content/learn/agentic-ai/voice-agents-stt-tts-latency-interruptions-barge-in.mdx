Every agent in this course so far has talked to its user through a text box. That works, but it leaves a whole class of business problem on the table. A customer who wants to check an order does not want to type. A patient calling a clinic does not want to fill out a form. A driver mid-route cannot read a chat window at all. The interface people actually reach for when they are busy, driving, or on the phone is their voice, and an agent that cannot speak and listen is cut off from all of those moments.

This lesson is about giving an agent a voice. We will look at the three-piece pipeline that most production voice agents are built from, why latency is the difference between a usable voice loop and a frozen-feeling one, how the agent decides when the user has finished talking, and how barge-in lets a caller interrupt without the whole conversation derailing. The Salesforce AI Research team put the framing plainly: a voice agent is an LLM agent with voice I/O, where the hard part is the reasoning and tool use, and the voice is an input and output layer wrapped around it. [^1] Keep that framing in mind, because it explains why this lesson builds on everything before it rather than starting over.

## The three-piece pipeline: STT, LLM, TTS

A voice agent is built from three components connected in a row. A speech-to-text model, STT, transcribes the user's audio into text. A large language model takes that text, applies the conversation history and system prompt, and generates a response. A text-to-speech model, TTS, turns that response back into audio the user hears. [^2] The three together form the STT to LLM to TTS pipeline, and nearly every production voice agent, from Vapi and Retell to systems built on Pipecat or LiveKit, runs some version of it. [^1]

The reason this pipeline dominates is not elegance. It is that the middle stage, the LLM, is where the actual intelligence lives, and the LLM wants text. The reasoning, the tool calls, the planning, the guardrails, all of that happens in the token space you have been working in all course. The voice components exist to translate the messy real world of audio into that token space and back out again. [^1] That is why the Salesforce team calls the voice an I/O layer: it is the ears and mouth, and the brain is the same agent you already know how to build.

![The three-piece voice pipeline: streaming STT turns audio into text, the LLM reasons and calls tools in token space, and streaming TTS turns the response back into audio, with partial transcripts priming the LLM on the way in and partial text letting TTS speak the first clause early on the way out](</diagrams/voice-agents-stt-tts-latency-interruptions-barge-in/1-three-piece-pipeline.png>)

There is an alternative worth knowing about. Realtime speech-to-speech models, like OpenAI's Realtime API and Gemini Live, skip the three-piece chain entirely. They accept raw audio in and return raw audio out, doing the understanding and generation inside a single multimodal model. [^2] This can get latency under 500 milliseconds and produces more natural prosody, because the model operates directly on the audio rather than through a text intermediate. [^2] The tradeoff is less control over the individual components and, depending on the provider, higher cost per token. [^2] The Salesforce team measured a representative open speech-to-speech model, Qwen2.5-Omni, and found it too slow for realtime interaction, with roughly 13 seconds of time-to-first-audio, which is why they built their tutorial on the cascaded streaming pipeline instead. [^1] So the practical default for an enterprise voice agent today is the three-piece pipeline, with speech-to-speech as a promising option to watch.

## Speech-to-text: streaming is the whole point

The first component, STT, converts the user's audio into text the LLM can process. The choice of STT model affects transcription accuracy across accents, noise levels, and domain vocabulary, and it affects how quickly the agent can start responding. [^2] The single most important decision is whether the STT streams or runs in batch.

Batch transcription waits for the user to finish the whole utterance, transcribes it, then hands the complete text to the LLM. Streaming transcription returns partial results as the user is still speaking, which cuts the time to the LLM's first token significantly. [^2] The LiveKit guide is blunt about the difference: streaming STT is what makes a production voice agent feel realtime, because the transcript is nearly final the instant the user stops talking. [^2][^3] The Soniox latency budget makes the same point from the other direction: recognition that emits words while the user is still speaking means the decision model can be primed with the partial transcript and start before endpointing even confirms the turn is over. [^3]

Latency in STT is not one number. Picovoice breaks it into components: network transmission, audio buffering, model processing, and endpoint detection. [^4] Network latency is the one you cannot hide, because it is dead time on the wire that overlaps with nothing useful. [^3] If your recognizer runs in Virginia and your caller is in Frankfurt, physics adds round-trip time before any computation happens, and a multi-vendor architecture pays that toll more than once. [^3] This is why serious deployments care about where the compute runs as much as how fast it is: co-locating recognition, the model, and synthesis in one region can recover a hundred milliseconds that no model tuning would. [^3]

Accuracy matters too, and the metric to know is word error rate, WER, the fraction of transcribed words that are wrong. WER degrades in real-world conditions, with accents, background noise, and domain vocabulary all taking a toll. [^2] The practical rule is to test your STT on the actual audio your users will produce, not on clean studio samples, because a model that scores well on a benchmark can still mangle a caller's account number over a car's hands-free system.

## Text-to-speech: the first audio is what the user feels

The third component, TTS, converts the LLM's text output into audio. The variables here are voice quality, latency, and how quickly the model can begin streaming audio after receiving the first few tokens. [^2] The most important consideration for a voice agent is streaming synthesis: the ability to start generating audio from partial text rather than waiting for the full sentence. [^2] This keeps the pipeline moving and reduces the perceived wait time, because the agent can speak the first clause of its reply while the model is still generating the rest. [^2][^3]

This is the mirror image of the STT lesson. On the way in, you stream partial transcripts so the LLM can start early. On the way out, you stream partial text so the TTS can start speaking early. The Soniox budget calls synthesis the biggest lever, because time-to-first-audio is governed by the first few words rather than the whole sentence. [^3] An agent that starts talking soonest feels fast, even if its full answer takes just as long to generate as a slower-starting rival. [^3]

## The latency budget: where the milliseconds go

Voice runs on a clock that text never had. A text user will wait a few seconds for a response without complaint. A caller on the phone will not, because human conversation runs on a tight clock. Studies of turn-taking across ten languages put the typical gap between speakers near 200 milliseconds, and people read delays past about a second as hesitation or a dropped call. [^3] An agent does not have to match human speed; it has to stay in the zone where the delay reads as thinking rather than broken. [^3]

The working target that most teams converge on is under one second of end-to-end latency from when the user stops speaking to when the agent starts responding. [^2] Soniox frames it as an 800 millisecond budget, sitting between the 200 millisecond human gap and the roughly one-second mark where a delay starts to read as a problem. [^3] Picovoice gives a similar range, 500 to 800 milliseconds, for a conversational system to feel responsive. [^4] The exact number matters less than the discipline: treat the silence before the agent speaks as a line item you pay for, and measure time-to-first-audio rather than total turnaround. [^3]

The budget only closes if the stages overlap. Run the pipeline sequentially, and the costs stack. Soniox lays out an illustrative sequential run: endpoint detection, STT finalization, the model's first token, TTS first audio, and network, which together overshoot the budget before you add a single network hop. [^3] The fix is not faster parts; it is arranging the parts so nothing waits for a stage to fully finish. A live agent pipelines them: recognition emits words while the user is still speaking, the decision model is primed on the partial transcript, and streaming TTS speaks the first clause while the model generates the rest. [^3] Overlapping makes no stage faster; it changes what you measure, from how long until everything is done to how long until the first sound comes out. [^3]

![The latency budget: human turn-taking runs on a roughly 200 millisecond clock, the Soniox working budget is 800 milliseconds, and delay past about a second reads as hesitation, so the target is under one second measured as time-to-first-audio; running the stages sequentially overshoots the budget, while overlapping them, with recognition emitting words while the user speaks, the LLM primed on the partial transcript, and streaming TTS speaking the first clause early, lands the first audio under budget](</diagrams/voice-agents-stt-tts-latency-interruptions-barge-in/2-latency-budget.png>)

The Salesforce team's measured result shows what this discipline buys. Their streaming pipeline, using Deepgram for STT, a vLLM-served LLM with function calling, and ElevenLabs for TTS, achieved a P50 time-to-first-audio of 947 milliseconds, with a best case of 729 milliseconds. [^1] That is a real, working enterprise voice agent responding in under a second, built from the same components you would pick today.

## Turn detection: knowing when the user is done

Before the agent can respond, it has to know the user has finished speaking. That decision is turn detection, and it is one of the most underestimated parts of voice agent architecture. [^2] Get it wrong in one direction and the agent cuts the user off mid-sentence; get it wrong in the other and the agent waits through an awkward silence while the user has clearly finished.

There are three levels of turn detection. Voice activity detection, VAD, detects the presence or absence of speech in an audio stream. It is fast and runs locally, which makes it a good default, but VAD alone cannot tell the difference between a natural pause mid-sentence and an actual end-of-turn. [^2] The next level is STT endpointing, where the STT provider exposes a confidence signal that the user has finished their thought; this is more semantically aware than raw VAD because it has the benefit of partial transcription context. [^2] The best level is model-based turn detection, where an LLM or a smaller classifier decides whether the user is done based on the meaning of the transcript so far. [^2] This handles cases like "I want to book a flight to, uh, New York," where VAD might prematurely trigger on the pause after "to." [^2]

Endpointing has a built-in tradeoff. A short silence timeout responds fast but can cut off users who naturally speak slowly or pause mid-thought. A long timeout accommodates natural pauses but adds perceived delay. [^4] The Soniox budget calls endpointing the largest line item and the one that gets the least attention, because it spends its budget on purpose: it waits to be sure you stopped talking, and every millisecond of that silence timer adds straight to response time. [^3] The fix is content-aware endpointing that finalizes the moment a sentence is complete, rather than a fixed silence timeout. [^3]

![Three levels of turn detection: voice activity detection is fast and runs locally but cannot tell a natural pause from an end-of-turn, STT endpointing adds a confidence signal with partial transcription context, and model-based turn detection decides from the meaning of the transcript so far, handling cases like a pause after the word to; a short silence timeout responds fast but can cut off slow speakers, while a long one adds perceived delay](</diagrams/voice-agents-stt-tts-latency-interruptions-barge-in/3-turn-detection-levels.png>)

## Interruptions and barge-in: the art of knowing when to shut up

Turn detection also enables the agent to stop speaking when the user interrupts. A well-architected voice agent detects the user speaking mid-response and immediately cancels the current TTS playback to listen instead. [^2] Without this, agents feel robotic and rigid. [^2]

The industry calls this barge-in: the capability that lets a caller interrupt the agent mid-utterance and have the system immediately stop speaking, process the caller's input, and respond accordingly. [^5] It matters more than most deployments treat it. PolyAI reports that barge-in happens in about 1 in 5 calls, and that the callers who interrupt are the most engaged, highest-intent callers: they have heard enough and want to move on, they spotted something to correct, or they have a time-sensitive need. [^6] How the agent handles that interruption tells the caller more about the system's quality than almost anything else in the conversation. [^6]

The mechanics of barge-in require coordination across several components. VAD continuously analyzes the caller's audio stream, even while the agent is speaking, listening for speech energy that meets a threshold indicating a real utterance rather than background noise. [^5] When VAD detects caller speech, it signals the synthesis layer to stop playback and switches the pipeline to active listening. [^5] The subtlety is that the agent's own audio can leak back through the caller's microphone and create false triggers, which is why echo cancellation, AEC, removes the agent's outbound audio from the inbound signal so VAD only responds to genuine caller speech. [^5] Once playback halts, streaming ASR processes the caller's utterance incrementally, which reduces the perceived pause between interruption and response. [^5]

The hard part is that barge-in is not a single setting. Hamming, which analyzes production voice agent calls, frames it as a policy that decides when caller input should stop agent audio, when it should be treated as a backchannel, when it should be ignored as noise, and what evidence should be logged so QA can replay the decision later. [^7] A caller saying "no, I meant Friday" should interrupt. A caller saying "yeah" while listening usually should not. A loud keyboard click should not cancel the TTS. [^7] The working rule is to interrupt when the user's intent is more important than the current audio, and to prove that decision in the logs. [^7]

![Barge-in is a policy, not a toggle: when VAD detects caller speech while the agent is speaking, with echo cancellation stripping the agent's own audio, the system classifies each input as a correction to interrupt on, a backchannel that usually should not interrupt, or noise to ignore, logs the decision for QA, and keeps payment, legal, and consent segments non-interruptible](</diagrams/voice-agents-stt-tts-latency-interruptions-barge-in/4-barge-in-policy.png>)

The failure modes are instructive. A false barge-in, where the agent stops for noise or a backchannel, is more damaging than a missed one, because the agent responds to something the caller never said, and two or three false interruptions in a single call is enough to lose that caller entirely. [^6] A missed correction, where the agent keeps talking over the caller, forces the caller to wait, repeat, or hang up. [^7] The balance between the two depends on context: PolyAI notes that for most enterprise contact center deployments, talking over a caller carries a higher cost than the occasional false positive, so more aggressive barge-in settings are usually the right tradeoff. [^6]

There is also a class of input that should not interrupt at all. Payment instructions, legal disclosures, emergency disclaimers, and consent prompts may need non-interruptible or DTMF-only segments, while general support conversations usually need speech interruption. [^7] The right design documents the policy per message type rather than applying one global rule across every turn. [^7]

## Worked example: a support call that goes off script

Put it together as one agent so the pieces stop being abstract. A company runs a voice agent that handles account support calls. A caller dials in, and the agent opens with a greeting and starts reading the account options. The caller, who has heard this menu before, interrupts mid-sentence: "No, I need to update my address."

Here is what has to happen for that to feel natural. The agent's VAD, running continuously, detects the caller's speech energy the moment they start talking, even though the agent is still mid-utterance. [^5] Echo cancellation has already stripped the agent's own voice out of the inbound signal, so the VAD does not mistake the agent's audio for the caller. [^5] The synthesis layer stops playback immediately, and the pipeline switches to active listening. [^5] Streaming ASR transcribes the caller's words incrementally, so the partial transcript is available almost immediately. [^5]

Now the agent has to decide what to do with that input. This is the policy layer. The caller's words are a true correction, not a backchannel and not noise, so the agent accepts the new turn and preserves the context of what it was about to say. [^7] The system shows the model where it was interrupted and what it was about to say, so the agent can respond to the interjection without losing track of its own thread. [^6] The agent pivots to the address update flow, and the conversation continues as if the interruption never happened.

The whole exchange only works because every stage was built for it. The STT streams, so the transcript is ready fast. The turn detection distinguishes a real correction from a backchannel. The barge-in policy stops the audio at the right moment and logs the decision. And the latency budget keeps the whole loop under a second, so the caller never feels the agent is slow to react. [^2][^3] This is the perceive-reason-act loop from lesson 3, re-equipped for audio: the agent perceives speech, reasons over the transcript, acts by switching flows, and leaves enough logged evidence for a reviewer to replay the decision. [^7]

## The pattern to take away

Voice agents fail for predictable reasons, and a numbered pattern avoids most of them.

1. Build the three-piece pipeline first: streaming STT, an LLM with tool calling, and streaming TTS. The voice is an I/O layer around the agent you already know how to build. [^1][^2]
2. Make every stage stream. Partial transcripts on the way in, partial text on the way out, so nothing waits for a stage to fully finish. [^2][^3]
3. Budget the latency and measure time-to-first-audio, not total turnaround. Target under one second end to end, and treat the silence before the agent speaks as a line item you pay for. [^2][^3]
4. Co-locate your compute. Network latency is the one cost you cannot overlap away, so keep recognition, the model, and synthesis in one region. [^3]
5. Choose turn detection deliberately. VAD for speed, STT endpointing for semantic awareness, model-based detection for the hard cases, and content-aware endpointing instead of a fixed silence timeout. [^2][^3]
6. Treat barge-in as a policy, not a toggle. Classify each input as a correction, backchannel, noise, or escalation, and log the decision so QA can replay it. [^7]
7. Make some segments non-interruptible. Payment, legal, and consent prompts may need to play through, even when general support turns allow interruption. [^7]

Get the voice layer right and the reasoning engine you built in the earlier lessons does the same job, just reachable by anyone with a phone. Get it wrong, and the agent will be technically capable but conversationally wooden, the kind of system callers are trying to get past rather than use. [^6]

## Try it

Take the support call example and make it your own. Write down the three components you would pick for a voice agent that handles a real task you care about, say a clinic appointment line or an order status check, and name one latency risk in each. Now sketch the turn detection: would you rely on VAD, STT endpointing, or a model-based classifier, and what is the failure mode you are most worried about? Next, write a short barge-in policy for that agent: list three things a caller might say while the agent is speaking, and mark each one as interrupt, backchannel, or ignore. Finally, decide which segments of the call, if any, should be non-interruptible and why. Write two or three sentences each, then compare your latency budget and your barge-in policy against the pattern in this lesson. The goal is not to build the agent. It is to practice deciding how the voice layer should behave before you wire up a single model.
