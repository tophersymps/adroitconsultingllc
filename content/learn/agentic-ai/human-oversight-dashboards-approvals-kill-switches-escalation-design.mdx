# Lesson 79: Human Oversight: Dashboards, Approvals, Kill Switches, and Escalation Design

Lesson 78 ended on a design decision: a deterministic skeleton with a bounded agentic step at the seam. This lesson is about what keeps that seam survivable once the thing is running at 3am and nobody is watching.

Almost every team that gets burned by an agent had human oversight on the slide deck. A reviewer. An approval step. A dashboard. The control existed. It just did not do anything, because oversight is not a feature you add, it is a system you build, and each of its parts answers a different question.

Four questions, four instruments:

- Is it behaving? A dashboard.
- Should this specific action happen? An approval gate.
- How do we stop it right now? A kill switch.
- Who decides when the answer is not obvious, and by when? An escalation path.

Skip any one of them and the other three degrade. A dashboard with no authority behind it is a spectator sport. An approval gate with no escalation path stalls the workflow the first time the approver is on a plane. A kill switch nobody has tested is a runbook, not a control.

## Autonomy is the variable you are oversighting

Before you design any of it, be precise about how much freedom you granted. AWS separates two ideas that get used interchangeably and should not be. Agency is the scope of actions a system is permitted and enabled to take: what systems it can touch, what operations it can perform, what resources it can modify. Autonomy is the degree of independent decision-making, meaning when it operates, how it chooses among actions, and whether a human must approve before execution. [^1] A single agent can have high agency and low autonomy (broad tools, every call approved) or the reverse (narrow tools, runs on its own schedule). The two dimensions need different controls. Agency needs boundaries and permissions. Autonomy needs oversight and behavioral monitoring. [^1]

AWS's Agentic AI Security Scoping Matrix turns that into four operating scopes. Scope 1 is read-only with human-triggered workflows. Scope 2 is prescribed agency, where the agent can modify the environment but every action of consequence requires explicit approval, which is the human-in-the-loop pattern. Scope 3 is supervised agency: a human triggers the work, the agent completes it without further approval, and humans can intervene but are not expected to. Scope 4 is full agency, self-initiated and continuous, where humans hold supervisory oversight and the security emphasis shifts to behavioral baselines, anomaly detection, and shutoff mechanisms. [^1]

Read that ladder and the lesson writes itself. Scope 2 is where approval gates carry the weight. Scope 3 is where dashboards do. Scope 4 is where kill switches stop being optional. Most teams cannot say which scope their agent is in, which is why their controls feel either theatrical or missing. Write the scope down. It decides the rest.

## Dashboards that change decisions

A dashboard for an agent is not a wall of infrastructure charts. The AWS Agentic AI Lens is blunt about the common failure: organizations instrument infrastructure metrics but not agent-specific spans such as reasoning iterations, tool invocations, and memory operations. [^5] CPU and latency tell you the box is alive. They tell you nothing about whether the agent has started behaving differently.

What belongs on the screen comes from the agent's actual job. The general categories are API call rate, data access volume, decision frequency, error rate, and resource consumption, but the specific measures have to be chosen per agent. A support agent might track outbound email volume and cross-customer retrievals. A coding agent might track commit rate and commands executed. A data agent might track query volume and cross-table joins. [^4] That specificity is the whole point: a threshold that fires on every agent equally fires on nothing usefully.

Then add a second, earlier layer. Quality evaluations, meaning correctness, tool-selection accuracy, and policy conformance scored per run, tend to drop before infrastructure alarms fire. AWS describes that score drop as an early warning that behavior is drifting. [^4] A dashboard that only shows cost and error rate will show you the damage after it happened.

Four questions is a good size for a first screen. Can I see what the agent is doing right now, per agent and per tool? What is drifting, compared to the baseline this agent established in normal operation? What is waiting on a human, and how long has it been waiting? What did it do that a person had to correct in the last 24 hours? If the screen cannot answer those, it is decoration.

Two habits keep a dashboard honest. First, name the action each panel would trigger. If a number turning red produces no decision, either the threshold is wrong or the panel is noise. Second, revisit the panel set on a schedule. The Lens flags dashboards defined at launch and never revisited, so teams keep measuring what used to matter while new failure modes go unwatched. [^5] Baselines have the same problem: set once at deployment, they produce false alarms when behavior legitimately evolves and hide slow drift that redefines normal. [^5]

One more panel deserves its own line, because nobody puts it up voluntarily: reviewer load. Queue depth, median time per decision, and the share of approvals completed in under a few seconds. That last one is the cheapest rubber-stamp detector you will ever build.

## Approval gates: the design is the context

The gate is where most oversight programs quietly fail, and the failure is usually one of two opposites. Route every action through review and you get reviewer fatigue, which produces approvals faster than anyone can read. Route nothing and you have autonomy with a compliance sticker.

AWS states the tradeoff directly: routing every agent action through human review produces rubber-stamp approvals, routing none produces unbounded autonomy, and risk-tiered approval pauses agents only for the decisions where human judgment changes the outcome, with enough context to make those decisions meaningful. [^2]

Tiering starts with a classifier, and the classifier must be deterministic. This is the part teams get wrong in an interesting way. A risk classifier built on an LLM reads the same untrusted content as the request being evaluated, so adversarial text can talk it into a low-risk label. Use policy engines and rule-based logic as the authoritative signal, and treat LLM-assisted classification as optional input that a deterministic layer re-checks. [^2] Combine static facts about the operation (what kind of action, against what resource) with dynamic signals (frequency, time of day, source, recent anomalies). [^2]

A workable baseline from the AWS guidance, worth adapting rather than copying: read-only operations proceed autonomously, low-risk writes require a single reviewer, and higher-risk operations such as financial transactions, data deletion, and external communications require stricter approval, whether single reviewer, multiple reviewers, or out of band. [^2] OpenAI's guide uses a simpler three-level tool risk rating (low, medium, high) based on read-only versus write access, reversibility, required permissions, and financial impact, and uses those ratings to pause before high-risk functions or escalate. [^8] Either scheme works. Having none does not.

The gate also has to be in the authorization path. A pause that the workflow can route around is a suggestion. Concretely: the approval decision should be what mints or releases the credential or token that performs the action, not a message that asks the agent nicely to wait. AWS's implementation pattern makes this literal, with a workflow that pauses on a task token and only proceeds when the approval application calls back with success. [^2]

Context is the difference between a decision and a click. AWS names insufficient context as an anti-pattern: showing the proposed action alone, without the reasoning chain, data sources, or potential consequences, turns review into a formality. [^2] The same guidance wants confidence scores, policy-check results, anomaly flags, and similar past decisions on the reviewer's screen so attention can be calibrated to actual risk. [^3] And the reviewer needs the four facts that make any decision possible: the action, the reason, the actor on whose behalf it runs, and the effect if it goes through.

Then handle the boring cases that break real deployments. Every gate needs a timeout policy and a safe fallback, usually blocking the operation, plus an escalation path for when the primary reviewer is unavailable. Without them, agent execution stalls indefinitely the first time a reviewer is out. [^2] Every decision gets logged with reviewer identity, timestamp, operation under review, decision, and escalation events, which is the record a compliance reviewer will ask for. [^2] And review the approval metrics on a cadence, looking for the patterns that suggest fatigue. [^2]

Persistent trust is a legitimate escape valve, used carefully. When a reviewer approves an operation pattern once so future matches proceed, human judgment moves from moment to moment to the moment of the grant. Bound each grant to a specific command, parameter shape, or resource, tier grants by risk, require re-confirmation at a defined cadence for the higher ones, and make the grants auditable and revocable. Wildcard grants that approve all future operations of a type should not be issued at all, since they remove oversight from an entire class of work. [^2]

## Kill switches: remove authority, not just the process

Most "kill switches" I have seen in agent demos pause a loop. That is a pause, not a stop. If the process halts and restarts with the same identity and permissions, and something upstream retries, you have bought minutes and called it containment.

The AWS containment guidance orders the sequence, and the order is the useful part. On detecting an anomaly above threshold, capture a forensic snapshot of the agent's memory, active sessions, and pending operations to durable storage. Then revoke the agent's credentials by attaching a deny-all policy to its role, while preserving the role itself for later analysis. Then broadcast a quarantine event so dependent workflows activate their circuit breakers. [^4] Evidence first, because recovery destroys it. Authority second, because permissions are the actual capability. Notification third, because everything downstream is about to find out the hard way otherwise.

The anti-pattern to memorize is quarantine by stopping the process without revoking credentials, since the agent can simply be restarted with the same reach. [^4] The second one is relying on a human to trigger quarantine, which lets a misbehaving agent run for hours while responders mobilize. [^4]

So a real kill switch has three levels, and you should know which one you are pulling:

- **Pause intake.** Stop accepting new work. The queue drains, in-flight tasks finish. Right for a suspected quality problem or a cost spike.
- **Revoke authority.** Kill the credential or scoped token. In-flight calls fail closed. Right for anything that smells like compromise or uncontrolled action.
- **Contain downstream.** Trip the circuit breakers in the workflows that depend on this agent, so callers route to a safe fallback rather than failing with an unhandled error. [^4]

Restoration deserves its own control because it is where incidents get made worse. AWS recommends manual override runbooks for quarantine and restore, with multi-person authorization for restoration to prevent premature re-activation. [^4] Two people to turn it back on, one person to turn it off. That asymmetry is deliberate: stopping an agent is cheap and reversible, restarting a compromised one is not.

Then test it. A kill switch that has never been pulled in a drill is a diagram. Put it in the incident rotation, pull it on a Tuesday, and time how long from decision to revoked credential. That number is your real containment latency, and it is almost always longer than the runbook claims.

## Escalation design: a state with a clock and an owner

Escalation is the part everyone treats as messaging and should treat as state machine. "Ping someone in Slack" is not escalation. Escalation is a defined transition with a trigger, a named owner, a time budget, and a default action when the clock runs out.

OpenAI's agent guide names the two triggers that cover most cases. The first is exceeding failure thresholds: the agent has retried or acted some number of times without success, so control goes to a human. The second is a high-risk action: sensitive, irreversible, or high-stakes, which gets human oversight until confidence in reliability grows. [^8] Add two more that agent systems specifically need. A policy boundary: the request is legal but outside what this agent was scoped to decide. And an ambiguity: the agent has two plausible paths and the wrong one is expensive.

For each trigger, write down five things: who owns the decision by name or role, how long they have, what the agent does while waiting (almost always: hold), what happens at timeout, and who takes over if the owner is unreachable. The fifth is the one that gets skipped, and it is the one that decides whether your workflow runs on a Saturday.

Multiple reviewers need their own rules. For critical operations, AWS recommends independent review with blind review, consensus logic, and an escalation path for disagreement, and warns against letting reviewers see each other's decisions before submitting, which turns review into anchoring bias. [^3] Consensus with visibility is not consensus, it is a queue for agreement.

Leave room for the human on the other side too. NIST's AI Risk Management Framework puts appeal and override into post-deployment monitoring, as part of the mechanisms an organization implements for users and affected parties. [^7] If a customer's refund was denied by an agent, there has to be a named path to a person and a recorded outcome. That is oversight pointed outward, and it is the part regulators notice first.

One more thing worth naming, because it is invisible until it bites: automation bias. NIST's generative AI profile defines it as excessive deference to AI systems, and lists it among the human-AI configuration risks, alongside algorithmic aversion, where people distrust a system that is actually right. [^6] Oversight tuned for the first failure mode tends to create the second. The fix is not exhortation, it is interface design: put the agent's confidence, its sources, and the counterfactual on the screen, and occasionally route a case the agent got right through review to see whether the reviewer catches that it was right.

## A worked example: the invoice runner

Here is the whole thing on one agent. An accounts payable agent receives supplier invoices by email, extracts the fields, matches them against purchase orders and receiving records, and initiates payment when the match is clean. Volume is 4,000 invoices a month. Two finance staff can review maybe 150 decisions a day between them, so routing everything to humans is not available even if you wanted it.

**Scope.** Supervised agency for matching, prescribed agency for payment. The agent decides matches on its own; every payment of consequence gets a human.

**Risk tiers.** Deterministic classifier, no model in the path.

- Clean three-way match, existing vendor, under $2,500: pay autonomously, log only.
- Clean match, existing vendor, $2,500 to $25,000: one finance reviewer, 4 hour window, hold on timeout.
- Any amount over $25,000, or any new vendor, or any changed bank detail: two reviewers, blind to each other, 24 hour window, hold on timeout.
- Any invoice where extraction confidence is low, or the PO does not exist, or the totals differ: exception queue, never auto-pay.

**Dashboard.** Auto-match rate by vendor. Exception rate against a 30 day baseline. Payment volume and total value per hour, which catches a runaway loop in minutes. Approval queue depth and median time to decision. Share of approvals completed under 8 seconds, tracked as a fatigue signal. Evaluation score for extraction accuracy, which drops before the exception rate does.

**Kill switch.** The payment tool is issued a scoped token minted per run. Pulling the switch revokes the token, which fails closed mid-run instead of finishing the batch. Snapshot the in-flight batch first, so finance can reconcile what was attempted. A second, separate switch pauses intake on the mailbox so the queue does not grow while the first problem is diagnosed.

**Escalation.** Every exception carries an owner from a rota, a 4 hour clock, and a default of hold. If the primary is unreachable, the deputy gets it at the 2 hour mark. Two reviewers who disagree go to the controller, and the invoice stays unpaid until one of them signs. If the supplier calls to complain, there is a named human queue and a recorded outcome, which is the appeal path.

**What this buys.** Ninety percent of invoices never touch a human. The ten percent that do arrive with the match, the PO, the receiving record, the amount, and the reason for the flag on one screen, so a reviewer can decide in under a minute and still be deciding. The agent's authority is narrow, its silence is monitored, and the thing that actually stops it is a revoked credential rather than a polite request.

That last sentence is the design principle of the whole lesson. Oversight that depends on the agent cooperating is not oversight.

## Try it

1. **Write the scope down.** One sentence: this agent has [read-only / prescribed / supervised / full] agency over [systems] and [none / single-reviewer / multi-reviewer] approval on actions of consequence. If you cannot fill the blanks, that is the finding.
2. **Put four panels on a screen.** Current behavior per agent and tool, drift against a baseline, queue depth and age, corrections in the last 24 hours. Next to each panel, write the action it would trigger. Delete any panel with no answer.
3. **Build the tier table.** Three or four tiers, deterministic rules, and for each tier the approver count, the window, and the timeout behavior. No wildcards.
4. **Write the approval card.** The action, the reason, the actor, the effect, the evidence, and the confidence. Show it to the person who will actually approve it and ask what they would need to say no.
5. **Order your containment sequence.** Snapshot, revoke, notify. Then write down what "revoke" means for each tool: which token, which role, which scope. If the answer is "stop the container", you do not have a kill switch yet.
6. **Pull the switch on purpose.** In staging, mid-run, and time it. Then try to restart and see how many people it takes.
7. **Add the fatigue metric.** Percentage of approvals under a few seconds, and median queue age. Check it weekly. When the first number creeps up, your gate is becoming a formality.

## Summary

Human oversight is four instruments, not one. A dashboard shows behavior per agent rather than the health of the box, and its panels earn their place by naming a decision. An approval gate classifies risk deterministically, sits inside the authorization path, and shows enough context that a reviewer can genuinely refuse. A kill switch removes authority instead of pausing a process, captures evidence before isolation, and needs two people to reverse. An escalation path is a state with a named owner, a clock, and a safe default, and it includes an appeal route for the people on the other end of the decision. Design them together, because each one is what makes the others real.
