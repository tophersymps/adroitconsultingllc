# Lesson 89: Your Roadmap: The Next 90 Days of Learning and Building

Lesson 88 ended at the organizational boundary, where the revision cycle of the protocol you depend on belongs to a steering committee you do not sit on. That is one kind of clock, and you cannot set it. This lesson is about the other kind: the ninety days in front of you.

Everything in this course so far has been a how. How to design the loop, how to ground retrieval, how to budget tokens, how to evaluate, how to govern, how to defend a design in a review. This lesson is about order, dates and evidence. If you finished the capstone sequence with a packet, a working slice and a first number, you already have the raw material for a plan. If you read eighty-eight lessons and built nothing, this is the lesson that fixes it, and it is the only one in the series where the deliverable is your own calendar.

Ninety days is a deliberate length. A week is too short to produce a number worth believing. A year hides a slipping plan until somebody reviews the budget. Ninety days is long enough to assemble a real evaluation set and short enough that failure shows up while it is still cheap to correct.

## Define the terms

**Roadmap.** A dated sequence of artifacts, each with a number attached to it. A reading list is not a roadmap. Neither is a list of tools you intend to try, because neither one can fail in a way you would notice.

**Arc.** A 30-day block that closes on one artifact and one number. Three arcs, three definitions of done, and a decision at the end of each about whether to continue, narrow the scope or stop.

**Build log.** One page per working session: what you tried, what broke, what you decided and what you would try next. Fifteen lines is enough. It is the raw material for the design packet in arc three, and the reason you will not spend week eleven reconstructing choices you made in week four.

**Spike.** A time-boxed experiment that answers exactly one question and gets thrown away after the answer is written down. Two hours and one question is the discipline. A spike without a written answer is just an afternoon of typing.

**Labelled history.** Past cases with the real outcome recorded beside them: which tickets were resolved correctly, which claims were paid as filed, which invoices were genuinely fraudulent. This is the asset every later measurement depends on, and it is the one thing you cannot manufacture late.

**Held-out set.** Cases you never tune against, scored only to produce an honest number. Lesson 84 built the split. This lesson only insists that your roadmap names the week it gets created.

**Leading indicator.** Something you control this week: sessions logged, evaluation runs completed, decision records written. **Lagging indicator.** Something the system produces: held-out pass rate, cost per unit, human override rate.

**Evidence trail.** The packet, the decision records, the evaluation results and the runbook, assembled as you go. Teams that treat it as a week-twelve writing task produce a document that describes the system they wished they had built.

**Tool budget.** The two or three technologies you will go deep on, and the explicit list of what you are postponing. A plan that names eleven frameworks has named none.

## Three arcs, three definitions of done

The arcs are not arbitrary thirds. Each one answers a different question, and each question can be answered no.

| Arc | The question | The artifact | The number that closes it | Ends early if |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Days 1 to 30 | Does this workflow produce a number at all? | A loop by hand, a labelled dataset, a first evaluation run | A held-out pass rate against a measured baseline | You cannot name the specific ambiguity the agent resolves |
| Days 31 to 60 | Can it survive contact with real work? | A slice used by named people, with tracing, guardrails, retries and a cost per unit | Cost per unit and override rate on live cases | There is no named user, no owner, or no trace you can replay |
| Days 61 to 90 | Can somebody else run it without you? | The design packet, decision records, a runbook, a scheduled review | An approval with conditions, or a documented no | Nobody outside the build has read the packet |

![Three 30-day arcs with the question each one answers, the artifact it produces and the number that closes it, and a gate between arcs where the plan can continue, narrow the scope or stop](</diagrams/your-roadmap-next-90-days-learning-building/1-three-arcs-with-gates.png>)

Arc one is the one people skip, and skipping it is why three months disappear. The failure mode of arc one is a demo that impresses a room and cannot be scored, usually because the workflow was chosen for how it looks rather than for whether the outcomes were ever written down. Anthropic's guidance on building agents makes the conservative version of this point: find the simplest solution possible and only increase complexity when it is needed, and treat the jump to a full agentic loop as a trade of latency and cost for task performance rather than an upgrade.[^1] Applied to a roadmap, that gives you permission to spend arc one running the workflow by hand with a structured output and one read-only tool. If a plain retrieval call with a good prompt clears your threshold on the held-out set, you have saved yourself eight weeks and you should take the win.

Arc two is where the schedule usually breaks, because real users are involved and their patience is not a resource you can plan around. Two things belong on the calendar before the first user touches it: tracing you can replay, and a cost per unit you are measuring rather than estimating. Lesson 86 covered the build. The roadmap question is only whether week seven or week eight contains the day a colleague uses the thing for the first time. Put a date on it and put their name next to it.

Arc three is the half that survives your departure. A packet, decision records and a review are not bureaucracy added to a working system. They are the reason the second person to touch this can change it without guessing.

## Pick the workflow before you pick the curriculum

The five screens from lesson 84 apply unchanged: volume, variance, a measurable baseline, error asymmetry and a named owner. For a personal 90-day plan there is one addition, which is access. You need the raw material in reach this month, meaning the case history, the system of record and a person who will answer questions in week three without treating it as a favor.

The outside view on this is not encouraging, which is why the screen matters more than the enthusiasm does. Gartner's prediction is that over 40 percent of agentic AI projects will be canceled by the end of 2027, with escalating costs, unclear business value and inadequate risk controls as the stated reasons, and the same release notes a poll of 3,412 webinar attendees in which most organizations described conservative investment rather than deep commitment.[^2] Gartner also calls out agent washing, the rebranding of assistants, RPA and chatbots as agentic products, and estimates that only around 130 of the thousands of vendors claiming agentic capabilities are the real thing.[^2] A 90-day plan is not immune to any of that. It is, however, the format in which a bad choice costs you a quarter instead of a program.

Which gives you the practical rule: choose the workflow where you personally can see the labelled history, and let the interesting technology wait until you are being paid to learn it. The curriculum for a 90-day plan is not the framework documentation. It is the last six weeks of your own failures.

A ratio helps. Four hours of building to one hour of reading, tracked in the build log, is sustainable for somebody with a full-time job. The reading that pays is narrow and reactive: the error message, the retrieval failure, the token bill, the thing the reviewer asked about. Reading ahead across the whole stack is how a plan turns into a subscription.

## A worked example: Priya's 90 days

Priya is a technical architect at a 700-person insurance brokerage. She has the Salesforce org, a service team of nine, roughly 2,800 cases a quarter, a service cloud instance nobody outside her team understands and six hours a week she can defend in her calendar. Her chosen workflow is case triage and draft replies for the three highest-volume intents.

![Priya's thirteen weeks as a timeline, each phase naming the artifact it produces and the number it is judged on](</diagrams/your-roadmap-next-90-days-learning-building/2-priya-thirteen-weeks.png>)

**Weeks 1 and 2, the baseline.** She exports 200 closed cases with the resolved-correctly flag, computes median handle time and rework rate, and reads 30 of them herself to see what the flags actually mean. The artifact is a one-page baseline with the date on it. Her screen fails one candidate immediately: the renewal-reminder workflow runs four times a month, so no per-case improvement can pay for the build.

**Weeks 3 and 4, the loop by hand.** No framework. A structured output schema with three fields (intent, confidence, proposed reply), one read-only tool that fetches the case and its contact record, and a prompt she keeps in the repository rather than in a scratch document. Twenty cases run end to end, logged in the build log. Anthropic's caution about frameworks is the reason for this ordering: abstraction layers can obscure the prompts and responses you need to debug, and starting with the API directly keeps the failure legible.[^1]

**Weeks 5 and 6, the evaluation set.** One hundred and fifty cases with recorded outcomes, split 100 for development and 50 held back, plus the adversarial cases history does not contain: instruction text inside a case body, the same case submitted twice inside a minute, a case whose contact was deleted mid-flow, and a routine-looking case belonging to a restricted account. Thresholds with consequences: triage intent above 80 percent on the held-out split, groundedness above 95 percent, and any drop of more than three points between runs halts new work until the cause is named.

**Weeks 7 and 8, production concerns.** Tracing she can replay, retries with a budget, timeouts, a cost per case measured across model calls and evaluation runs, and one class of action removed from the surface entirely: nothing writes to the billing object, ever. That is a rule with no exceptions, so it does not live in the prompt.

**Weeks 9 and 10, two users.** Two service agents use the drafts for every case in the three intents. A 15-minute daily review of ten percent of the automated decisions produces the override data, and the runbook grows from what breaks.

**Weeks 11 and 12, the packet.** Ten parts, six to ten pages, plus three decision records for the choices somebody will argue about: the model tier, the refusal to write to billing, and the decision to route restricted accounts to a human.

**Week 13, the review.** A colleague who did not help build it reads the packet and tries to break the evaluation plan. The output is an approval with conditions or a documented no. Then the next 90 days get written from what the numbers said rather than from what she hoped they would say.

Notice how little of that is about technology choice. The Microsoft Cloud Adoption Framework organizes agent adoption into four areas, planning for agents, governing and securing them, building them, and managing them, and it presents agent types on a spectrum from productivity agents that retrieve and synthesize, through action agents that perform defined tasks, to automation agents that run multi-step processes with minimal oversight.[^3] The same framework is direct about why the governance and management work cannot wait: agents introduce nondeterministic behavior, which makes robust testing and governance a requirement rather than a phase you reach later.[^3] Priya's plan is that guidance compressed into thirteen weeks for one person with a day job.

## Build the scoreboard before the schedule

A schedule tells you what to do. A scoreboard tells you whether it worked, and it has to exist before the first week starts, because a metric invented in week nine is a metric chosen by its own result.

| Metric | Type | Priya's target | Consequence when it misses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sessions logged | Leading | Four sessions a week, 90 minutes each | The arc deadline moves, the target does not |
| Evaluation runs | Leading | Weekly from week five | No feature work until a run is recorded |
| Held-out pass rate | Lagging | Above 80 percent on intent | Stop adding intents, diagnose the failing cases |
| Cost per case | Lagging | Under 30 cents including retries and evaluation calls | Drop the model tier or trim retrieved context |
| Override rate | Lagging | Under 15 percent for the draft action | Demote the action to draft-only until the cause is fixed |
| Requests from named users | Lagging | Two users asking unprompted by week 12 | The workflow choice failed the access screen |

Every lagging metric needs a leading one that moves it, and every threshold needs a consequence written next to it in advance. Without the consequence column you have a dashboard, and dashboards do not change behavior. The 30-day cadence also matters for a reason that has nothing to do with motivation: an arc is short enough that a wrong decision produces a visible miss while you still have two thirds of the plan left.

Score the arc in a 30-minute review on the last day of it, on paper, with the build log open. Three questions: what shipped, what number moved, and what am I no longer doing. The third one is the one that keeps the plan honest, because every arc you complete adds maintenance to the previous two.

## Make the unbreakable rules deterministic

Most of what you write for an agent is an instruction to a model, and models are reliable without being guaranteed. Claude Code's documentation draws the line sharply: an instruction is either non-deterministic, meaning the model reads it and decides what to do, or deterministic, meaning the harness executes it every time regardless of what the model decided, which covers hooks, deny rules and managed settings.[^4] The same page is honest about the trade: for most decisions you want the model deciding, since it can read the situation in front of it, and the deterministic kind is for the things that must always happen or must never happen.[^4]

Your roadmap should carry that split as a short list, written in week seven and enforced from then on:

1. The rules that cannot have exceptions belong in hooks, deny rules and tests, because the harness runs them whether or not the model decides to cooperate. Never write to the billing object. Never send anything to a customer without a human click. No production credentials in the agent's environment.
2. Give the system a check to run. The documentation's diagnosis of a common failure is that the agent reported success without anything verifying it, because it had no way to check its own work.[^4] The fix is mechanical: a schema validator, a dry run, a test that fails loudly.
3. If you keep rewriting an instruction to make it stick, that is evidence the instruction belongs in the deterministic column rather than in the prompt.[^4]

![A decision split between an instruction the model can decline and a harness control that executes every time, with the three rules that belong in the harness column](</diagrams/your-roadmap-next-90-days-learning-building/3-prompt-or-harness.png>)

That list is the difference between a plan that ends in a review and one that ends in an incident report.

## Choose observability you already run

Your arc-two metric needs instrumentation, and the temptation is to spend three weeks evaluating agent observability platforms. The 2025 Stack Overflow developer survey suggests that most teams do not, at least not yet: among developers who work on agents, 43 percent used Grafana with Prometheus for monitoring and 32 percent used Sentry, with dedicated tracing tools well behind that.[^5] Teams adapted the monitoring stack they already operated rather than adopting something AI-native, which is the pragmatic choice for a 90-day plan where the instrument itself is not the deliverable.

That same survey is worth reading for a second reason, and it has nothing to do with tooling. It found that 84 percent of respondents use or plan to use AI tools in their development process, with 51 percent of professional developers using them daily, while favorable sentiment fell to just 60 percent, down from above 70 percent in 2023 and 2024.[^5] Usage up, sentiment down, in the same year, on the same sample of 33,000-plus developers.

Read that pair against your own roadmap. The reason to keep a build log, a baseline with a date, held-out numbers and decision records is that this is what an evidence trail is for: it is the difference between a claim about your agent and a measurement of it. When the room turns skeptical, and it will, you will be asked how much of the workflow ran correctly and how you know. A repo full of configuration files will not answer that question.

## The handoff is the deliverable

The last 30 days are for the person who inherits this. Not a colleague on the same team in a month, but whoever holds it in a year when your priorities have moved.

The test is cheap and most teams fail it: somebody else runs the workflow for a week, with you reachable but not present, and the system either keeps producing numbers or it does not. What makes that possible is unglamorous. A runbook that names the escalation path and the on-call owner. Decision records that state what was decided, what was rejected and what would change the answer. An evaluation set that runs on a schedule whether or not anyone is watching. A packet that names the out-list so the next person does not have to rediscover the boundary by breaking it.

If a plan has no handoff step, it was a personal project with a deadline. That is a legitimate thing to want, and it should be written down as the goal rather than discovered in month four.

## Exam traps

![The seven exam traps sorted by the arc each one breaks, two for arc one, two for arc two, two for arc three, and one that applies to any arc](</diagrams/your-roadmap-next-90-days-learning-building/4-exam-traps-by-arc.png>)

**Planning by tool instead of workflow.** A 90-day plan titled "learn three agent frameworks" has no number that can fail. The workflow produces the number. The frameworks are interchangeable parts.

**Skipping arc one because the demo looked good.** The demo is the reason arc one exists. If the held-out pass rate is not in the plan, nothing in the plan is measurable.

**Scoring on the cases you tuned against.** A held-out split that has quietly been used for prompt iteration is a training number wearing a test label, which is the most common way a roadmap reports success without delivering any.

**Confusing a working slice with a shipped number.** Cost per unit and override rate on live cases are arc-two numbers. They are also the two figures a reviewer will ask for, and neither one can be produced retroactively.

**Climbing autonomy faster than the evidence.** The ladder in lesson 84 requires measured evidence per action. A roadmap that puts a write action live in week six because the reads went well has skipped the evidence step rather than accelerated it.

**Scheduling the review and then canceling it.** The review is a deliverable. If it gets moved for a busier week, it will get moved again, and the packet stops being written for a reader and starts being written for a drawer.

**Leaving the handoff to the end without a runbook.** Every week you postpone the runbook, it gets harder to write, because you forget exactly which workaround covers which failure.

## Try it

One afternoon, five artifacts, all of them reusable in whatever plan you write.

1. Write three arcs on a single page, in the table format above. Artifact, closing number, and the condition that would end the arc early. If you cannot name the closing number for arc one, pick a different workflow.
2. Export at least 100 closed cases with the real outcome recorded. Count how many have a usable label. Under 100 means your first arc is a labelling project, and that is a legitimate finding worth writing down before you commit the quarter.
3. Build the scoreboard: three leading indicators, four lagging ones, and a written consequence beside every threshold. Do this before the schedule, not alongside it.
4. Write the deterministic list. Every rule that has no exceptions gets a hook, a deny rule or a test, and nothing on that list stays in the prompt.
5. Put the review date in the calendar for day 90 and name the reviewer. Then put the weeks 9 and 10 user date in as well, because that is the appointment most likely to slip.
6. Book four 90-minute sessions a week for the next twelve weeks, and decide in advance which existing commitment they replace. A plan that adds six hours without removing six hours is a wish.

The one step people skip is number six, and it is the one that determines whether any of the others happen.

## Summary

- A 90-day plan is three 30-day arcs, each closing on one artifact and one number, with a written condition that would end the arc early.
- Pick the workflow by access to labelled history as much as by value, then let the technology choice follow the evaluation results. Most agentic projects fail on cost, value and risk controls that were never scoped.[^2]
- Arc one is a loop by hand, a dataset and a first number. Start with the simplest construction that could clear the threshold.[^1]
- Arc two produces the two numbers a reviewer asks for, cost per unit and override rate on live cases, with the instrument you already run.[^5]
- Arc three produces the packet, the decision records, the runbook and a review held on the day you scheduled it.
- Rules that cannot have exceptions get enforced by the harness, because an instruction the model can decline is not a control.[^4]
- Build the scoreboard before the schedule, and treat the handoff as the deliverable rather than the epilogue.
