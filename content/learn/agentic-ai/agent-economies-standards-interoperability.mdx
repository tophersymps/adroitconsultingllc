# Lesson 88: The Future of Agent Economies: Standards and Interoperability

Lesson 87 closed with three layers, a repair ladder and a permission boundary, and every boundary in it belonged to Meridian. Their workflow, their pipeline, their loop, their rules about what the system may repair on its own. The hard part starts when the next box on the diagram belongs to somebody else: a carrier's tracking agent, a supplier's warranty agent, a payments provider that will not take a phone call from your code.

Two things change the moment an agent crosses an organizational boundary. You can no longer read the other side's implementation, so the published contract becomes the only thing left to audit. And you are no longer the only party whose mistakes matter, so authority has to travel with the work as structured data instead of as a paragraph in a design document. Everything below follows from those two facts.

This is not a futurology segment. The protocols in this lesson have published specifications, reference implementations, license files and steering committees you can find today, and the design decisions they force land in your architecture review this quarter. If you have spent the earlier lessons wondering when this stops being internal plumbing, the answer is: when a stranger's agent asks your agent for something.

## Define the terms

**Agent economy.** A market in which agents act on behalf of principals, buying, selling or performing work across organizational lines, with each exchange recorded in a form the parties can point at later. The word that carries the weight is "recorded." An agent that transacts without leaving evidence is a liability with an API.

**Interoperability.** The property that two systems built by different teams can exchange work without a custom adapter written for that specific pair. It is measured in connections you did not have to build, not in features you happen to share.

**Protocol.** A published, versioned contract that covers message shape, the error cases, and who is allowed to change what. A capability without a written error case is not a protocol. It is a demo that happens to work between two teams who talk on Slack.

**Integration tax.** The cost of connecting systems to each other, which grows with the number of pairs rather than the number of systems. It is the reason protocol work exists, and the arithmetic is in the next section.

**Agent Card.** A JSON metadata document describing an agent's identity, capabilities, service endpoint, skills and authentication requirements, published so that clients can discover the agent and work out how to interact with it securely.[^3] It is a business card you can fetch.

**Opaque agent.** A remote agent you may delegate to but cannot inspect. The A2A project uses the word deliberately: to the client, the remote agent's internal workings, memory and tools stay hidden, and agents interact without sharing internal memory, tools or proprietary logic.[^2][^3] Opacity is what makes cross-vendor delegation tolerable. It is also what makes the contract the whole relationship.

**Task.** A stateful unit of work with a unique identifier and a defined lifecycle, which is the difference between delegating work and sending a message into the dark.[^3]

**Artifact.** The concrete output a remote agent produces during a task, such as a document, an image or structured data, and the thing your system actually receives and has to decide whether to trust.[^3]

**Mandate.** In the Agent Payments Protocol, a tamper-proof, cryptographically signed digital contract that records a user's instruction, signed with verifiable credentials and used as the evidence behind a transaction.[^6] It is the difference between an agent that spends your money and an agent that spends your money inside limits you signed.

**Verifiable credential.** A signed, machine-checkable claim about a party or an instruction. When a credential signs a mandate, authority becomes an object your system can validate rather than an assertion your system has to believe.

**Machine payment.** Settlement between a client and a server per request rather than per relationship, with the A2A x402 extension handling payment inside an agent exchange.[^6] Useful for metered services where a monthly contract would be absurd.

**Standard versus implementation.** A standard is the document. An implementation is one vendor's code that passed that vendor's own tests. Two agents can both be compliant and still disagree about what a message means, and that gap is where the second half of this lesson lives.

## The integration tax is the reason standards exist

Put a number on the problem first. Every pair of systems that must exchange work without a protocol needs its own adapter, and adapters are counted per pair, not per system.

| Systems that must exchange work | Pairwise custom links | Implementations needed with one shared standard |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | 3 | 3 |
| 5 | 10 | 5 |
| 10 | 45 | 10 |
| 20 | 190 | 20 |

Twenty systems that all want to talk to each other is 190 integrations if every pair negotiates privately, and 20 if one published standard sits in the middle as long as each side implements it once. The MCP documentation makes the practical version of this argument: a shared protocol reduces the development time and complexity of building or integrating an AI application or agent, and it exists because the alternative is a connector per data source per consumer.[^1]

Meridian's returns operation is a good place to watch the tax arrive. In lesson 86 the agent had nine internal tools and one owner. By lesson 87 it fed three downstream workflows. Now add three external parties: the carrier's tracking agent, the supplier's warranty agent, and the payment provider that issues refunds. Without a standard, that is three bespoke integrations, each with its own authentication scheme, its own retry behavior and its own idea of what a case identifier looks like. Add a fourth party next quarter and the count goes to four, then six, then ten, and the maintenance owner is whoever touched it last.

There is a second motive that matters more to architects than the adapter count. A protocol hands you a governance answer for free. Someone else maintains the specification, publishes revisions, argues about extensions and runs a conformance story. You inherit that work, and you also inherit its clock.

**Protocol choice is partly a governance question.** A2A was originally developed by Google and donated to the Linux Foundation, and it is maintained by a technical steering committee with representatives from AWS, Cisco, Google, IBM Research, Microsoft, Salesforce, SAP and ServiceNow, with extensions and custom bindings governed by a tiered promotion process so the core stays stable.[^2] Read that list again from a dependency point of view. Your integration's rate of change is set by a committee of vendors that mostly do not compete with each other on this layer, which is a slower and more predictable clock than any single vendor's roadmap. When you review a protocol dependency, ask what happens to your integration if the owner changes direction, and whether the answer includes a published promotion path and a license you can live with.

## The three layers you will meet

Almost every cross-organization conversation lands on one of three layers. Knowing which one you are standing in decides the failure mode you have to instrument.

| Layer | What it standardizes | The concrete construct | What breaks without it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Agent to tool | How an agent reaches tools, data and prompts | MCP servers and clients, described as a USB-C port for AI applications[^1] | A bespoke connector per tool per consumer, and a credential story per connector |
| Agent to agent | How two agents from different vendors discover each other and delegate work | The A2A Agent Card, Task and Artifact, over HTTP with JSON-RPC payloads[^2][^3] | Work delegated by hand, or by a shared database nobody can audit |
| Agent to payment | Who authorized a spend, and how the money settles | AP2 mandates, ACP checkout flows, per-request payment through the x402 extension[^5][^6] | Refunds and purchases that only a human can approve, at human throughput |

The first two layers are complements, not rivals, and the A2A documentation says so directly: MCP standardizes agent-to-tool communication, A2A standardizes agent-to-agent communication, and the two are designed to work together, with MCP equipping an individual agent and A2A letting that agent collaborate across frameworks.[^2] A common wrong reading treats them as competing standards to be adjudicated. They are not. The tool layer is how your agent does its job. The agent layer is how your agent hands part of that job to a system you do not own.

That distinction has a practical consequence for scope. Your MCP surface is an internal attack surface you can inspect, version and test. Your A2A surface is a trust boundary with a counterparty whose code you will never see, which means the controls are contractual and cryptographic rather than code review.

## Discovery is the part that decides whether the economy works

Delegation to a stranger starts with discovery, and discovery runs on the Agent Card. Here is one reduced to six fields, using an illustrative name rather than a real vendor:

```json
{
  "name": "insureco-warranty-agent",
  "endpoint": "https://agents.insureco.example/a2a",
  "capabilities": ["warranty.lookup", "warranty.claim.file"],
  "authentication": {"schemes": ["oauth2"]},
  "skills": ["warranty-eligibility", "claim-intake"],
  "protocolVersion": "pinned-by-caller"
}
```

The card lists identity, the service endpoint, the agent's A2A capabilities, its authentication requirements and its skills, and clients parse it to decide whether the agent fits the task, how to structure requests, and how to communicate securely.[^3] One caution before you treat any of those fields as a fact: a card is a claim made by the counterparty. Your side still has to authenticate the caller, validate what comes back, and decide what your system will accept.

A cold introduction at Meridian looks like this, and each step is a place where a design decision hides:

1. **Discovery.** The returns agent fetches the warranty agent's card, or finds the counterparty through whatever registry the ecosystem runs. The card tells it the endpoint, the auth scheme and the skills on offer.
2. **Scope negotiation before work starts.** Only two of the four advertised skills are relevant to a return, and the caller requests those. Requesting everything the card advertises is how an integration acquires permissions nobody will remember in six months.
3. **Authentication.** Credentials travel in HTTP headers, separate from the protocol messages themselves, with the requirements declared in the card.[^3] That separation is the reason your secret management does not need to know anything about the agent protocol.
4. **Task creation.** The call opens a stateful task with a unique identifier and a defined lifecycle, rather than a single request-response exchange that may or may not still be running.[^3]
5. **Progress.** Long-running warranty claims return over streaming responses, by polling, or through a push notification to a webhook the caller provided, which is the right option for a claim that resolves in three days.[^3]
6. **Artifact.** The remote agent returns a concrete output, in this case a verdict plus the evidence it used, and Meridian's system decides what to do with it.
7. **Recording.** The task identifier, the artifact and the counterparty go into the ledger next to the case, so a dispute three months later has a source.

Step six is where a lot of teams get the design wrong. The artifact arrives from an opaque system, which means it is untrusted input in the same way a web form is untrusted input. A warranty verdict is fine to display to a person. A warranty verdict that triggers an automatic refund of 400 dollars needs a rule, a threshold and a record. The opacity that makes the protocol workable is exactly why you cannot skip that step.

## Authority and money travel with the task

The moment agents transact, a question that payment systems never had to answer appears in the middle of the flow: did this human actually authorize this purchase? AP2 approaches it with mandates, and the mechanism rewards a careful read because it is a design pattern you can steal even if you never touch agentic commerce.

A mandate is a tamper-proof, cryptographically signed digital contract that serves as verifiable proof of a user's instructions, signed by verifiable credentials.[^6] There are two shapes, and they map onto two very different business problems.

**Human present.** You ask an agent to find new running shoes. The request is captured in an intent mandate, which becomes the auditable context for the whole interaction. When the agent presents a cart, your approval signs a cart mandate, which creates an unchangeable record of the exact items and price.[^6] This is the flow most teams mean when they say "human in the loop," with the useful addition that the approval is bound to what was on screen.

**Human not present.** You delegate a task like buying tickets the moment they go on sale. You sign a detailed intent mandate up front that specifies the rules of engagement, including price limits, timing and other conditions, and it serves as verifiable pre-authorized proof that lets the agent generate a cart mandate on your behalf once those conditions are met.[^6] In plain language, the mandate is a sentence like: two replacement filters for the model we discussed, under 90 dollars total, before November 12, from a seller rated 4.5 or better, otherwise ask me. The conditional clause is not decoration. It is the difference between a delegation and a blank check.

![Two mandate shapes side by side: human present, where an intent mandate captures the request and an approval signs the cart mandate, and human not present, where a delegated intent mandate carrying price limits authorizes the agent to sign on your behalf](</diagrams/agent-economies-standards-interoperability/3-mandate-two-shapes.png>)

The chain from intent to cart to payment creates a non-repudiable audit trail that answers the three questions the protocol was built for: authorization, authenticity and accountability.[^6] Translate those into your own review language and they get sharper. Who allowed this action, is the request actually what the principal wanted, and who is accountable when it is wrong. Every agent system you build will eventually be asked all three, and only one of them has a technical answer.

Two more shapes are worth knowing because enterprises will meet them on the commerce side rather than the agent side. The Agentic Commerce Protocol is an open standard, developed by Stripe and OpenAI and published under Apache 2.0, for programmatic commerce flows between buyers, agents and businesses, in which the business stays the merchant of record and decides what can be sold, how it is presented and how orders are fulfilled, and where an agent's transaction can be accepted or declined per agent, per transaction, or by the business's own logic.[^5] That last clause is the one procurement teams read first, because keeping the merchant of record means keeping the customer relationship. The second shape is per-request settlement. The A2A x402 extension applies HTTP payment semantics to individual requests, so a service can be charged per call, and AP2's authors describe it as a production-ready path for agent-based crypto payments developed with Coinbase, the Ethereum Foundation and MetaMask.[^6] Metered rolling payments change the economics of an integration from a contract review to a budget cap, which is why the spend controls from earlier in this course end up doing double duty here.

## What interoperability costs you

Interoperability is a trade, not a win. Five failure modes show up in cross-organization agent work, and only the first one has a well-documented control.

| Failure mode | How it shows up | The control that helps |
|---|---|---|
| Confused deputy | A proxy that speaks for many clients can be tricked into issuing an authorization code to an attacker without the user seeing a consent screen | Per-client consent recorded per user and checked before the third-party authorization flow begins[^4] |
| Token passthrough | A server accepts a token that was not issued to it and forwards it downstream, laundering the audience | Reject tokens whose audience is not this server, and never forward the caller's credential[^4] |
| Opaque result treated as trusted input | An artifact from a counterparty drives an irreversible action in your system | Provenance on the artifact, a threshold, and a named rule for what may be automated |
| Silent version drift | The counterparty upgrades, your fixtures still pass, production starts disagreeing on edge cases | A pinned protocol version, a deprecation watch, and a conformance suite built from real exchanges |
| Inherited outage | Their incident becomes your timeout becomes your backlog | Timeouts, retry budgets and a circuit breaker on the counterparty, plus a queue you can drain |

The confused deputy entry is worth reading in full, because the conditions are specific and you can check them against your own architecture in twenty minutes. The attack becomes possible when an MCP proxy server uses a static client ID with a third-party authorization server, allows clients to register dynamically, and relies on a consent cookie that the third party set on the first legitimate authorization, with no per-client consent check of its own.[^4] Those four conditions together let an attacker register a client, send a crafted authorization request to a user whose browser still holds the consent cookie, have consent skipped, and receive the authorization code at a redirect URI they control.[^4] The required protections are unglamorous and exact: keep a registry of approved client identifiers per user, check it before starting the third-party flow, validate redirect URIs by exact string match rather than pattern, make state values single-use with a short expiry, and never set the consent cookie or session before the user has approved the consent screen.[^4]

The generalizable lesson is that anything acting as an intermediary for another party's identity multiplies its own trust surface. A proxy that fronts five systems with one client ID is not five integrations. It is one credential holding five sets of permissions, and the blast radius belongs to whoever wrote the proxy.

![The four conditions that let a confused deputy attack succeed placed beside the four controls that stop it, with the outcome described for the case where all four conditions hold](</diagrams/agent-economies-standards-interoperability/4-confused-deputy-conditions-controls.png>)

**Four controls that survive a change of counterparty.** Pin the protocol version and watch for deprecation announcements rather than tracking the latest build. Give each counterparty its own credential with the narrowest scope the task needs, so revocation is a single action. Require artifacts to carry provenance, meaning which agent produced them, under which task identifier, from which source records. And keep a conformance suite made of real exchanges you have captured, because the disagreement that matters is never in the happy path.

## Exam traps

**Calling a standard and an implementation the same thing.** Two compliant agents can still disagree about what a refund means, and compliance certificates will not catch it. Test semantics with fixtures, not with the specification's wording.

**Assuming interoperability removes the need for a contract.** It removes the need for a custom adapter. The commercial questions, from liability to data use to uptime expectations, are untouched by any protocol.

**Trusting an Agent Card as a statement of fact.** A card is a claim by the counterparty about itself, and you still authenticate the caller and validate the payload.[^3]

**Choosing a protocol by vendor popularity.** Ask who can change the specification, on what cadence, with what deprecation policy, and what your exit looks like. Governance is a feature.

**Treating agent-to-tool and agent-to-agent as competing standards.** MCP and A2A solve different problems and are designed to work together, so a review that forces a choice between them has the layering wrong.[^2]

**Leaving a proxy with a shared credential.** The confused deputy conditions are checkable, and a proxy with a static client ID plus dynamic client registration plus no per-client consent is the pattern they describe.[^4]

**Letting a counterparty's artifact fire an irreversible action.** Interoperability gives you the artifact. It gives you nothing about whether the artifact is right.

## Try it

Take one system you own and spend a week on these six steps.

1. Count the pairwise integrations in one business process. Write the number of systems, then the number of custom links that exist today, then the number you would have with a shared standard. The gap is the argument you will need in a funding conversation.
2. Write an Agent Card for one of your own agents, using the fields the A2A concepts use: identity, endpoint, capabilities, skills, authentication requirements.[^3] Publish it internally. Writing down authentication requirements forces the conversation about scope.
3. Draft one pre-authorized mandate in plain language for a task you would delegate: what may be bought, the ceiling, the deadline, the condition that voids it, and the fallback when the condition fails. Then decide which fields your system can actually enforce today.
4. Audit every proxy or gateway that holds a credential on behalf of more than one caller. Score it against the four confused deputy conditions from MCP's security guidance, and fix per-client consent before anything else.[^4]
5. Delegate one real task to a third-party agent in a sandbox, then write down what came back: was there a task identifier, a version, a source record, and a named producer. Anything missing is a provenance gap in your ledger.
6. Pick the one protocol you already depend on most and write its governance paragraph: owner, license, who can change the specification, revision cadence, and your exit. If you cannot fill in the exit, you have found the dependency to watch this quarter.

Step five is the one that produces an uncomfortable answer for most teams, and it is the fastest way to see whether your agent is interoperable or merely connected.

## Summary

- Standards exist to cut the integration tax, which grows with pairs of systems rather than with systems, and they hand you a governance answer as a side effect.
- Three layers cover most cross-organization work: MCP for agent to tool, A2A for agent to agent, and AP2, ACP and x402 for who may spend and how it settles.[^1][^2][^5][^6]
- Discovery runs on the Agent Card, and a card is a claim by the counterparty rather than a fact your system may rely on.[^3]
- Authority travels as data. A signed mandate with price limits, timing and conditions is what separates delegation from a blank check.[^6]
- Interoperability widens the trust surface. Any component that holds one credential for many callers is the pattern the confused deputy attack relies on, and per-client consent is the control.[^4]
- Choose protocols by governance, version the boundary, give each counterparty its own scoped credential, and require provenance on every artifact.
