# Lesson 50: Privacy: PII, Data Minimization, Retention, and Consent for AI

Lesson 49 secured the network: it controlled who reached the agent and what the agent was allowed to move out of it. That answered the question of access. Privacy is a different axis, and it comes first in the design. An agent can have perfect network security and still be illegal, because it was built to ingest a person's data, keep it forever, and process it without ever establishing a lawful ground. Security governs who can act. Privacy governs what may be done with information about a person at all.

The one idea to carry out of this lesson: **an agent that touches someone's personal data is allowed to touch the least of it, for the shortest time, on a defensible legal basis, and the person it concerns has to be able to tell what was done and stop it.** Every agent pipeline translates that sentence into four concrete controls. PII has to be recognized, because a system cannot protect data it cannot see. Data minimization has to be in the architecture, not the prompt. Retention has to expire the data automatically. And the lawful basis, often consent, has to be real, not a checkbox.

## What privacy means for an agent

Privacy is the discipline of giving individuals control over how information about them is collected, used, and disclosed. For an AI system most of that control law takes the form of the EU General Data Protection Regulation, or GDPR, and the strongest statements of the boundaries come from its principles of processing. The GDPR does not ban processing personal data. It makes processing lawful only under conditions, and it makes the controller, the organization that decides why and how the data is processed, accountable for being able to prove those conditions were met. [^1]

An agent complicates this in ways an ordinary database does not. A database holds rows you can inventory. An agent holds data in motion: the prompts users type, the context it retrieves, the tool calls it makes, the trace it records, and, if you train or fine-tune on it, the data that ends up baked into model weights. Every one of those is processing under the GDPR, and every one of them is a place where a privacy failure happens. The same property that makes agents useful, that they gather and act on context, is the property that makes them privacy-heavy.

So the framing difference from lesson 49 matters. Security asks "can an attacker get this out?" Privacy asks "was it ever lawful for us to put a person's data in here in the first place, what exactly did we take, how long are we keeping it, and can they pull it out of our influence?" The answers to the privacy questions determine how much data the security layer even has to protect.

## PII: recognizing what counts as personal data

The first control is recognition. You cannot minimize, retain, or get consent for data you never identify as personal in the first place.

The GDPR defines personal data as any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person, a "data subject." [^2] The phrase "identifiable" is wider than the phrase "identified." A name is obviously personal data. So is a user ID, an email address, a phone number, an IP address, a cookie identifier, and any other identifier that can be used, directly or in combination with other information, to single a person out. [^2] The GDPR's own definition lists identifiers such as name, identification number, location data, and online identifiers, and says that which factors count depends on what can reasonably be used to identify someone. That last clause is what makes the category a moving target for agents: an agent that holds a list of "just email addresses" is still holding personal data even though no name appears alongside.

PII is the practical, industry term for this same class. Audit and governance standards often talk about PII as the set of data that could identify an individual, alone or combined, and the use of the term usually signals that the author is describing a concrete data-handling regime rather than the GDPR's full legal frame. [^3] The overlap is what matters for your implementation: when you design the data pipeline, treat everything that could reasonably point back to a person as data that needs the privacy controls.

Two refinements make the boundary sharper for agents.

The first is that context turns a neutral value into personal data. A customer ID with no accompanying information looks like a random string, but the moment the agent pulls the account record that ID points to, the two together relate to a person. Recognition has to happen across the assembled context the agent builds, not field by field in isolation.

The second is that some personal data carries a higher bar. The GDPR singles out "special categories," the data about racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious beliefs, trade-union membership, health, sex life or sexual orientation, and genetic and biometric data, and generally prohibits processing them unless one of a narrow set of conditions applies. [^4] For an agent this matters because seemingly benign inputs can contain special-category signals, a support ticket that mentions a medical condition, a document that references an ethnic background, a photo that could be used for biometric matching moves that input into a much stricter legal lane. The recognition layer has to be able to flag the strict cases, not just the obvious ones like names and phone numbers.

## Data minimization: keep the least it needs

Data minimization is the GDPR principle that personal data be adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purpose for which it is processed. [^5] It is the closest thing the GDPR has to an architectural command, and in the agent world it is the most powerful privacy control you will build, because it reduces the blast radius of every other control downstream.

The principle sounds obvious and is routinely violated. The reason it is routinely violated is that agents want context, and teams read "give the model the whole thing" as the way to make the agent smart. Minimization is the corrective: give the model exactly the fields the task needs, and nothing that names more people, reveals more about them, or extends further into the past than the task requires.

For an agent, minimization shows up in concrete places.

The retrieval filter is minimization. When the agent answers a question, the retrieved context should include only the records relevant to that question, not a slurp of everything available. A support agent that fetches a customer's full profile to answer "what is the status of order 182" has already over-collected, because the shipping address, payment method, and past orders were not necessary for that purpose. The minimization test is per request, not per persona.

The tool interface is minimization. The least privilege you granted in the identity lesson applies to data as much as to actions: the tool call should pass the specific identifier and the specific fields needed, not the entire object graph it happens to have access to. A function that returns the whole customer row because the function happens to exist is a minimization failure even if no one reads the extra fields, because the data entered the context where it did not need to be.

The input transformation is minimization. Where a task genuinely needs a person's data but not its identifying form, redact or tokenize first. Lesson 18 showed you Presidio and category-label masking for output; the input side of the same discipline means replacing an email with `[EMAIL]` or a customer number with a token before the value ever reaches the model. If the model only needs to know "this is the same customer as last week," it does not need the actual customer number.

The privacy reason for minimization is that data that never enters the agent cannot leak, cannot be memorized, cannot be retained, and does not need consent beyond what the narrow purpose required. Lesson 49 made the security version of this point with DLP. The definitions line up cleanly: fewer real customer bytes held means less the DLP layer has to catch, and less the minimization reviewer has to defend. [^5] They are the same design decision reached from two directions.

## Retention: put a clock on the data

Retention is the companion principle to minimization. The GDPR's storage limitation principle says personal data must be kept in a form which permits identification of data subjects for no longer than is necessary for the purposes for which the personal data is processed. [^6] In plain terms: you may keep a person's data only as long as you still have a lawful reason to have it, and once the purpose is served, the data should go.

For an agent the retention problem is that everything is a copy, and copies proliferate. The prompt the user typed is a copy. The context the retrieval step assembled is a copy. The trace the observability layer recorded is a copy. The embeddings in the vector store are a copy, usually one that searches without ever looking like a name. The log line that records what the agent did is a copy. If you only delete the user's source record, you have deleted one copy out of six, and the agent is still retaining personal data.

So retention for agents has to be applied per store, not once. Decide the retention window for each place personal data lands, and make the deletion automatic so it happens without a human remembering to run it. The vector store needs a TTL on embeddings that correspond to personal records. The trace store needs a policy that expires the prompts and retrieved context while keeping the metadata needed to reconstruct the action for the audit trail from lesson 29. The key distinction holds: you keep the metadata that proves what happened, you expire the personal payload that exposes people. [^6]

![Retention per store: deleting the source record removes one copy out of six, so each store where personal data lands, the prompt, the retrieved context, the trace, the embeddings, and the log line, gets its own retention window applied automatically](</diagrams/privacy-pii-data-minimization-retention-consent/4-retention-per-store.png>)

The EU AI Act reinforces this from the training direction. For high-risk AI systems built from data, it requires data governance and management practices that ask, among other things, about the original purpose of the data collection. [^7] That includes examining relevance, sufficiency, and error-freedom, and it applies the data-governance obligations for high-risk systems in a way that operates alongside the GDPR rather than replacing it. [^7] A training set is not exempt from storage limitation just because it is training data. If the collection purpose is spent, keeping the personal data inside the training set is a retention problem too.

## Consent: the lawful basis that is easy to get wrong

Minimization and retention limit what you keep. Consent is one of the grounds on which you may process it at all. The GDPR makes processing lawful only if at least one of a set of lawful bases applies, and consent is the one most people name first. [^8] It is also the one most often misapplied.

The GDPR defines consent as any freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous indication of the data subject's wishes by which they, by a statement or by a clear affirmative action, signify agreement to the processing of their personal data. [^2] Parse those four words, because each is a separate test.

Freely given means the individual has genuine choice and is not cornered into agreeing. It cannot be bundled so that refusing to consent means losing the service they are actually using. [^2] An agent that will not run unless the user consents to its data being used for model training has probably not met this test, because consent is presumed not freely given if the service is conditional on consent that is not necessary to provide the service. [^2]

Specific means consent is tied to a particular purpose, not a blanket "we may process your data." Separate consent options are needed for different processing purposes. [^2] An agent that asks for one consent covering both "answer your support question" and "train a model on your question" has collapsed two distinct purposes into one box, and that is a specific-specificity failure.

Informed means the individual is told who is processing the data, what will be done with it, and for what purpose, in clear and plain language, before they consent. [^2] This is a transparency obligation up front, not a privacy policy buried in a settings screen.

Unambiguous and affirmative means consent has to be a clear action, a checkbox, a button click, not a pre-ticked box or silence. Pre-ticked boxes are not a valid mechanism for consent.

Two operational consequences follow, and the GDPR states them as hard requirements. The controller has to be able to demonstrate that consent was given, which means the agent has to record the consent event, its date, its version of the terms, and its granularity, not just assume it. [^9] And the individual has the right to withdraw consent at any time, with withdrawal as easy as giving it, and withdrawal not affecting the lawfulness of processing that happened before withdrawal. [^9] For an agent this means consent state has to be looked up on every session, and a withdrawal has to be honored for future processing while the past processing stays valid.

The European Data Protection Board's guidance on consent under the GDPR consolidates these requirements and stresses the two-part nature of the test: consent must be both **unambiguous** and given by a **clear affirmative action**, and it cannot be tied to an imbalance of power between the individual and the controller. [^10] The Board also emphasizes that consent must be distinct and separate from other matters, and that controllers must be able to demonstrate compliance. [^10]

For AI specifically, there is a tension worth naming plainly: the data you most want to keep for training, the natural prompts, the interactions, the whole corpus of what real users asked, is exactly the data on which consent is hardest to collect cleanly and easiest to withdraw. A consent form that promises "we will train on your prompts" meets a hard standard of specificity and freedom long before a broad click-through does. The practical move is to treat consent as the default name for the legal basis only where the GDPR actually expects it, and to recognize that other lawful bases exist and may be more accurate for ordinary operations. But consent is a real and often unavoidable basis, and when you use it, the agent has to honor every word of the definition rather than approximating it.

## Worked example: a support agent that holds the least, for the shortest time

Put the four controls together on the customer support agent from lesson 49, and watch the privacy layer change what the security layer has to protect.

The request arrives. The user asks, "what is the status of order 182?" The agent needs only the order status. It does not need the customer's full profile, their address, their payment method, or their history. The retrieval filter fetches the order record only, and the tool interface passes the order ID and returns the status field, not the whole row. That is minimization applied in two places. [^5]

The agent recognizes what it is holding. The order status itself is not personal data, but the account it belongs to is, and the session knows which account it is. The recognition layer tags the data as relating to a person so the retention and consent controls know they apply. If the ticket had mentioned a medical condition, the same layer would flag special-category data and route the case to the stricter lane. [^4]

It checks consent before it acts. The session looks up the user's current consent state: for this purpose, answering a support question about their own order, on this version of terms. The GDPR requires the agent to be able to demonstrate that consent is specific to this purpose, not a blanket grant from an unrelated screen. [^9] If consent is missing or withdrawn for a purpose that requires it, the agent refuses rather than processing anyway.

It retains only the metadata. The trace records that the agent answered the order-status question, on which model version, in which session, at what time, for the audit trail. It does not keep the full prompt or the retrieved order record past a short window. A TTL expires the personal payload while the metadata survives for the audit horizon from lesson 29. [^6]

The consent is withdrawable. The user can change or withdraw consent from the same control they used to give it, as easy to withdraw as to give, and the next session picks up the change. [^9] Past lawfulness stands; future processing stops.

That is the whole shape: recognize the PII, keep the least of it, expire it automatically, and process it only under a real, demonstrable, withdrawable basis. None of those four on their own is enough. Together they are the privacy layer that determines which data the security architecture from lesson 49 even has to defend.

## The pitfalls to avoid

The first is thinking minimization is a prompt instruction. Telling the model "only use necessary data" is a request, not a control. The model may comply and may not, and the minimization failure happens before the model is ever called, in what the retrieval step assembled and what the tool returned. Minimization belongs in the architecture: what you fetch, what you pass, what you transform, not in the system prompt.

The second is applying retention once instead of per store. The source record is one copy. The prompt, the context, the trace, and the embeddings are separate copies, and each needs its own expiration. Delete the source row and the agent still holds the person's data in three other places.

The third is treating any click as consent. A pre-ticked box, a bundled agreement, a terms page the user never reads, none of these meet the definition. Consent has to be freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous, and the agent has to be able to prove it was given and let the user withdraw it as easily as they gave it. [^2] [^9]

The fourth is applying consent where it does not fit. Consent is one lawful basis, not the only one, and forcing everything through a shaky consent flow when another basis is more accurate does not make the processing lawful. It makes the consent dishonest, and the recorder of a fake consent is the least defensible position to be in. [^8]

## Try it

1. **Classify a request.** Take a real tool call your agent makes and list every data field it passes. Mark each field personal or not, directly or assembled. Then answer a per-request minimization test: is every field necessary for that specific task, or is any of it there because the function happens to return it? [^5]

2. **Map the retention copies.** Trace one user request through your agent: the prompt, the retrieved context, the trace, the log, the embeddings if any. Write down each store where a copy lands and a proposed TTL for each. Mark which copies carry the personal payload and which carry only the metadata the audit trail needs. [^6]

3. **Audit a consent flow.** Find where your agent, or a product you use, asks for consent. Test it against the four words: is it freely given, or is the service conditional on it? Is it specific to one purpose, or does one box cover several? Is it informed in clear language, and is it a clear affirmative action rather than a pre-ticked box? Then check whether the system can demonstrate the consent was given and make withdrawal as easy as giving it. [^2] [^9]

## What's next

The privacy layer decides what an agent may hold about the people it serves and on what basis. The next lesson, **Agent identity: OAuth, service accounts, and scoped permissions**, flips the direction: instead of protecting people from the agent, it proves who the agent itself is, a machine actor presenting credentials to the systems it touches. Next you will control what it may do as a trusted machine.
