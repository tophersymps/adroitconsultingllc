You built the automation. It took effort, you hit the right settings, you got the output you wanted. Then a month passes. You open the tool and you cannot remember which prompt produced the good version, what the workflow depends on, or why you set that one checkbox the way you did. The automation still runs, but the knowledge of how it runs lives in your head, and heads forget, drift, and get swapped out for other concerns. That gap, between the working tool and the missing record of how it works, is what this lesson is about closing.

The skill under examination is documentation. Not the documentation you write for an audience, but the documentation you write for the only person guaranteed to need it later: yourself. This is the lesson where the patterns from every earlier step, the template prompts from Lesson 7, the review gates from Lesson 13, the tests from Lesson 10, the version log from Lesson 9, all get written down into one place that outlasts your memory of them.

## What a runbook is, in plain words

A runbook is a written record of how to run, operate, and repair a system. The term comes from IT operations, where a runbook collects the routine procedures that keep a system healthy: how to start it, how to stop it, how to monitor it, and what to do when something goes wrong. [^1] The word matter-of-factly describes its purpose: it is the "how to run this thing" book.

A plain way to hold the definition is this. A runbook is the answer to the question, "If I knew nothing about how I built this, what would I need in front of me to run it safely and fix it when it breaks?" It is an anti-amnesia document. It exists so that a procedure survives the person who first figured it out.

Three terms anchor the idea.

A **personal runbook** is the same document, scaled to one person's own tools. You are not running a server fleet, you are running five automations in a messaging app, a spreadsheet, and a scheduling tool. The personal runbook captures how those five work so that one person, you, three months from now, can pick them up again. It borrows the discipline from IT without importing the bureaucracy.

The **automation stack** is the whole set of tools and workflows you rely on to get your work done without doing it by hand. It is your prompts, your connected apps, your scheduled jobs, your templates, and the rules you follow for using them. The word stack emphasizes that these are not isolated things. They layer on each other, they share inputs, and they break in ways that ripple. A runbook describes the stack the way a map describes a city: it shows what exists and how the parts connect.

A **single source of truth** is the one place a piece of information lives so you never have to wonder which of three scattered notes is current. When every fact about an automation points to one document, the record stays consistent. When facts scatter across three apps, nobody, especially you, knows which one is right. The AWS Well-Architected guidance on runbooks makes the underlying goal explicit: a runbook should contain the desired outcome, the tools and permissions needed, and instructions for handling errors. [^2] Those three things, stated once and kept in one place, are a complete record for any single workflow.

## Why the record matters more than the workflow

It is tempting to treat documentation as the boring cleanup after the real work. This lesson argues the opposite: for a system you intend to keep, the record is the asset and the workflow is the thing that needs the record to stay useful.

Consider what actually degrades over time. An automation that goes unmaintained typically does not fail loudly and all at once. It quietly drifts. The tool updates, and a setting you chose stops meaning what it meant. A colleague changes a data file's column order, and your template now reads the wrong field. You get promoted or change roles, and the person who inherits your process finds a working tool with no instructions, which is one debugging session away from a dead tool. Every one of those outcomes is a documentation failure, not a technical one. The tool was fine. The knowledge of how to keep it fine evaporated.

This is why every mature operations discipline treats documentation as load-bearing. A runbook allows other operators, people with the right background but not your memory, to manage and troubleshoot a system effectively. [^1] The phrase "with the right background but not your memory" is the whole point. A good runbook does not need to teach the subject from zero. It needs to hand someone who already knows the domain exactly the specific details you would otherwise carry in your head: what this particular automation depends on, what good output looks like, and where it tends to break.

![Two paths for the same working automation: without a written record the tool quietly drifts and dies, with a runbook someone with the right background but not your memory can run and repair it](</diagrams/documenting-your-automation-stack-a-personal-runbook/2-with-vs-without-record.png>)

There is also a quieter reason the record matters. Writing something down changes your relationship to it. The act of documenting forces you to understand the thing you built. You cannot write a clear runbook entry for an automation you do not actually understand, because the entry requires you to name the dependency, the trigger, and the failure mode. The moment you write it, you either confirm you understand the workflow or you discover the gaps in your understanding. Both are wins. Documenting is not just preserving what you know, it is the fastest way to find out what you do not know yet.

## The worked example: Hamid documents his five automations

Hamid works in finance and, over this course, built himself a small automation stack. He does not think of it as software engineering. He thinks of it as "the scripts that do my week." By the time he reaches this lesson, he runs five things: a daily expense report assembler, a weekly vendor-payment reminder, a monthly budget-variance checker, a request-intake form that routes to the right folder, and an approval-draft generator. Each one started as a one-off prompt in earlier lessons and grew into a repeatable workflow.

For five weeks he has told himself he will write it all down soon. He has not. The details are still in his head because they are recent. Then he gets a good reason: he is moving to a new team in six weeks, and someone will inherit his process. If he does not write it down, his replacement gets five working black boxes and a guess.

Hamid decides to write a personal runbook. He does it in a way that matches this lesson's advice: one document, one entry per automation, kept short enough to maintain. Here is the entry he writes for the monthly budget-variance checker, the automation he is proudest of and the one he most wants to survive.

> **Monthly budget-variance checker**
>
> **Purpose:** Each month, flag the accounts where actual spend ran more than ten percent over budget, so the finance lead can review them before close.
>
> **Trigger:** Runs on the first business day of the month. I set it to run automatically; no manual start needed.
>
> **Inputs:** The current month's ledger export and the budget sheet. The ledger has one row per transaction; the budget sheet has one row per account. I upload both to the same folder the workflow watches.
>
> **Output:** A list of flagged accounts: account name, actual spend, budget, and the variance as a percentage. It only lists accounts over ten percent.
>
> **What good looks like:** About three to six accounts flagged in a normal month, each with a variance over ten percent. If nothing is flagged, something is off and I check the date range in the export.
>
> **Common failure and fix:** Last month it flagged everything. The fix was that the ledger export was missing the "account" column header, so every row looked like it matched every budget row. Fix: confirm the export has all column headers before uploading.
>
> **Owner:** Hamid. Anyone on the team can run it, but until the handoff I am the one who checks the output each month.

The entry is not long, and that is a feature, not a defect. A runbook you dread updating never gets updated. Hamid sized each entry so it takes him a few minutes to write and, more importantly, a few minutes to keep current when he changes something.

That last line is the discipline that makes the whole thing work. Hamid decides that whenever he changes an automation, he updates the runbook entry at the same time, in the same sitting. He borrows the habit from Lesson 9's versioning rule. A change to the workflow without a change to the record is a change that erases its own documentation. Keeping the two moves together is what stops the runbook from decaying into a historical curiosity.

## What belongs in a runbook entry

Hamid's entry is a useful skeleton because it maps to the questions your future self will actually ask. Structure a personal runbook entry around those questions and it will serve you far longer than any prose summary.

A complete entry answers six questions.

**What does it do?** One or two sentences on the purpose. This is the line that lets you decide, months later, whether this automation is still worth keeping, without re-reading the whole thing.

**When does it run?** The trigger. Is it scheduled, is it event-driven, or does someone start it by hand? Naming the trigger tells you where to look when the output does not appear. If it should have run and did not, the trigger is the first suspect.

**What does it need?** The inputs and the dependencies. What files, what data, what other tools, what permissions. This is the section that saves you from the "it broke overnight" investigation, because more often than not the thing that broke was an input, not the automation itself.

**What does good output look like?** A description of the healthy result, with any expected range or shape. This is your baseline for "is today's output wrong?" without a baseline, you cannot tell a normal result from a broken one. Lesson 18's quality-bar thinking belongs exactly here.

**Where does it tend to break?** The known failure modes and the fixes for them. You do not need to predict every future failure. You need to capture the ones you have already solved, because rote repetition of a diagnosis you have made before is the purest waste of your time.

**Who owns it and where does it live?** The owner and the location of the underlying prompt, template, or configuration. If the workflow lives in a tool that stores versions, name where those versions are kept, so you can roll back when a later lesson's lesson 9 discipline demands it.

Six questions is not a rigid template every entry must fill with a paragraph. Some entries will need a long failure-and-fix section and a one-line purpose. Others will be mostly trigger and inputs. The skeleton is a reminder of what to consider, not a quota you must hit in every box. Miss an answer today and the entry is still useful; the completed answers are the ones that will save you.

## Documentation as the bridge to sharing

There is one more reason to take the record seriously, and it points directly at the next lesson. Documentation is not only about preserving your own knowledge. It is the raw material for sharing that knowledge with other people.

The design decision in this course has been consistent: the transferable pattern is what matters, and concrete tools are only ever examples. A personal runbook written from that same principle is already most of the way to a shareable one. When you write an entry that describes the purpose, the inputs, the trigger, the failure modes, and the owner of an automation, you have written a document that a colleague with the right background can follow. The step from "my runbook" to "our runbook" is not a rewrite. It is a small edit: change the owner line and soften the first-person voice, and the entry stops being private and starts being a handoff.

This is why the documentation lesson sits where it does in the course, between sustaining your own workflow and multiplying it across a team. You cannot hand over what you have never written down. The entry Hamid wrote for the budget checker is, with a few pronoun changes, the document his successor will open on day one. The work of documenting is not overhead on top of building automations. It is the thing that makes everything you have built transferable.

The proof of a well-documented automation is simple. If you were hit by a bus and a thoughtful colleague with no memory of your process opened your runbook, could they keep your five workflows running? If they could, you have documented them. If they would have to call you, or guess, or start from the tool and reverse-engineer it, the work of documentation is not done yet.

## Build a runbook entry for one of your automations

Give yourself twenty minutes to write the first entry of your personal runbook. You are not writing a manual for the whole course content. You are writing one entry that proves the habit works for you.

Step one, pick the target. Choose the automation you run most often or the one you would most hate to lose. That is the one with the highest payoff for a written record, and it is the one you know well enough to document without research.

Step two, answer the six questions. Write one entry using the skeleton from this lesson: what it does, when it runs, what it needs, what good output looks like, where it tends to break, and who owns it. If you cannot answer all six, answer the ones you can and note the gaps; the gaps are the honest measure of what you still do not understand about your own workflow.

Step three, make the habit explicit. Decide the moment when you will update the entry, and tie it to a change you already make. The simplest rule, the one that works, is: whenever you edit the automation, edit the entry in the same sitting. No separate scheduling, no remembering later. The two edits are one act.

> Runbook entry for: [the name of your automation]
> Purpose: [one or two sentences on what it does]
> Trigger: [when and how it starts]
> Inputs: [the files, data, tools, and permissions it needs]
> Good output: [what a healthy result looks like, with any expected range]
> Known failures and fixes: [the ones you have already solved]
> Owner and location: [who runs it and where the prompt or template lives]

Step four, keep it honest. A runbook that describes the workflow the way you wish it worked, instead of the way it works, is worse than none, because it sends your future self down the wrong path with confidence. If you do not actually know what triggers the automation, write "trigger unknown, need to check," and check it. A truthful entry with a gap is a roadmap; a polished entry with a silent guess is a trap.
