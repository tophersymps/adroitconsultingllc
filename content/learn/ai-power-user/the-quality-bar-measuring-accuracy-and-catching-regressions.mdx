The last two lessons did something important before this one could exist. They showed you, in full, how a workflow is built and where its pieces catch a failure. By the end of them you have a running workflow you own, one that pulls from a source of truth, runs its steps, and pauses at a review gate. This lesson asks the question that has to come next: how do you know it is still good?

Not how you know it once, at the moment you finish building it. How you know it next week, and the week after, and the first time you change a prompt or your tool vendor rolls out a new version. Building a workflow is one skill. Keeping one honest is a separate one, and it is the subject of this lesson. A workflow you built that nobody can judge is a workflow you had best stay nervous about.

## Define the terms

Three terms carry this lesson, and they are worth holding separately because people use them loosely and that sloppiness is how quality quietly drains away.

A quality bar is the repeated measurement you use to tell whether a workflow's output is actually good. The definition matters as much as the number. The Quality Bar is not a single score you compute once and pin on the wall. It is a habit, a small set of checks you run on a rhythm, so that "is this still working" stops being a feeling and becomes an answer. Label Studio, the data annotation company, describes the underlying practice this way: AI evaluation is the structured process of determining whether a model performs well enough for its intended use. [^3] The key word is structured. You are not asking "does the output feel right." You are asking a question you can answer with evidence, on a schedule, the same way every time.

Accuracy is how often a workflow produces correct output. In this course accuracy is treated as a checkbox, not a silver bullet. For a workflow where the output either meets the spec or does not, accuracy is the share of cases that meet it. A report that lands on the right numbers in 8 of 10 runs is 80 percent accurate on those ten. Accuracy is simple, it is answerable, and it is never the whole story, because it says nothing about how bad the misses are. Label Studio makes exactly this point: accuracy alone is rarely sufficient, depending on the task you may need to weigh precision, consistency, failure rates, or how costly an error is. [^3] Keep accuracy as your starting measurement, and treat it as a floor that other judgments build on, not a finish line.

A regression is a change that makes something that was working correctly stop working correctly. GitHub, which has published a careful plain-language explanation of the term, defines a regression as a bug that occurs when a change introduces unintended side effects or breaks existing functionality that was working correctly before. [^1] In a codebase a regression is usually blamed on a line of code. In a workflow a regression looks different: you edit a prompt, you swap in a new model version, your review gate changes how it passes drafts, and suddenly a step that produced good output for weeks starts producing bad output. Nothing obvious broke. The tool still runs, the connection still works, the output still looks formatted. It is just wrong now, in a way it was not wrong before. That is the regression, and it is the silent killer of automation because it arrives without a crash.

## Why a working workflow can quietly stop working

Before the method, the diagnosis. People who use AI day to day get surprised the first time a workflow they trusted starts failing, because it feels like the tool changed its mind. A large language model is not deterministic the way a spreadsheet formula is. Mastra, the AI framework company, states the core problem directly: the same prompt can produce different outputs on successive runs, so you cannot rely on simple equality checks, and there is a wide gap between "it runs" and "it works correctly." [^2] The output comes from a probability distribution, not from a fixed rule. That means a workflow does not have an obvious "breaking point" you can spot by eye the way you can spot a broken formula or a missing field.

This is the sentence to remember from this lesson: a workflow can be running perfectly and still be failing. The tool fires, the connection resolves, the steps execute, the output is well formatted, and every one of those things can be true while the answer is quietly wrong, a salary misfigured, a client name misspelled, a statistic invented. That is the it-runs versus it-works-correctly gap Mastra warns about, and it is exactly where regressions hide. [^2] Your review gate from Lesson 13 catches some of this, because a human reads the output before it ships. [^13] But a gate catches what it happens to look at. The quality bar is the tool that makes you look systematically, at the same cases, every time, so a regression gets caught because you are measuring accuracy on a set you already know the answer to, not because you got lucky.

## The golden set, the heart of the bar

Here is the practical move, and it does not require any technical skill. Build a golden set.

A golden set is a small collection of test cases where you already know what correct looks like. Each case is a specific input to your workflow paired with the output you have hand-verified as correct. Mastra calls this the single source of truth for quality assessment, the checkpoint every release must pass. [^2] The size surprises people. You do not need hundreds of cases. You need a dozen or so cases that cover the routine and a couple of the tricky edges, the cases where your workflow is most likely to drift, and where a wrong answer would cost the most. Ten well-chosen cases beat fifty generic ones, because the value is not the count, it is that you hand-verified the answers once and can now use them forever as a ruler.

Building the set is a one-time afternoon. The first time your workflow produces good output, keep the good examples instead of throwing them away. For each case, write down three things: what you asked, what the workflow produced, and a one-line note on why that answer is correct, the fact it needed to land, the tone it needed to hold, the numbers it needed to match. Then grade the workflow on the set once to get your baseline. Baseline is not a fancy word. It is just the accuracy number you start from, the one you measure later runs against. This is the practice your versioning lesson already pointed at: a baseline and a changelog let you trace what changed and roll it back when it breaks. [^9] The golden set is the baseline made concrete for quality.

## The worked example: Priya's weekly report

Meet Priya. She is a finance operations person at a mid-sized company, and since Lesson 16 she has run a weekly revenue report as a workflow: it pulls sales data, drafts the summary, and drafts the commentary that goes into the Monday leadership email. [^16] For three weeks the report has been good. Her review gate signs off on it every Monday, and nothing has gone wrong. She trusts it, and that trust is exactly why she needs a bar.

Her golden set is eleven cases. Ten of them are routine weeks, ordinary revenue, a recovery in one region, a flat week in another, each with the numbers she verified when she built the workflow. The eleventh is the edge case that bit her once, a week where a big enterprise deal closed on the last day and the normal drafting buried it instead of leading with it; she kept the corrected output as a case so the workflow never forgets how to handle it. She writes the top line down: one number the draft must report, one note on the emphasis, and she grades each case pass or fail against those.

Her baseline comes out at 9 of 11. Good enough to ship, with two known weak spots she has on her list. She records it in her changelog, the habit from Lesson 9, so she has a record of where she started. [^9]

Then the regression happens. On the fourth week her tool vendor ships an update to the drafting model. The report still runs, the format is unchanged, the numbers look clean to her review gate. But when Priya sorts the eleven cases and re-runs the set, the grade is 5 of 11. Six cases that passed last week fail this week. The model version changed the way it phrases a softening in revenue, and it keeps burying the finance language her team depends on. Nothing crashed. No error logged. Only the set, re-run against the same eleven cases, revealed that a change she did not make had quietly degraded the workflow. [^2] That is the regression, caught by measurement, not by luck.

The fix is boring and that is the point. She checks her changelog, confirms the element that changed is the model version, and rolls the drafting step back to the previous version, the rollback your versioning lesson made possible. [^9] Re-running the set, she is back to 9 of 11. Then she logs the whole episode in the changelog: what changed, what the set caught, what she did. Next time the vendor ships an update, she re-runs the set again before she lets it into Monday's workflow.

## Here is how to measure without breeding distrust

The trap people fall into when they first learn about a quality bar is to treat it as a verdict on the tool or on themselves, a score to defend. That is the wrong frame. The bar is a tripwire and a map, not a grade. Treat it that way and it earns its keep.

First, the golden set answers the question "did the thing I know about break?" The re-run after every change is the regression check. GitHub's plain-language guide describes the practice directly: regression testing re-runs selected cases after a change to make sure existing functionality still works as expected, and it exists because minor alterations can unexpectedly impact seemingly unrelated functions. [^1] Your golden set is that selected set for your workflow. Re-run it whenever you edit a prompt, swap a model, change a template, update a connected tool, or move a step. If the set still passes, ship the change with confidence. If a case that used to pass fails, you found a regression before it reached a real report. [^2]

Second, the bar is a map because a change in accuracy tells you where to look, not just that something is wrong. If accuracy drops only on the edge-case weeks, the drafting step likely lost its handling of edge cases. If it drops on the routine cases too, the change is probably structural, in the template or the data pull. The set gives you both a yes or no and a direction.

Third, keep accuracy humble. This lesson leans on a checkbox for a good reason: it is cheap, it is repeatable, and a checkbox is honest. But a checkbox cannot see that a report is technically correct and also boring, or that a draft is accurate and also off the brand voice. Your review gate from Lesson 13 still owns the judgment, the taste, the reads that a ruler cannot grade. [^13] The quality bar measures the objective floor. The review gate measures the human ceiling. A workflow you can fully trust needs both.

## Where the bar is cheap, and where it is overkill

The golden set earns its place exactly where the workflows in this course earn their place: the recurring, stable job with output someone acts on. If the workflow runs every week and the answer matters, a ten-case set and a monthly re-run are a rounding error for the confidence they buy.

The bar is overkill in two directions. If a workflow is brand new and you are still changing it every day, do not build a set yet, the ruler would keep moving. Let it settle, then build the set from its first good weeks. And if the output is low-stakes and throwaway, a summary nobody reads three minutes after it is sent, do not grade it endlessly. The cost of caring about a ruler is not worth it when nothing is lost by a miss. Use the choosing test from Lesson 8: does it recur, does the frame stay stable, and does the output matter. Whenever those are and, the bar pays for itself. [^8]

## Try it

Give yourself an hour and build a bar for one workflow you already trust. You are not building anything new. You are making the confidence you already feel measurable.

Step one, gather the cases. Collect the last ten to twelve outputs the workflow produced, and pick the ones you have actually checked: the routine ones and at least two where you corrected at the review gate. The corrected ones are the most valuable, because they are the edge cases the workflow has not fully mastered.

Step two, write the ruler. For each case write one line on what correct means: the number it must report, the fact it must not skip, the tone it must hold. If you cannot write the line, you do not know the case well enough to grade it, so set it aside.

Step three, get your baseline. Grade the set on the current workflow and write the score down, with the date, in your changelog. This is your starting point, the one every future run is measured against.

Step four, commit to the re-run. Re-run the set the next time you change anything: a prompt edit, a model update, a template tweak, a new connected tool. That re-run after a change is the whole game.

Below is a try-it prompt that builds the ruler without letting the tool flatter it. The trap in a quality bar is a tool that grades everything as passing because it is afraid to criticize the workflow, so you want it to hunt for the cases that would actually fail.

> You are helping me build a quality bar for a workflow I run. I will paste several output examples I have already verified, each labeled CORRECT or WRONG, with a one-line note on what makes each correct or wrong. Based on these, give me two things. First, write a concise grading rule for each case, a checkable line that says what correct must include, so I can grade future runs against it objectively. Second, tell me which two or three cases would be the most sensitive early detectors of a regression, the ones most likely to fail first if a model update or prompt change degrades my workflow, and why. Be specific. Do not tell me my examples all look great. I want the cases that will catch a regression before a real report does.

Run that once and you will have what this lesson is teaching: not a score to defend, but a small, permanent, honest ruler that turns "I think it is still working" into "I measured it, and I know." A workflow you own is a workflow you can measure, and a workflow you can measure is one you can trust to keep working.

## What's next

This lesson gave you the way to judge a running workflow: a golden set of known-good cases, a baseline accuracy, and a regression check you re-run after every change. You can now know, with evidence, whether a workflow is still good and catch it the moment it stops being good.

The next lesson turns from quality to the other constraint that shapes every workflow you build. Lesson 19 is titled Cost and limits: token budgets, rate limits, and your tool's constraints. Where this lesson measured whether your output is good, Lesson 19 measures what running it costs: the token budgets that cap how much a model can process, the rate limits that cap how often it will respond, and the hard constraints your chosen tools place on what a workflow can do. It is the other side of sustaining a workflow you own, because a workflow that is accurate but unaffordable, or accurate but rate-limited out of its own schedule, is not sustainable.

*Note: a quality bar is a transferable pattern, not a feature of any one vendor. Some tools offer logging, versioning, and evaluation features built in, some leave you to keep your own golden set in a document or a spreadsheet. What transfers everywhere is the practice: a small set of hand-verified cases, a recorded baseline, and a re-run after every change. Build your bar with whatever you already have, and keep the fixed set small enough that you actually re-run it, because a ruler you never pick up measures nothing.*
