Lesson 1 made a claim: the payoff in becoming a power user lives in a small slice of your recurring work, the roughly 20 percent of tasks that carry most of your effort. That claim is only useful if you can find that slice in your own week. This lesson is the method for finding it. It is an audit, and like any audit it starts with evidence, not with your memory of how you think you work.

The trap is that most people answer the question "what do I do repeatedly?" from memory, and memory flatters. You remember the interesting tasks, the visible ones, the ones you would rather be doing. You forget the quiet hour of reformatting the same spreadsheet, the weekly status email you rebuild from scratch, the meeting notes you re-clean every time. An audit fixes that by measuring the week as it actually ran, not as you remember it.

## What an audit is and why it beats guessing

An audit is a structured look at where your time and effort actually go, recorded close to the moment it happens rather than reconstructed later. The word comes from accounting, where an audit verifies that the books match reality. The same idea applies to your work: you are checking your assumption about how you spend your week against the evidence of how you actually spent it.

The reason this matters is that your intuition about your own work is systematically wrong in one direction. You overestimate the time you spend on tasks you find engaging or urgent, and you underestimate the time you spend on tasks that are quiet, repetitive, and easy to forget. Atlassian's guidance on the Pareto principle makes the same point about teams: a large share of time goes to routine, repetitive work that is well suited to automation, and most people do not notice how much of their week that routine work eats. [^1] The audit is the correction for that blind spot.

An audit is not a personality test and it is not a productivity scorecard. It produces one thing: a list of your tasks ranked by how much of your week they actually consume, with the repetitive ones flagged. That list is the raw material for every workflow you will build in the rest of this course. Without it, you are guessing at the target. With it, you are aiming.

## The audit method: three passes

The audit has three passes, and each one answers a different question. You can run all three in a single sitting, or spread them across a week. The single-sitting version is faster and good enough to start; the week-long version is more accurate because it catches tasks that only show up on certain days. Start with the single sitting, and if the result surprises you, run the week-long version to confirm.

**Pass one: list the recurring tasks.** Write down every task you do more than once a month, in the order you do them. Do not judge yet. Include the small ones. The weekly status update, the monthly report, the recurring meeting summary, the follow-up email, the data cleanup, the invoice, the timesheet, the slide deck you rebuild for the same audience. If you are not sure whether something counts, include it. You can cut it later.

**Pass two: estimate the effort.** For each task, write two numbers: how often you do it, and roughly how long each run takes. Multiply them to get a weekly or monthly total. This is where the audit starts to bite, because the numbers rarely match your intuition. A task you do every day for ten minutes is 50 minutes a week, which is more than a task you do once a month for two hours. The daily small task wins, and most people miss that.

**Pass three: flag the repetitive ones.** A task is a candidate for automation when it has the same shape every time: the same inputs, the same steps, and the same format for the output. Mark each task that meets that test. These are the ones where a saved setup pays for itself, because every repetition after the first is cheaper. The tasks that change shape each time, the ones that need your judgment about what to include or how to frame it, are not candidates yet. They might become candidates later, but not first.

The output of the three passes is a ranked list. At the top sit the tasks that are both frequent and same-shaped. That is your repetitive 20 percent, the slice Lesson 1 pointed at, and the place to build your first workflow.

## A worked example: Marcus runs the audit

Marcus is a program coordinator at a nonprofit. He runs a lot of small programs, and he has a vague sense that he is busy all the time but not sure where the time goes. He decides to run the audit on a Tuesday afternoon, and he is honest with himself about the small tasks.

**Pass one: his list.** Marcus writes down what he actually does: the weekly program status email to his director, the monthly grant report, the recurring meeting agenda he drafts for his team's Monday sync, the data cleanup he does before each grant report, the follow-up emails to vendors, the slide deck he rebuilds for the quarterly board update, and the timesheet he fills in every Friday.

**Pass two: the numbers.** This is where Marcus gets surprised. The weekly status email takes him about 25 minutes each Monday, so that is roughly 100 minutes a month. The monthly grant report takes him two hours, but he does it once a month, so that is 120 minutes. The Monday meeting agenda takes him 15 minutes a week, about 60 a month. The data cleanup before each grant report takes him 45 minutes, and he does it every month, so 45. The vendor follow-ups are irregular, maybe 20 minutes a week, 80 a month. The board slide deck is a big one, four hours, but he only does it quarterly, so about 80 a month. The timesheet is 5 minutes a week, 20 a month.

When he adds it up, the weekly status email, the meeting agenda, and the data cleanup together eat more of his month than the big quarterly board deck. The small, frequent tasks win, exactly as the audit predicted, and exactly opposite to what he would have guessed from memory. He had been thinking of the board deck as the thing that took his time, because it was the most visible and the most stressful. The audit showed him the quiet weekly tasks were the real cost.

**Pass three: the flags.** Marcus marks the tasks that have the same shape every time. The weekly status email: same inputs (the week's numbers), same format (a short update to his director). The meeting agenda: same structure every Monday. The data cleanup: same columns, same fixes, every month. The timesheet: identical every week. Those four are his repetitive slice. The grant report and the board deck change shape each time, because what leadership wants to see shifts, so he leaves those for later. The vendor follow-ups are too irregular to automate cleanly.

Marcus walks away with a concrete answer. His repetitive 20 percent is not the dramatic work he would have named from memory. It is the quiet weekly machinery, the status email, the agenda, the data cleanup, the timesheet, and that is exactly the slice where a workflow pays off. He has not built anything yet. He has found the target, which is the whole job of this lesson.

## What makes a task a good first candidate

The audit gives you a list, but not every item on it is equally good as a first workflow. Three tests separate the strong candidates from the weak ones, and they are worth applying before you commit to building anything.

**It is frequent.** A task you do weekly beats a task you do quarterly, even if the quarterly one is bigger, because the setup cost of a workflow pays for itself faster the more often you run it. The Pareto idea is about the slice of work that repeats, and frequency is the first measure of that. [^1]

**It is same-shaped.** The task has the same inputs, the same steps, and the same output format every time. This is the test that separates automation from judgment. If the task changes shape each run, if you have to decide what to include or how to frame it, then a fixed workflow will fight you. Save those for later, when you understand the pattern well enough to build the judgment in.

**It is low-risk to get wrong.** The output of the task, if it is wrong, does not cause serious harm. A draft status update that you check before sending is low-risk. A financial figure that goes straight to a client without review is not. The low-risk tasks are where you build first, because you can trust the workflow to draft and you stay in the loop to decide, which is the human-in-the-loop pattern Lesson 1 introduced. [^2]

A task that is frequent, same-shaped, and low-risk is the ideal first candidate. Most people have at least one of these hiding in their audit, usually the one they have been doing by hand for years without noticing.

## Try it

Run the audit on your own week. It takes about twenty minutes, and it produces the list you will use in Lesson 3.

First, take a blank note and run the three passes:

1. List every task you do more than once a month, in the order you do them. Include the small ones. Do not judge yet.
2. For each task, write how often you do it and roughly how long each run takes. Multiply to get a weekly or monthly total. Rank the tasks by that total.
3. Flag the tasks that have the same shape every time: the same inputs, the same steps, and the same output format. These are your repetitive slice.

Second, get a second opinion with a tool. Paste this into any chat tool you already use, with your list from the note:

> Here are the tasks I do repeatedly at work, roughly in the order I do them: [your list]. For each one, tell me whether it has the same shape every time, meaning the same inputs, the same steps, and the same output format, or whether it changes each run. Then rank the same-shaped ones by how much of my week they likely consume, given how often I do them and how long each takes. Lead with the single task that is both most frequent and most same-shaped, and tell me why it is the best first candidate for a fixed workflow. Do not write the workflow yet. Just name the task and confirm it is the right target.

Read the answer the way the AI at Work course taught you. The tool is a helpful colleague with good instincts and no idea how your week actually runs. Its ranking is a starting point for your own judgment, not a verdict. [^3] What you are walking away with is a named task, the repetitive one, that Lesson 3 will ask you to decompose into a repeatable flow.

## What's next

You have now found the repetitive slice of your work and named the single best first candidate. The next step is turning that named task into something you can actually build on. Lesson 3 is about decomposing a task into a repeatable flow: input, tool, output, review. It takes the task you just identified and breaks it into the four parts that every workflow needs, so you can see exactly where the setup goes and where your judgment stays. [^1]

That decomposition is the bridge between finding the target and building the workflow. You cannot design a repeatable flow for a task you have not named, and you cannot build a flow you have not decomposed. Come to Lesson 3 with the task you just flagged, the frequent, same-shaped, low-risk one, and you will turn it into a flow you can actually run.

For now, take the twenty minutes. Run the three passes, rank your tasks, and flag the same-shaped ones. That single act, measuring your week instead of remembering it, is what separates a power user from someone who just uses a tool well. You are no longer guessing where your effort goes. You are looking at the evidence, and that is the foundation everything else in this course builds on. [^1] [^2]
