Lesson 15 assembled the pieces into a machine. It gave you the vocabulary, pipeline, step, handoff, and it showed you how to wire components you already built into one running flow. What a pattern needs after that is proof it holds up under a real job. That is what this lesson is. It is the first full worked example of the course, and it is deliberately ordinary. No exotic use case, no clever trick. Just a weekly operations report, the kind a person in almost any department runs, taken from its first trigger to its finished output with every join explained.

A worked example is a complete, concrete walkthrough of a task, done in full so you can see the reasoning behind each move rather than just the move itself. The word comes from teaching, where a worked example shows the working, not only the answer. That is the whole point of this lesson and the next one. You have the pattern. These two lessons show you the pattern in motion on real, recurring jobs, so you can recognize the same moves in your own work and copy them.

## Define the terms

Three terms carry this lesson, and two of them you already know from the course.

A worked example, as above, is a full walkthrough of one task with the reasoning shown. It is the difference between being told that a pipeline has a trigger, processing steps, and an output, and watching a specific person's pipeline run from start to finish.

An ops workflow is a workflow that keeps an operation running. Operations, in the plain sense, are the day-to-day activities that keep a business or a team functioning: orders fulfilled, deliveries made, tickets closed, inventory counted, shifts staffed. An ops workflow is a repeatable flow that handles one of those recurring operational jobs, and it is usually triggered on a schedule because the job itself recurs on a schedule.

A reporting workflow is a workflow whose output is a report. It gathers data, shapes it into something a person can read and act on, and delivers it to the people who need it. Operational reporting, as Jaspersoft, a business intelligence company, defines it, is the process of regularly producing detailed reports of an organization's day-to-day operations and activities, reports that give a tactical view of the present and are instantly actionable. [^1] The key words are regularly and actionable. A reporting workflow exists to put the right numbers in front of the right people on a rhythm, so they can act while the information is still current.

Two more terms belong to reporting specifically, and they will matter in the example.

A metric is a number you measure to judge how something is going. On-time rate, error rate, tickets closed per week, budget variance. A metric is only useful if everyone agrees on how it is counted, which is where the next term comes in.

A single source of truth is the one agreed place a number comes from, so that every report and every person bases decisions on the same data. Salesforce, the software company, defines it as a data architecture concept designed to ensure that every person in an organization bases business decisions on the same, unified data set, with every data element mastered in one place. [^2] The reason it matters to a reporting workflow is simple: if two reports count the same metric differently, the reports disagree, and the disagreement is worse than either wrong number, because nobody knows which to trust.

## The job: Nadia's weekly operations report

Meet Nadia. She runs operations for a small regional delivery company, about forty drivers moving packages across three counties. Every Monday morning she produces a weekly operations report for the leadership team. It answers four questions: how many deliveries did we complete, what percentage arrived on time, which deliveries went wrong and why, and what should we watch this week.

Before she built a workflow, this report cost her most of Monday. She opened the delivery tracker, exported the week's rows, opened a second sheet for the incident log, copied the problem deliveries, computed the on-time rate by hand, wrote a short narrative, and pasted the whole thing into an email. It was slow, it was error prone, and it was exactly the kind of recurring, stable job this course has been pointing at since the choosing lesson. [^8]

So she built a workflow. Here is the whole thing, end to end, with the reasoning at each join. Notice how every step is a piece she already made in earlier lessons.

## The workflow, end to end

Step one, the trigger. Lesson 12: Nadia sets the flow to start every Monday at 8am, which pulls the previous week's completed deliveries from the tracker and the week's incidents from the incident log. [^12] The trigger is a schedule because the job recurs on a schedule. Nothing about this step is clever. It just removes the need for Nadia to remember to start.

Step two, the data pull. This is the input step, and it is where the single source of truth earns its keep. The tracker is the one agreed place delivery data lives, and the incident log is the one agreed place problems live. Nadia's workflow pulls from both, and she names the handoff as she builds it: the tracker delivers rows with three fields she cares about, delivery ID, scheduled date, and on-time flag, and the incident log delivers rows with two, delivery ID and a one-line reason. [^6] She writes the downstream steps to expect exactly those fields. This is the moment the whole report becomes trustworthy or not, because everything after this inherits whatever quality this pull has.

Step three, the compute. This is a processing step. The workflow takes the pulled rows and calculates the metrics: total deliveries, on-time count, on-time rate, and the count of incidents. It also flags the exceptions, the deliveries that were late or went wrong, because those are what leadership actually needs to act on. The compute step is built with the structured, maintainable prompt habits from Lesson 14, so a later edit to one metric does not silently break the others. [^14]

Step four, the draft. Another processing step. The workflow takes the computed numbers and the flagged exceptions and writes the report: a short summary paragraph, the key metrics, and a list of the exceptions with their reasons. This is where the tool does the writing Nadia used to do by hand. The draft is built as a parameterized template in the style of Lesson 7, with the metrics and exceptions in clearly marked slots, so the shape of the report stays consistent week to week. [^7]

Step five, the review gate. Lesson 13: the draft stops here for Nadia to read and approve before it goes to leadership. [^13] This gate earns its place because of the four triggers from that lesson. A report to leadership is externally visible, a wrong number in it is expensive to unwind, and the tool cannot see the context Nadia holds, the driver who is on leave, the client who is already upset. So a human belongs in this loop. The gate passes only the approved draft, and only the approved draft, to the next step.

Step six, the output. The approved report is posted to the shared team folder and a copy goes to the leadership email list. That is the finished output, the end of the flow.

That is the whole workflow. Six steps, five handoffs, one recurring job, done. What makes it a workflow and not six separate chores is that Nadia named each handoff as she built it, so when something goes wrong she can see which boundary lied.

## The reasoning at each join

The value of a worked example is in the joins, so spend a moment on the reasoning Nadia made at each one, because these are the decisions that separate a working report from a brittle one.

At the data pull, she chose to pull from the tracker and the incident log rather than from a spreadsheet she maintains herself. That is a deliberate single-source-of-truth decision. If she kept her own copy of the numbers, she would have two versions of the truth, and the report would eventually disagree with the tracker, and she would spend Monday reconciling instead of reporting. [^2] The pull is the foundation, and she keeps it boring on purpose.

At the compute step, she decided what to count and how. On-time rate is deliveries marked on time divided by total deliveries. She wrote that definition down once, in the prompt, so it does not drift week to week. This is the metric-definition discipline from the single source of truth idea: the number means the same thing every time it appears. [^2]

At the draft step, she made a judgment call about what the tool should and should not write. The tool writes the summary and the exception list, but it does not invent recommendations, because recommendations carry judgment the tool does not have. That is the review-gate instinct applied at the step level: keep the tool doing the mechanical writing, and keep the judgment where a human can apply it. [^13]

At the review gate, she decided the report earns a pause and the data pull does not. The pull is routine and reversible, it earns rhythm, not a pause. The report is externally visible and expensive to get wrong, it earns a gate. [^13] This is the same test she has used all course, and it is what keeps the workflow fast where it can be and careful where it must be.

## Where the reliability pieces catch a real failure

A worked example is honest about failure, so here is the one that happened in Nadia's third week, and it shows why the pieces from the reliability lessons are not decoration.

One Monday the on-time rate came back at 99.4 percent, which was impossible. The company had a bad week, several drivers out sick, a route that flooded, and Nadia knew the real number was nowhere near that. Her first instinct was to poke at the compute step, to rewrite the prompt and hope. She caught herself and did the version thing instead.

She keeps a changelog for this workflow, the habit from Lesson 9. [^9] It showed one change from the week before: the tracker had added a new field, and the person who maintains it had started marking some deliveries with a status code instead of the on-time flag. The pull step, which expected the on-time flag, was reading the new status field as blank, and the compute step was counting blank as on time. The handoff had lied, exactly where the interface lesson said it would. [^15]

The fix was one line in the changelog: the pull step now maps the new status code to the on-time flag before the compute step reads it. Nadia re-ran the workflow, the number came back at 91.7 percent, which matched what she knew, and she approved the corrected report. Without the changelog she would have spent the morning regenerating and rewriting, treating a handoff problem as a prompt problem and likely making it worse. Instead she found the change in minutes and reversed it.

This is the garbage-in, garbage-out principle made concrete. GIGO, as the computing phrase goes, is the concept that flawed or poor-quality input produces output of similar quality, no matter how good the process is. [^3] Nadia's compute step was fine. The input had changed shape, and the whole report inherited the flaw. The reliability pieces, the changelog, the named handoffs, the review gate, are what let her catch it before leadership saw a number that was wrong.

## When this pattern applies, and when it does not

This worked example is a reporting workflow, but the shape transfers to any recurring operational job. Inventory counts, ticket summaries, shift coverage, budget variance, vendor performance. If the job recurs on a schedule, pulls from a source of truth, produces something a person reads and acts on, and has moments where a wrong output is expensive, this is the pattern.

The same honesty from the choosing lesson applies here. [^8] A report you run once and never again does not earn a workflow. A report where the source data changes shape every week does not earn a workflow yet, because the handoffs would break faster than you could maintain them. And a report where nobody acts on the output does not earn a workflow, because a report nobody reads is not worth automating. The test is the same one you have used all course: does it recur, does the frame stay stable, and does the output matter.

## Try it

Give yourself forty minutes and run your own recurring report through the same moves Nadia made. You do not need to build anything new. You are auditing what you already run.

Step one, name the report. Pick a report you produce on a rhythm, weekly, monthly, whatever it is, that someone actually reads and acts on.

Step two, name the source of truth. Where does the data for this report come from, and is there one agreed place, or are you maintaining your own copy? If you keep your own numbers, that is your first finding.

Step three, name the steps and handoffs. Write the flow in order, from trigger to output, and for each boundary write what the previous step produces and what the next step needs. This is the step that surfaces the handoffs that will break first.

Step four, find the gate. Which step in your report would be expensive to get wrong, and does a human check it before it ships? If the answer is none, that is your second finding.

Below is a try-it prompt that audits your report rather than flattering it. The trap in reporting workflows is that the numbers look right and nobody questions them, so you want the tool to hunt for the places where a wrong number could slip through.

> You are helping me audit a recurring report I produce. I will paste the report's purpose, where its data comes from, and the steps I run to produce it. Be critical. Check these things: is there a single source of truth, or am I maintaining numbers that could disagree with the real system, is any handoff unnamed, where the output of one step does not match what the next step expects, is there a step where a wrong number would reach a reader without a human checking it, and is any step doing work the job does not justify. For each gap, tell me the exact step that will fail first and why. Do not tell me my report is fine. I want to find the weak join before my next real run does.

Run that once and you will have what this lesson is really teaching: the confidence that a recurring report can run as a workflow you own, from trigger to finished output, with every join visible and every wrong number caught before it ships.
