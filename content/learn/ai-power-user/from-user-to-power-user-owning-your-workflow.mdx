# Lesson 1: From User to Power User: What Owning Your Workflow Means

If you are reading this, you already use AI at work. You draft emails with it, you summarize meetings, you ask it to start documents and untangle spreadsheets. You have finished the AI at Work primer or something close to it. That puts you ahead of most people in your building, and it is also exactly where the interesting part starts.

Because here is the gap this whole course sits on: using a tool well and owning your workflow are not the same thing. A user gets a good result from a tool. A power user gets a good result they can repeat, change, and trust, without re-deciding the whole thing from scratch each time. Lesson 1 draws that line, names what ownership actually requires, and gives you the first concrete step toward it. The rest of this course is those first steps, one at a time.

## What separates a user from a power user

Start with a working definition, because people throw "power user" around loosely. Lenovo's glossary puts it plainly: a power user is someone who goes beyond basic usage, digs into the advanced features and customization, and tailors the technology to their own needs. [^1] The part worth noticing is the last clause. A power user does not just use the tool faster. They configure it to fit their own work.

Hold that against how most people actually use AI. The most common pattern at work looks like this: a task shows up, you open a chat, you describe the task fresh, you get a result, you copy it out, you close the window. The next time the same kind of task shows up, you do the whole thing again, from a nearly blank conversation, describing it the same way you did last week. That is using a tool. It works. It is also leaking effort, because you are rebuilding the same instructions every single time.

The power user stops doing that. They take the task they do repeatedly and turn it into a setup they control: a saved set of instructions, a fixed order of steps, a check they run on the output. Each repetition after the first one is cheaper, because the setup does the re-deciding. Lenovo makes the same point when it talks about optimizing a workflow: tools like task automation and scripting speed up the repetitive work and give you back the time for the things that actually matter. [^1]

So the line is not about technical skill. It is about ownership. A user takes the tool as it comes and asks it for things. A power user takes a piece of their own recurring work, designs how it should run, and makes the tool carry the parts that do not need their judgment. That is the transformation this course is built around, and the rest of this lesson is about what "owning" means before you try to build anything.

## What owning your workflow means

"Own" is a strong word, and it is doing real work here. To own a workflow means three things, and they each matter on their own.

**You designed it.** The workflow came out of your head, based on how your job actually runs, not out of a vendor's demo. You chose which task to automate, which steps to include, what the inputs are, and what a good output looks like. When you design it, you understand it. When you understand it, you can decide when to trust it and when to change it.

**You can change it.** This is the test that separates owning from renting. If your workflow does something wrong, you can open it up and adjust it, because it is your design. If the tool changes and breaks part of it, you can repair that part instead of starting over. A setup you cannot edit is not something you own. It is something you use, and you are back to being a user, just with better branding.

**You answer for it.** That is accountability, and it is the piece people feel most uncomfortable about, so let me be direct. An AI tool drafts the output, but the work that leaves your hands is yours. Atlassian's guidance on human-in-the-loop systems makes the point bluntly: the agent does the routine execution, and a human controls the decisions that carry real consequences. [^2] Ownership means that when the output is good, you get the credit, and when it is wrong, you own that too, because you chose to send it. You stay in the loop at the point where the output could still be wrong. That is not a weakness in the workflow. It is the whole reason the workflow is safe to rely on.

Those three things, design, change, and answer, are the real definition. A workflow you designed, can edit, and stay accountable for is work you own. A tool that does things for you that you could not reproduce or change is work you have rented, and the difference shows up the first time something goes wrong.

## Where the payoff actually lives

Part of becoming a power user is learning where to point the effort, and there is a well-tested answer. The Pareto principle, the 80/20 rule, describes a pattern that shows up across business and everyday life: roughly 80 percent of your results come from about 20 percent of your causes. [^3] Atlassian's explanation of the rule applies it directly to work: a large share of most teams' time goes to work that is routine, repetitive, and well suited to automation, like answering the same questions, routing the same kinds of asks, and sending the same follow-ups. [^3]

That is the lever. The 80/20 idea frames the whole course. You do not need to own every task you touch, and you should not try. You need to find the relatively small slice of your work that you repeat over and over, and that is where building a workflow pays off. Automate the 80 percent of repeatable work so you can point your attention at the 20 percent that actually needs your judgment. [^3]

The reason this matters for Lesson 1 is that it sets your aim. Most people start automating by picking the wrong target. They grab the biggest task, or the most interesting one, or the one a colleague mentioned, and they build a workflow that turns out to be a lot of setup for a task they only do twice a year. The Pareto framing says to look the other direction: find the task you do on a near-fixed schedule, the one with the same shape every time, and build there first. That is where the setup cost pays for itself fastest and where a workflow becomes a habit instead of a project.

Lesson 2 turns this from an idea into a method. For now, hold the target: a slice of your recurring work, roughly a fifth of your tasks that produces a large share of your effort, is the first candidate for ownership.

## A worked example: Priya stops re-deciding her week

Priya is a member of her team's operations group at a mid-size company, and she is in the exact position this course assumes. She uses AI daily. She drafts her weekly status update with a chat tool, she summarizes her team's long email threads, and she asks the tool to clean up meeting notes. She likes the results. She is, by her own description, "quite good with AI."

Here is what Priya does not realize: she is spending her week re-making the same small decisions. Every Monday she opens a fresh chat and writes, roughly, "summarize this week's numbers for leadership, keep it under 150 words, flag anything urgent." Every meeting she drags her jumbled notes into a prompt and asks for a clean summary, phrasing it slightly differently each time. Every Friday she stares at a pile of raw notes and thinks about how to turn them into a status update. None of these tasks is hard. Added together, they cost her an hour a week of deciding and rewriting, forever, and they all fall inside the repetitive slice the Pareto rule points at.

Priya makes the power-user shift in three moves, and each move lines up with the three parts of ownership.

**Move one: she designs it.** Priya writes down the weekly status task as a fixed flow: pull the week's numbers, feed them a saved instruction that says what the update must include and how long it must be, then check the result against her own notes before sending. The order, the inputs, and the check are her choices, made once. That is the design part of ownership. She did it with one saved instruction instead of rewriting the ask every Monday.

**Move two: she can change it.** Two weeks in, the update is missing a metric leadership now cares about. Because Priya designed the flow, she opens the saved instruction, adds the line, and the next run includes it. A user would have rewritten the prompt by hand that week and forgotten by the next. Priya changed the thing she owns, and the change stuck. That is the difference in practice.

**Move three: she answers for it.** The check is where Priya stays in the loop. She does not send the draft on the tool's say-so. She reads it against the numbers she pulled and her own notes, fixes anything the tool got wrong, and only then hits send. She recently caught the tool turning a "roughly forty percent" from her notes into a confident "the rollout is over halfway complete," which would have embarrassed her in front of leadership. The tool drafted; she decided. [^2]

The result is not dramatic in the demo sense. Priya did not build a robot. She saved herself the hour of re-deciding her week, she made the output more consistent, and she kept accountability where it belonged, on the piece of work that carries her name. That is the whole point of this course in miniature: not more automation, but a small slice of your own work, designed by you, changeable by you, and answered for by you.

## Try it

The goal of this lesson is not to build anything yet. It is to start seeing your own work the way the rest of the course will ask you to. This exercise takes fifteen minutes and produces something you will use in Lesson 2.

First, take a blank note and answer these three questions about your own week:

1. What are the tasks you run on a near-fixed schedule: the weekly update, the monthly report, the recurring meeting summary, the same follow-up email?
2. For each one, do you re-describe it from scratch every time, or do you have a setup that does the re-describing for you?
3. Of those recurring tasks, which one has the same shape every single time, the same inputs and the same format for the output?

The third question is the one you are really looking for. That task is your first candidate for ownership, and the Pareto idea says it is where the payoff is concentrated. [^3]

Second, get a second opinion with a tool. Paste this into any chat tool you already use, with your list from the note:

> Here are the tasks I do repeatedly at work, roughly in the order I do them: [your list]. Lead with which ones are the most repetitive, meaning they have the same inputs and the same output shape every time. For the single most repetitive one, tell me what you would need to know to turn it into a fixed workflow: what should the input be, what steps would it run in order, and what should I check before the output leaves my hands. Do not write the workflow yet. Just name the task and what a design would need.

Read the answer the way the AI at Work course taught you. The tool is a helpful colleague with good instincts and no idea how your week actually runs. Its list is a starting point for your own judgment, not a verdict. [^4] What you are walking away with is a named task, the repetitive one, that Lesson 2 will ask you to audit properly.

## What's next

You have just identified the shape of your own recurring work and named the first task worth owning. The obvious next question is which tasks actually qualify, and that is exactly what the next lesson covers. Lesson 2 is an audit: auditing how you actually work, and finding the repetitive 20 percent. It takes you through the method for measuring where your week actually goes, separating the tasks that are worth automating from the ones that are not, and producing your own honest list of the repetitive slice that the Pareto rule says carries most of the payoff. [^3]

That audit is not busywork and it is not a personality test. It is the foundation everything else in this course builds on, because you cannot design a workflow for a task you have not found or measured. Come to Lesson 2 with the note you just made, the one naming your most repetitive task, and you will turn it into a proper audit you can actually run.

For now, take the fifteen minutes. Find the task that repeats, the one with the same shape every time, and write it down. That single act, seeing your own work through the ownership lens instead of the usage lens, is the moment this course is organized around. You are no longer asking what the tool can do for you. You are asking what you should build and own, and that is the power-user question. [^1] [^3]
