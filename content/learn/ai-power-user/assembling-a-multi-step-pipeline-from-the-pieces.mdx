The previous fourteen lessons handed you a box of parts. A templated prompt that returns a consistent shape. A review gate that puts a human where a miss costs too much. A changelog so you can undo what broke. A schedule so the work runs without you remembering to start it. Each part is useful alone, but alone is not the point. The point of this course is that you own a workflow, a single flow that takes a job nobody wants and runs it to the end. This is the lesson where the parts stop being a box and start being a machine.

The idea has a name in the tools world: a multi-step pipeline, or a multi-step workflow. Zapier, a widely used automation platform, describes it plainly: you can take a single trigger, a form submission, a new row, a scheduled check, and run a chain of actions from it, and Zapier lets you add as many steps as you need and orders them top to bottom. [^1] You do not need Zapier to use the idea. The idea is that a job gets decomposed into ordered steps, and each step hands its result to the next.

That word, handoff, is the entire lesson.

## Define the terms

A pipeline is a set of ordered steps that together turn an input into a finished output. Think of water moving through connected pipes: it enters at one end, travels through the sections in sequence, and comes out changed at the other. Each section does one job, and the output of one section becomes the input of the next.

A step is a single unit of work in that chain. A prompt that turns raw data into a summary is a step. A check that a human reviews and approves is a step. A step that copies the final output into the place your team reads it is a step. The step has a job, it has an input it expects, and it has an output it produces.

A handoff is the boundary between two steps, the exact place where the output of one becomes the input of the next. This is the detail that separates a working pipeline from a brittle one, and it is the reason most pipelines quietly fail. Every step was designed by you to consume a specific shape of input. If the previous step hands over something a slightly different shape, the mismatch appears downstream, and the whole flow looks broken when really one boundary lied.

Anthropic, the company behind Claude, calls this pattern prompt chaining, and describes it in terms you can borrow without any of its tooling: a sequence of steps where each step processes the output of the previous one. [^2] The label matters less than the structure. The structure is, one step's output is the next step's input, and the chain is only as strong as the handoffs that connect it.

## The pieces you already own

Everything this lesson needs you have built. Here is the inventory, so you can see the box you are working with.

The input step. Lesson 6 gave you a structured output, a request to your tool to return data in a format a downstream step can consume, and Lesson 7 gave you a parameterized template, a reusable prompt with clearly marked variable slots. [^6] [^7] Together they are the front of the pipeline: raw material goes in the slots, and a consistent shape comes out.

The processing steps. Between input and output sit the transformations that do the real work. Summarize, classify, reformat, combine, translate. Each is its own step, built with the structured, maintainable, commented prompt habits from Lesson 14, so a later edit does not break the chain. [^14]

The review gate. Lesson 13 taught you where a human belongs: pause the flow at the moments where a miss costs too much, and let the harmless steps run untouched. [^13] The gate is a step like any other, except its output is a decision, approve or reject, instead of a transformed document.

The reliability wrapper. Lesson 10 gave you dry runs and edge cases to test a step before it ships. Lesson 11 gave you the failure vocabulary, silent errors, hallucinations, tools that break mid flow, and how to catch them. Lesson 9 gave you the changelog so you can roll back when one change to a step goes wrong. [^10] [^11] [^9] These are not separate concerns. They are the guardrails a pipeline needs once it runs on a schedule, which is where Lesson 12 comes in, the cadence or trigger that starts the whole flow at the right time. [^12]

One by one these are habits. Assembled, they are a system.

## The interface is where it breaks

Before the worked example, spend a sentence on the most common failure, because it will save you an afternoon. When a multi-step pipeline goes wrong, the fault is usually not inside a step. It is at the handoff.

Each step you built quietly made a contract with the next. The template slot promised to deliver a number. The review gate promised to pass along a cleaned, honest draft. The formatting step promised output a teammate could read. If any step changes what it produces, even in a way that looks fine on its own, the downstream step that was consuming the old shape is now reading the wrong shape, and it misbehaves in a way that looks like that step's fault.

The discipline this teaches is to name each handoff when you build it. What exactly does this step produce? What does the next step need? Write both down, even in one line, and you have made the interface explicit. The pipeline then fails loudly at the named boundary instead of mysteriously, and with your changelog from Lesson 9 you can trace which change broke which handoff. [^9]

## The worked example: Maya wires four components into one flow

Maya runs the weekly newsletter for a small consulting agency. Doing it by hand cost her an afternoon every Friday: pull the week's completed project lists from the team's project tracker, write a short note on each finished milestone, shape it into a clean draft, get a colleague to approve it, then post it. She has spent the course building pieces, and now she assembles them.

Here is the pipeline she builds, and notice how every step is a piece she already made.

Step one, the trigger. Lesson 12: she sets the flow to start every Friday at noon, which pulls the week's finished items from the tracker. [^12]

Step two, the draft. Lesson 7: her template prompt takes the pull as its input and returns a draft with each item named, what it was, and a one-line note of what it accomplished. Lesson 14: the prompt is structured, with the weekly pull in a clearly marked variable slot and each rule stated once. [^7] [^14]

Step three, the gate. Lesson 13: the draft stops here for Maya's colleague to read and approve, because a newsletter sent wrong is a visible, costly miss, and a human belongs in that loop. [^13]

Step four, the output. The approved draft is posted to the newsletter tool and a copy lands in the shared team folder. That is the finished output, the end of the flow.

What makes this a pipeline and not four separate chores is the handoffs. Maya names each one as she builds: the tracker delivers rows with three fields and she writes the template to expect exactly those three; the template delivers a draft with headings she tells the reader to expect; the gate passes the approved draft, and only the approved draft, to the poster. Each boundary is explicit, which means when something goes wrong she can see which handoff lied.

The first real run catches a handoff bug. The tracker exports the client name in one column, but last week someone started tagging the item with a bracket suffix, and Maya's template, which expects a plain name, produces a draft that reads slightly off. The chain did not break, it degraded, which is the nastier failure. Because her handoffs are named, she spots it at the draft step in seconds, and because she keeps a changelog she logs the fix as one line: template now strips the tag suffix before it fills the name slot. [^9]

That is the payoff. Maya no longer spends Friday assembling four separate chores. She runs one flow, checks the gate, and reviews the output. The pieces she spent the course building each still do exactly what they did, but arranged in sequence they now do a job no single piece could.

## When a pipeline is not worth it

Assembly is power, but it is also cost, and this course has been honest about that tradeoff from the choosing lesson onward. [^8] A two-step flow where the second step is a manual copy is not a pipeline, it is a chore wearing a costume, and every extra step is a new handoff that can break. Add steps because each one removes manual work or adds a necessary control, never because a chain of steps looks more impressive.

The test is the same one you have used all course: does the flow recur, and does the frame stay stable? A job you run weekly with a steady shape earns assembly. A one-off you will never repeat does not, and forcing it into a pipeline is ceremonial weight with no payload. Build the pipeline when the handoffs buy you something, and leave the one-offs alone.

## Try it

Give yourself forty minutes and assemble the pieces you have been building all course into one end to end flow. Do not build new steps. Use the ones you already made: the template from Lesson 7, the structured prompt from Lesson 14, the review gate from Lesson 13, the schedule from Lesson 12. [^7] [^14] [^13] [^12]

Step one, name the job. Pick the workflow you have refined through the course, the one that is worth running on a schedule.

Step two, write the steps in order. List what happens from the moment the job starts to the moment the output is finished. Three to five steps is plenty. Name each step's job in one line.

Step three, name the handoffs. For each boundary, write what the previous step produces and what the next step needs. This is the step that separates a machine from a pile of parts, and it is the one most people skip.

Step four, run it once end to end. Watch the output come out the back. If a handoff is wrong, you will see it here, at the boundary you named, and you can fix the producing step or the consuming step with a logged change.

Below is a try-it prompt that audits your assembly rather than flattering it. The trap in assembling is that you chain components and assume the handoffs work. You want the tool to hunt for unstated interfaces.

> You are helping me assemble a multi-step pipeline from pieces I already built. I will paste each step: its name, what input it expects, what output it produces. Be critical. Check these things: is any handoff unnamed, where the output of one step does not match the input the next step says it expects, are there more steps than the job justifies, and does any step produce output nothing downstream consumes. For each gap, tell me the exact handoff that will break first and why. Do not tell me the pipeline is well built. I want to find the broken boundary before my next real run does.

Run that once and you will have what this lesson is really teaching: the confidence that the parts you built now run as one machine you own, from trigger to finished output, with every join visible.
