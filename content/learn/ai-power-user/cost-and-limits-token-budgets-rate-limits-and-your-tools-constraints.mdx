Lesson 18 gave you the truth about whether a workflow is still good: a small set of known-good cases, a baseline, and a re-run after every change. That is the quality side of sustaining a workflow. This lesson is the other side. A workflow can be excellent and still fail, not because it is wrong, but because it is too expensive to run, or because the tool simply will not answer fast enough, or because the data does not fit in the first place. Those are not quality problems. They are the cost and limits that sit underneath everything you have built in this course.

You need this lesson now because every workflow you own, no matter how well built, runs inside a set of hard ceilings you did not choose. The model has a fixed amount of attention. The provider has a policy about how much that model will do for you per minute. The plan you pay for has a monthly number. None of these are optional, and none of them care how good your workflow is. Learn them once and you stop being surprised when a workflow that worked last week suddenly will not run.

## Define the terms

Four terms carry this lesson, and they build on each other.

A token is the unit of text that an AI model actually works with. Humans read words and letters. Models read tokens, and the mapping is not one to one. IBM, in its explainer on context windows, gives the shape of this precisely: a token can stand in for a single character, a part of a word, a suffix or prefix, an entire word, or even a short multiword phrase. [^1] The same bit of text maps to different tokens in different places. The letter "a" alone is its own token. The "a" that prefixes "moral" to make "amoral" is part of a different token. The "a" inside the word "cat" is not worth a token at all. [^1] For your purposes you do not need to count them by hand. You need to know that every model counts its work in tokens, because that is what it bills you for and what it measures its limits in.

A context window is the amount of text, measured in tokens, that a model can consider at any one time. IBM calls it the model's working memory, the thing that decides how long a conversation it can carry and how much of a document it can process at once. [^1] When your data exceeds that window, something has to give: the model must truncate the input, or summarize it, before it can proceed. [^1] This is the first ceiling. It is physical, it is real, and no amount of good prompting removes it. You can only work within it or restructure your input to fit.

A token budget is the amount of a model's capacity, weighed against its price, that a workflow is allowed to use. The word budget leans on the accounting meaning on purpose: a budget is a limit you set in advance, not a number you discover after the fact. For a single run the budget is mostly determined by the context window, what fits in one response. Across a month it is determined by money, what you are willing to spend. OpenAI's own guidance treats these as two separate controls: usage tiers set how much an organization can use, and spend limits set what an organization will pay for. [^2] A token budget is where those two meet for your workflow specifically.

A rate limit is the cap a provider puts on how fast you can call its service. Rate limits are restrictions on the number of times a client can access a service within a specified period. [^2] They are usually expressed as requests per minute, tokens per minute, requests per day, or tokens per day, and they are measured across whichever limit you hit first. [^2] You might have room in your token allowance and still be blocked, because you exhausted your requests per minute. [^2]

These four terms are the vocabulary, now the reasons.

## Why these ceilings exist

The constraining part is not that the limits are there. It is that they seem unfair, an annoyance the tool maker added to frustrate you. They are not. Each ceiling exists for a reason you can act on, and knowing the reason tells you how to work around it.

IBM explains the cost side of the context window directly. Generally, a larger context window translates into more accuracy and fewer hallucinations, but it also demands more computational power, and therefore more cost. [^1] This is the bargain you are living inside. Every token you let a model consider, whether it is your homework or the model's answer, costs compute, and compute costs money. The bigger the window, the more it costs to run anything in it. That is why there is no free lunch in a big context window, and why the instinct to paste in everything is a bad instinct.

OpenAI explains the rate limit side. Rate limits protect against abuse, they help ensure everybody gets fair access, and they let the provider manage the load on its infrastructure. [^2] Translate that into your situation. The provider is running a shared service. If any one user could flood it, everyone would slow down, so nobody gets to flood it. You are not being singled out. You are sharing a road with a speed limit.

The spend limit is the one you choose yourself. A spend limit is a cap you configure on your own organization or project, and it can either send an alert or stop traffic entirely when your tracked spend reaches the amount you set. [^3] This is the ceiling that is not imposed on you. It is the one you impose on yourself, and it is the cheapest insurance available against the third failure in this lesson: the run that quietly runs up your bill.

## The three ways a workflow stops because of a limit

Here is the practical frame. When a workflow stops working and nothing is wrong with the output, the cause is almost always one of three things, and each one shows up differently.

First, it stops fitting. The context window is full. You added a year of data, you pasted a long document, you let the conversation grow for weeks, and now the input exceeds what the model can consider. The tool truncates or summarizes, which does not always announce itself. [^1] A chunk of your data simply did not make it into the answer, and the answer is quietly wrong in the way you learned to catch in Lesson 18. [^18]

Second, it stops being allowed. You hit a rate limit. You tried to check all of your golden-set cases at once, or a scheduled run fired at the same moment as a manual one, and the tool refused to answer for a while. [^2] This one usually does tell you, because the tool returns an error. The trick is knowing it is a rate limit and not a bug, so you wait or spread the calls out instead of blaming your workflow.

Third, it stops being affordable. You did not set a spend cap, or you set it high, and the workflow ran more than you expected, or bigger than you expected, and the bill climbed faster than you noticed. [^3] This is the silent one in cost form. Nothing errors, nothing stops, the model answers every request, and at the end of the month you are surprised by what it added up to.

All three failures have the same shape: the workflow was technically fine and still could not run. And all three have the same first response: do not blame the tool. Audit what you are feeding it, how often, and what it costs, and you will find the limit you are brushing against.

## The worked example: Priya's report stops fitting, then gets throttled, then costs too much

Priya is back. She is the finance operations person from Lesson 16, and since Lesson 18 she judges her weekly revenue report against an eleven-case golden set. [^16] [^18] The report has been clean for a month. Then, over three weeks, she hits all three ceilings, and each one teaches her something separate.

The first week the report stops fitting. She decides the Monday leadership summary should also compare each region against the same week last year, a genuinely useful addition. She adds the previous year's data to the workflow, one more appended block, and runs it. The output comes back, and it is shorter than she expects, and a region is missing entirely. Nothing errors. The model simply ran out of room: the new data took the input past the model's working memory, so it silently set aside the oldest part. [^1] She learns the fix by trimming, not by asking the model to try harder. The workflow does not need every row of last year's data; it needs the summary figures per region. She restructures the input to a compact table of last year's totals, it fits, and the region comes back. The lesson: feed the model the summary, not the firehose, and you stay inside the window.

The second week she hits a rate limit. On Monday she runs the report and, on a whim, decides to re-run her full eleven-case golden set right then to check for the regression from Lesson 18. [^18] Six calls in, the tool starts refusing. She checks the error and sees she has blown through her requests per minute, not her token budget, because she fired off the whole set at once. [^2] The fix is mechanical: her golden-set re-run does not need to happen in one burst, so she spaces the cases out across the morning, or runs fewer at a time, and the limit stops being a wall. The lesson: a rate limit is about how fast you ask, not whether you are allowed to ask at all, and you can usually pace yourself past it.

The third week it costs too much. The report is scheduled, the golden set is back under control, and everything runs. But that month she also set the report drafting on a new, larger model, and doubled the context, because bigger looked better after Lesson 18's accuracy work. [^18] Nothing errors. The model answers happily, every run, all month. Then the bill shows up and it is four times her usual. She had never set a spend alert, so nothing told her she was spending more. [^3] She sets a spend alert at the amount she would actually want to know about, and a hard cap at the number she cannot afford to blow past, so the supplier would stop her instead of letting her find out in the mail. [^3] The lesson: a denominator she had never tracked, cost, was silently rising, and a cap she set once would have caught it in week one instead of week four.

Three weeks, three ceilings, three habits. She feeds the summary, not the firehose. She paces her high-volume checks. She caps her spend. Her workflow still runs, and now it runs inside limits she can see instead of surprise her.

## Audit your own workflows for the same three

You can run the same audit on any workflow you already own, and you probably do not need to change anything to start. The point is to know where each ceiling is before it bites.

One, does it fit? Look at the largest single run your workflow does, the biggest input you feed it, and ask whether that run could ever exceed the model's working memory. [^1] If the answer is it could, decide in advance what gets trimmed. Notices, summaries, oldest data first. Decide it now, so a growth spurt does not make the decision for you silently.

Two, do you ever burst? Look for the places where your workflow makes a dozen or more calls close together: a golden-set re-run, a batch of items, a review that checks everything at once. If those bursts could exceed requests per minute, spread them out, or accept that a burst means a short pause. [^2] A rate limit you expect is a schedule, not a failure.

Three, what does it cost? Find the usage page for whatever tool you use, and answer two questions: what did this workflow spend last month, and is there a cap I could set that would warn me or stop me before a surprise? [^3] If you do not know the number, the first time you will hear about it is the bill. A spend alert turns that around.

None of this requires technical skill. It is a bookkeeping habit, the same way your golden set from Lesson 18 was a bookkeeping habit. [^18] You are counting what fits, how often, and what it costs, and a workflow whose three numbers you know is a workflow you can run without dread.

Below is a try-it prompt that turns the audit into words you give the tool, so it can help you pin down the specific ceilings you are brushing against.

> You are helping me audit the cost and limits of a workflow. I will describe the workflow, what it does, how often it runs, and roughly how much text it sends and receives each run. Tell me three things. First, where is the biggest risk that the input exceeds the model's context window, and what would I trim first if it did. Second, where is the biggest risk I would hit a rate limit, whether from one burst or from overlapping schedules, and how could I pace it. Third, how should I estimate the monthly cost of this workflow, and what would be the most useful spend alert threshold for it. Do not reassure me. Assume every run is honest, and tell me the real numbers I should watch.

Run that on one workflow you actually use and you will have a map of its three ceilings. You will probably find that at least one of them is closer than you thought, and that is the point, because a limit you can see is a limit you can work around, and a limit that surprises you is a bill or a broken run you did not plan for.
