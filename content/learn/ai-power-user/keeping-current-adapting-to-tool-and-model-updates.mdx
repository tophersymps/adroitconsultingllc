Here is the quietest way a workflow dies. It does not crash. It does not throw an error. It keeps running, week after week, and the output slowly stops being what you need. The numbers are still there, the format still looks right, and a reader who does not know the work would not notice. But you notice, because you know what good output used to look like. You did not change anything. The tool changed under you.

This is the reality every automation lives in, and it is the reason the course has spent so long on versioning, testing, and documentation. Those habits are not about the day you build a workflow. They are about the years it has to survive. This lesson is about the specific skill that keeps a workflow alive across that time: noticing when the ground under it moves, checking whether the move broke anything, and deciding, honestly, whether to ride the change out or adapt to it.

## Define the terms

Four terms carry this lesson, and the first two are easy to confuse.

A tool update is a change to the software you run your workflow in. The app ships a new version, the interface moves, a setting changes meaning, a feature you relied on gets renamed or removed. You can usually see a tool update coming, because the app tells you, and you can usually read what changed in its release notes.

A model update is a change to the AI model underneath the tool. This is the sneaky one. When you use a chat tool or an automation platform, you are almost always talking to a model the vendor can swap without telling you. The interface looks identical. The prompt is identical. The model behind it is not. Model updates are the reason a workflow can change behavior with no visible change to anything you control.

Behavior drift is the term for what happens when the same tool, same prompt, same input, produces different output than it used to, because the model behind it changed. It is not a bug in your workflow. It is the model's behavior shifting under a workflow that assumed it would stay still. The Stanford and Berkeley researchers who documented this in 2023 put it plainly: the behavior of the same LLM service can change substantially in a relatively short amount of time, and that is why continuous monitoring is needed. [^1] Their study compared the March and June 2023 versions of the same models and found large swings on identical tasks, including a case where one model's accuracy on a math task fell from 84 percent to 51 percent in three months. [^1] The model did not get worse at everything. It got worse at some things and better at others. The point is that it moved, and nobody told the users it moved.

A deprecation is a vendor's formal announcement that a model or feature is being retired. It is the opposite of a silent drift. The vendor names the thing, gives a date, and tells you what to move to. Deprecations are the predictable part of keeping current, because vendors publish them in advance. OpenAI, for example, says it provides at least six months of notice before retiring generally available models, three months for specialized variants, and as little as two weeks for preview models. [^2] Anthropic publishes a model status page that marks each model active, legacy, deprecated, or retired, and gives at least sixty days of notice before a publicly released model retires. [^3] Google tracks deprecation and shutdown dates for its Gemini models on a dedicated page. [^4] The pattern across vendors is the same: they tell you, they give you time, and they expect you to move before the date.

A smoke test is a quick check that a workflow still produces its expected output after something changes. The name comes from hardware, where you power on a new circuit and watch for smoke. In your workflow it means running a known-good input through the flow and comparing the result to what you know it should produce. It is the Lesson 10 testing discipline applied to the specific moment a tool or model changes. [^7]

## The worked example: Nadia's pricing scan drifts, then gets a deadline

Nadia runs a weekly competitive-pricing scan for her retail team. She built it in the style of Lesson 7: one template prompt, a list of competitor URLs as the input, and a clear output shape, a table of prices with a column flagging anything that moved more than five percent. [^5] It has run every Monday for four months, and the team trusts it.

Two things happen to it, and they are the two ways every workflow in this course will eventually be tested.

The first is silent. In week seventeen, Nadia notices the flagged column is empty. The scan ran, the table came back, and nothing is flagged, even though she knows two competitors changed prices that week. She re-runs it. Same result. She checks her prompt, her input, her settings. Nothing changed. She is looking at behavior drift, and she does not know it yet. The model behind her tool was updated, and the updated model stopped following the part of her instruction that said flag anything over five percent. Her workflow did not break. It quietly stopped doing the thing it was built to do, and only someone who knew what good output looked like would catch it. [^1]

The second is announced. A month later, Nadia opens the tool and sees a notice: the model her workflow has been using is deprecated, and it will be retired in six months. The vendor names a replacement and recommends migrating before the date. [^2] This is not a mystery. It is a deadline, and it is the kind of change the whole keeping-current habit is built to handle.

![Nadia's pricing scan gets tested two ways: a silent behavior drift in week seventeen (the flagged column comes back empty, caught by running the saved baseline through the workflow and comparing), then an announced deprecation deadline a month later (the model is retired in six months, so she adapts on a schedule by testing the replacement against her baseline while the old model still works).](</diagrams/keeping-current-adapting-to-tool-and-model-updates/4-nadia-timeline.png>)

Here is what Nadia does, and it is the pattern this lesson is teaching.

First, she catches the drift with a smoke test. She keeps a small set of known-good inputs from when the workflow worked, the same three competitor pages and the same expected output, saved as her baseline in Lesson 9's style. [^6] When she suspects the output has changed, she runs the baseline through the workflow and compares. The comparison shows the flag is missing, which tells her the change is real and not her imagination. That is the whole value of a baseline: it turns a vague feeling that output is off into a specific, checkable difference. [^6]

Second, she reads the release notes and the deprecation page. The tool's release notes tell her the model was updated and what the update changed. The deprecation page tells her the old model is being retired and names the replacement. [^2] [^3] She now knows both what already changed and what is about to change, which is the difference between reacting to a broken workflow and planning for a scheduled one.

Third, she decides whether to ride it out or adapt. This is the judgment call the lesson is really about, and it is not automatic. For the drift, the answer is adapt: the updated model no longer follows her flag instruction reliably, so she rewrites that part of the prompt to be more explicit, tests it against her baseline, and logs the change in her changelog. [^6] For the deprecation, the answer is also adapt, but on a schedule: she has six months, so she tests the replacement model against her baseline now, while the old one still works, rather than waiting until the retirement date forces the move. [^2] She does not panic and she does not procrastinate. She uses the notice period as runway.

## How to notice an update before it breaks a workflow

The drift in Nadia's example was caught because she had a baseline and a habit of checking. The deprecation was caught because the vendor announced it. Both are notice mechanisms, and you want both, because they catch different kinds of change.

The announced kind is the easier one to build. Every tool you rely on has a place where it tells you what changed. Most have release notes, a running list of what each version changed. Most have a deprecation or status page that names retired and retiring models. [^2] [^3] [^4] The habit is to check these on a cadence, not just when something breaks. Once a month, open the release notes and the deprecation page for each tool your workflows depend on, and scan for two things: a change to a feature you use, and a retirement date for a model you rely on. This is the same cadence discipline the course built for review gates and version logs. [^8] It takes ten minutes a month and it turns the scariest kind of change, the one that arrives with no warning, into the kind you saw coming.

The silent kind is harder, because no vendor announces it. The only way to catch behavior drift is to test for it, and the only way to test for it is to have a known-good baseline to compare against. [^1] [^6] This is why the versioning lesson told you to save the version that works and never overwrite it. [^6] That saved baseline is not just for rollback. It is your drift detector. When you suspect output has changed, you run the baseline through the workflow and look for differences. No baseline, no way to tell drift from your own mistake, and you end up rewriting a working prompt to fix a problem that was never in the prompt.

## Smoke-test your workflows against a change

When a tool or model updates, the question is not whether to check your workflows. It is which ones to check and how. The answer is a smoke test, and it is deliberately small.

A smoke test has three parts. First, a known-good input, the baseline you saved. Second, a known-good output, what that input should produce. Third, a comparison, running the input through the updated workflow and checking the output against what you expect. [^7] If the output matches, the update did not break that workflow, and you move on. If it does not, you have found the break before it reached anyone, which is exactly what the testing lesson promised. [^7]

![The smoke test has three parts: run a known-good baseline input through the updated workflow, then compare the output to the known-good output. A match means the update did not break the workflow and you ride it out; a difference means you found the break before it reached anyone and you adapt.](</diagrams/keeping-current-adapting-to-tool-and-model-updates/2-smoke-test.png>)

The discipline is to run the smoke test on every workflow that touches the changed tool or model, not just the one you happen to be thinking about. A model update can change the behavior of every workflow that uses that model, and the ones you are not looking at are the ones most likely to break quietly. [^1] This is where the runbook from Lesson 21 earns its keep: it tells you which workflows exist, what each one does, and what good output looks like, so you know what to smoke-test and what to compare against. [^9] A team that shares workflows, as in Lesson 22, smoke-tests the shared ones too, because a change that breaks a workflow five people run is five times more disruptive than one that breaks a workflow only you run. [^10]

## Ride it out or adapt: the honest decision

The last skill is judgment, and it is the one most people get wrong in both directions. Some people adapt to every update, rewriting workflows every time a tool changes, and burn their time chasing a moving target. Others ride out every update, assuming nothing changed, and let their workflows quietly rot. The honest answer sits between them, and it depends on the kind of change.

Ride it out when the change does not affect your workflow. A minor patch that fixes a bug you never hit, a cosmetic interface change, a model update that your smoke test shows produces the same output, none of these require action. The discipline is to confirm that with a smoke test, not to assume it. Riding out a change you have verified is efficient. Riding out a change you have not checked is neglect.

Adapt when the change affects your workflow, or when it is scheduled to. Two triggers force adaptation. The first is a smoke test that fails, which means the update changed your output and you need to fix it. The second is a deprecation date, which means the model you rely on will stop working and you need to move before it does. [^2] [^3] In both cases, adapt on a schedule, not in a panic. Test the replacement against your baseline while the old version still works, fix what breaks, log the change, and move when you are ready, not when the deadline forces you. [^6]

There is a third case worth naming, and it is the Lesson 20 honesty applied to updates. [^11] Sometimes the update is the moment you realize the workflow is not worth keeping current. If a model you rely on is being retired, and the replacement does not do the job as well, and the workflow was already marginal, the honest move may be to sunset it rather than to migrate it. Keeping current is not a mandate to keep every workflow alive forever. It is a mandate to know, for each workflow, whether it is worth the maintenance. [^11]

## Try it

Give yourself thirty minutes and build the keeping-current habit for one workflow you actually run. You need three things, and you may already have two of them.

Step one, confirm your baseline. If you saved a known-good version in Lesson 9, you are set. If not, run your workflow once now, on a normal input, and save the output as your baseline with today's date. [^6] This is the version you will compare against when you suspect drift.

Step two, find the notice pages. For each tool your workflow depends on, find its release notes and its deprecation or status page. [^2] [^3] [^4] Bookmark them, or add them to the runbook you built in Lesson 21. [^9] You cannot check what you cannot find, and most people never look for these pages until something breaks.

Step three, run a smoke test. Take your baseline input, run it through the workflow, and compare the output to your baseline. [^7] If they match, you have a working workflow and a working test. If they do not, you have just caught a drift you did not know about, which is the entire point.

Below is a try-it prompt that audits your keeping-current setup rather than flattering it. The trap in this habit is assuming you will remember to check, so the tool should hunt for the places where your setup will fail you.

> You are helping me keep one of my workflows current as the tools and models it depends on change. I will paste the workflow's purpose, the tools and models it uses, and any baseline output I have saved. Be critical. Check these things: do I have a saved known-good baseline to compare against, do I know where to find the release notes and deprecation pages for each tool and model I rely on, is there a specific cadence for checking them, and do I have a smoke test I can run to tell whether an update changed my output. For each gap, tell me the exact step that will let an update break my workflow without me noticing, and why fixing it matters for keeping the workflow alive, not just for this week.

Run that once and you will have what this lesson is really teaching: the difference between a workflow you built and a workflow you keep. Building it is one skill. Keeping it alive as the ground under it moves is the skill that makes everything else in this course last.
