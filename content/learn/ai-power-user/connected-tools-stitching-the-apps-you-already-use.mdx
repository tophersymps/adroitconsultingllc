Lesson 4 gave you a custom tool built to your task: tailored instructions, saved context, and a project of your own that holds the work. That tool is a strong first link. But most of your work does not live in one place. It lives in a spreadsheet here, a notes app there, an email draft in another window, a tracker somewhere else. The tool you built can work on any one of those things, but only if you carry the thing over to it yourself.

This lesson removes that carrying. It stitches the apps you already use into one connected flow, where one app's output becomes the next app's input and nobody has to copy and paste between windows. The idea is simple and it transfers to whatever software you already use, because it is a pattern, not a product.

## What a connected workflow is

A connected workflow is a chain of tools where the output of one becomes the input of the next. You already know the shape from Lessons 3 and 4. Your four-part flow was input, tool, output, and review. A connected workflow just means the tool does not stop at a chat window. It hands its output to the next tool in the chain, which does something with it and hands its own output to the tool after that, until the chain ends at an output you review.

Think of the difference this way. Marcus from the last two lessons used to type his weekly status request into a chat, wait, read the draft, and copy it into his email. That is a helpful tool with a human copy-paste step around it. A connected version does the same work but the chain carries the output: the week's figures land in the chat automatically, the draft appears, and the last thing a human touches is the check before it goes out. The human's job shrinks from carrying everything to reviewing the final result.

The piece that makes two apps become one flow is called a handoff. A handoff is the moment one tool passes its output to the next without you relaying it by hand. Apps can pass information to each other directly, the way two people exchange a document at a meeting, and they do it through a standard each side understands. That is where the term API enters, and it is worth a clear definition before it shows up everywhere around you.

## The API, the handoff, and why you already use them

An application programming interface, or API, is the agreed way two pieces of software talk to each other. IBM defines an API as a set of rules or protocols that enables software applications to communicate with each other to exchange data and features. [^1] The name sounds technical, but the idea is a contract. Two programs agree on how one makes a request and how the other answers, and that agreement lets them hand work back and forth without a human in the middle.

You already benefit from APIs every day even if you have never heard the term. Oracle points out that a typical internet user constantly benefits from APIs without realizing it, and gives a concrete example: a marketing dashboard that manages multiple social media accounts depends on the APIs that connect the social platforms to the dashboard and pull in the data. [^2] Every time a site shows you a map, every time a form saves into a database you never open, an API is carrying the handoff.

AWS describes the practical effect you care about. API integrations are software components that automatically update data between clients and servers, and automatic sync is the everyday version: your photo gallery syncing to the cloud, your laptop's clock updating when you travel. [^3] The word to hold is automatic. Once an integration is set up, the data moves without you asking it to move, which is exactly the copying you want to delete from your day.

You do not need to write an API to use one. You need to recognize when two of the apps you already use can hand off to each other, and then tell them to. The apps you already run very likely connect through an integration platform or a built-in connection, and most of those need no code.

## The enabler: no-code integration tools (a sidebar, not a dependency)

The thing that turned APIs from a developer topic into something a non-technical professional can use is the integration tool: software whose only job is to let you connect apps by pointing at them, not by coding. These platforms sit between your apps and move the handoffs for you, often on a trigger. When something happens in one app, the trigger fires, and the platform runs the matching action in the next app.

You have probably heard the common names, Zapier, Make, IFTTT, and automation features now built into suites like Microsoft Power Automate and Google's tools. They all do the same thing at their core: they let you say "when this happens in app A, do that in app B" and then they run it every time. Make, for example, describes its product as a platform for building and automating workflows without code, and Zapier describes a similar no-code automation capability that connects your apps into automated workflows. [^4] [^5]

Treat these as examples, not as the lesson. The names and buttons change faster than this course can teach them, and your company may only allow certain tools anyway. What transfers is the pattern: pick an app that already has the input, connect it to an app that should act on it, and let the trigger do the busywork. Whatever integration tool your company offers, or whatever built-in "automate this" button your suite has, is the same pattern wearing a different label.

## The pattern, stated plainly

Strip the brand names away and the connected workflow is four decisions.

**Where does the trigger live?** Something has to start the chain. It is usually a new thing appearing somewhere: a new row in a spreadsheet, a new email, a new form submission, a file added to a folder. AWS's description of automatic sync applies here: the integration notices the new thing and acts without you telling it to. [^3]

**What is the first input?** The trigger carries the raw material the chain works on. A new form response is the input to the next step. A new row in your tracker is the input to your review. Name what enters, the same way you named input in your four-part design in Lesson 3.

**What does each tool do with it?** Every link in the chain has a job. One tool may clean the data, another may draft text, another may file the result. Each one takes the output of the previous step and transforms it, the way your custom tool from Lesson 4 turns raw notes into a shaped draft. [^6]

**Where is the review?** The chain should end at a step you still check before it matters. Human-in-the-loop still applies exactly as it did in Lesson 3: treat the chain's output as untrusted until you have verified it. [^7] The review moves from being the only thing you do to being the last thing you do, but it does not disappear. That is the whole safety of the design.

If you can answer those four questions about a task, you can build its connected workflow with whatever tool your company already offers. You do not need to learn a product. You need to see your task as a chain.

## When connected workflows earn their keep, and when they do not

Connected workflows are not a hammer for every nail. They are worth building when a task has all three signs you learned to spot in Lesson 2: it is frequent, it is same-shaped, and it is low-risk. [^8] If you move the same kind of data between the same two apps twice a week, a connection pays for itself. If it is the same shape every time, a trigger can be written once and trusted. If a wrong move is cheap, the automation is safe to run without a human watching every link.

They are a poor fit when the task is irregular, transforms unpredictably, or runs into information only you can provide. There is no reliable trigger for "sometimes, if the quarter looks about right, email finance." Automation wants a clear signal. When the shape changes often, you end up fighting the very thing you built to save effort. Lesson 2's test for whether a task is automatable is the same test for whether a chain is worth connecting. [^8]

The other caution is a cost that sneaks up quietly: a chain with an error buried in the middle. If step one is wrong, every step after it is wrong, and the final output looks confident even though it is built on a mistake. That is why the review has to be at the end, and why you should check the chain's links when you first build them, not just the final product. A connected workflow concentrates your mistakes into one place, which is a feature if the review catches them, and a trap if you stop checking.

## A worked example: Marcus connects his weekly status

Marcus has built his custom tool for the weekly status email, a project that holds his standing instruction and his saved context. [^6] His remaining friction is the carrying. Every Monday he opens his tracker, pulls the week's numbers, opens his notes app, finds his notes, pastes both into the project, waits for a draft, then copies the draft into email. Four apps, four round trips, and the output is the only thing he actually needs.

He connects them one link at a time, using the integration tool his company already pays for.

His first link is the trigger. His notes live in a company notes app, and every Friday he adds a short entry: what happened, what is at risk, what needs a decision. He sets the trigger to fire when that entry is created. The moment he saves Friday's entry, the chain starts. Nothing on his part beyond saving the note he was going to write anyway.

His second link gathers the data. The trigger's output, the note text, feeds a step that pulls the related week's numbers. Company policies keep his tracker behind a stricter gate, so in practice this step hands the note to his custom-tool project, where his saved context supplies the project background and the instruction drafts the email from the note. He chose this split deliberately. The connection carries the note over, and his own tool does the drafting, so he still controls the style and the checks through the project he built in Lesson 4. [^6]

His third link is the output and the review. The draft lands in his email as a ready-to-send message, but not set as sent. The integration stops there on purpose. Marcus reads it, checks the numbers against his notes, fixes anything the tool got wrong, and sends it himself. The chain did the carrying and the drafting. The review stayed a human decision, exactly the human-in-the-loop boundary from Lesson 3. [^7]

The result is not a magic button that sends his email without him. It is a chain that removes the four round trips, so his Monday morning shrinks from half an hour of copy-paste to a two-minute read of a draft he would have written anyway. The work he kept is the work only he can do: judge it before it goes out.

## Try it

Map one two-step chain of your own. Do not build it yet. Design it. Take about twenty minutes and answer these four questions for a task you already shuttle between two apps.

1. **The trigger.** What new thing starts the chain? A form submission, a new row, a new email, a file drop? Name it so specifically that you would know it when you see it.
2. **The first input.** What does that trigger carry? The raw data the chain acts on: the response text, the row's values, the request.
3. **The tool's job.** What should happen to that input? A draft response, a file move, a meeting scheduled, a summary written? This is often your custom tool from Lesson 4, acting on the input a trigger just delivered. [^6]
4. **The review.** Where does a human still check before the result matters? Name the specific check, and set the chain to stop there rather than shipping on its own. [^7]

Then get a second opinion with any chat tool you already use. Paste this:

> I do [describe the task] repeatedly. It moves [what] from [app A] to [app B]. Using the connected-workflow pattern, here is my draft: trigger is [trigger], first input is [input], the tool does [job], and the review is [check]. Tell me where the trigger is too vague to fire reliably, where I have skipped a step, and whether the review I named is the right one to catch the mistakes this task is prone to. Do not build it. Just sharpen the design.

Read the answer the way AI at Work taught you: the tool is a helpful colleague with good instincts and no idea how your week actually runs, so its suggestions are a starting point for your judgment, not a verdict. [^9] What you are walking away with is a two-link chain you could build once your company's integration tool is in front of you. The design is the transferable part, and the design is done.

## What's next

You have taken the custom tool you built in Lesson 4 and seen how it becomes one link in a chain, handing its output to the next app instead of stopping at the chat window. You can name a trigger, a first input, a tool's job, and a review, which is everything you need to design a connected workflow with whatever software your company already offers.

The next lesson addresses the quiet problem every handoff touches. A chain can only work if the output of one step arrives in a shape the next step can actually consume. A paragraph of prose is fine for a human reader and unreliable for a downstream tool. Lesson 6 is about structured output: getting data back in a format downstream tools can consume. It shows you how to ask a tool for its answer as a structured list or table, so the next app in the chain can read it cleanly instead of trying to parse prose. That is the skill that makes your connected chains rugged rather than fragile.

Come to Lesson 6 with the two-step chain you just designed, because the next step is making the output of the first link reliably readable by the second.

*Note: connected workflows and the APIs that power them are a general pattern, not a feature of one vendor. The specific integration tools, trigger names, and buttons change over time, and some of the links in a chain only connect with the tools your company uses and allows. Treat your company's data and acceptable-use policies as the binding rules, verify any tool's current behavior against the vendor's own documentation, and design the chain, trigger, input, tool, review, using whatever software you already have. The pattern is what transfers.*
