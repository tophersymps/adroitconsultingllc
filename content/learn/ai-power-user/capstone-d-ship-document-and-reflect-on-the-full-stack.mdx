Your workflow now survives a bad month. Capstone C gave it a regression set, a gate that blocks a bad draft, and failures that announce themselves instead of hiding. [^12] That is real progress and it is still not shipped, because everything the workflow knows about itself lives in your head. You know which folder is the authoritative source when two look alike. You know why the digest is monthly and not weekly. You know which warning message means retry and which means stop. None of that is written down, and heads forget, drift, and go on holiday.

Shipping is the work of closing that gap. It is a small amount of writing compared to the build, and it decides whether the thing you built survives its second year.

## Define the terms

**Shipping** in this course means handing a working workflow into regular operation with the written material needed for someone other than its builder to run it. It is borrowed from software release, with the same core idea: a build becomes a product when it can be operated without the person who assembled it standing nearby. Shipping does not mean publishing to a marketplace or telling a client. It means the workflow now has an owner, a page, and a way to be handed over.

**A runbook entry** is the one page per workflow that says how to run, check, and fix it. A runbook is a compilation of routine procedures and operations that an operator carries out, and the useful standard is that it lets another operator, with prerequisite expertise, manage and troubleshoot the system. [^1] In your case the other operator is usually you in six months, which is a person with alarmingly little memory of build day.

**A decision record** is a short note that captures one choice, the situation that made it necessary, and the condition under which you would reverse it. The architecture decision record format from software practice is deliberately small: a title, the context and forces at play, the decision, and the consequences. Decisions that are reversed stay in the record, marked as superseded, because knowing that something used to be the decision is part of understanding the current one. [^2]

**A retro** is a look back at a finished piece of work to decide what to keep and what to change. In software teams the retrospective is a meeting held at the end of a project or iteration to discuss what was successful, what could be improved, and how to carry both into the next round of work. [^3] Your version is smaller and more useful: one sitting, four questions, and numbers.

**A handover test** is what makes the runbook real. You give the entry to someone who has never seen the workflow, ask them to run or explain one cycle from the page alone, and watch where they stop. Every stop is a missing field. A document nobody has tried to follow is a document with unknown errors in it.

**The bus factor** is the number of people who have to disappear before the work stalls. It is defined as the minimum number of team members who have to suddenly vanish for a project to stop due to lack of knowledge or capability, and a bus factor of one is a single point of failure. [^4] Every unshipped workflow has a bus factor of one by construction. Shipping is how you raise it.

## Why shipping is its own job

Three reasons, and each one is a job the build does not do for you.

The first is that build knowledge is tacit. On the day you assemble a pipeline you hold a dozen small facts in working memory: which sheet tab is the live one, that the vendor list needs a trailing space trimmed, that the retry only works if you run it after eight in the morning. Working memory is exactly the wrong storage for operational facts, because it is the memory that fails first and silently.

The second is that maintenance arrives at the worst possible moment. A workflow breaks on the morning it is due, not on a quiet afternoon when you have an hour to spare. The person fixing it that morning is under time pressure and holds no context. Written material is a gift to that person, and that person is you.

The third reason is compounding. The retro you run at the end of this workflow is the input to your next one. Skip it and every build starts from zero lessons, which is how people end up rebuilding the same mistakes across five automations and calling the fifth one easier. The record is what turns four separate builds into a practice.

There is a fourth, quieter reason. A workflow with no page is not really yours in any durable sense. You cannot delegate it, you cannot defend its cost, you cannot improve it deliberately. Ownership that lives only in your habits is a claim about your memory, and memory is not a system.

## Write the runbook entry

One entry per workflow, one screen, seven fields. The order matters less than the completeness.

![Anatomy of a runbook entry: seven fields covering purpose, trigger, inputs with exact locations, ordered steps and the pieces each loads, the checks that must pass, failure messages with a first action, and owner, review date and retirement condition, with the bare minimum for week one set against what you add after the first month](</diagrams/capstone-d-ship-document-and-reflect-on-the-full-stack/2-runbook-entry-anatomy.png>)

Start with the sentence a stranger needs. Not the steps, the job. "Every month, on the second business day, sends my manager a one-page digest of vendors flagged for risk." A reader who understands the job can absorb the steps faster, and a reader who does not understand the job cannot follow the steps at all.

Then the trigger, including the two details people always omit: the time zone, and what happens on a holiday. Then the inputs with exact locations and the naming pattern each file must match. Capstone C produced a real lesson here. Priya's validate step counted files in a folder and passed when the wrong file was sitting in it. [^12] The runbook field that would have prevented it is the naming pattern, written down as a checkable rule rather than a folder name.

Then the ordered steps with the piece each one loads. Name the template. Name the house-style instruction. The point is not documentation for its own sake, it is that six months from now "the draft step" will be a vague memory whereas "the vendor digest template, version dated August, with the house-style instruction attached" is a thing you can find. [^8]

Then the checks, written as statements that can fail. "Flagged-row count in the digest equals the count in the source" is a check. "Looks right" is not. This field is where your definition of done from Capstone A lives in operational form. [^7]

Then the failure messages and the first action for each. Every failure your workflow can produce should have appeared at least once during hardening, which means you already know the exact text and what to do about it. Write both. [^12] A failure with no documented action is a failure that gets handled by intuition at the worst time, or handled by ignoring it.

Finally, the ownership line: who maintains it, when you will next look at it on purpose, and the condition under which it should be switched off. That last part is not pessimism. Workflows have a maintenance tax and a retirement point, and writing the retirement condition at ship time is far easier than defending a workflow that stopped earning its keep two quarters ago. [^9]

## Record the decisions, not just the steps

The runbook answers how. It does not answer why, and why is what gets silently reversed. Keeping those two jobs on separate pages is not fussiness. Documentation frameworks separate how-to guidance from explanation because a page that mixes them serves neither reader well, and the same split holds here. [^6]

Two or three decision records per workflow is enough. Each one is a paragraph with three parts: what the situation was, what you chose, and what would change your mind. Keep them short. The value is not in the length, it is in the third part, because a decision with no stated reversal condition cannot be revisited honestly. It can only be argued about.

Compare two ways of writing the same choice. The weak version is "We use a monthly cadence." The useful version says: the input files are published monthly and the manager's review cycle is monthly, so weekly runs would produce three digests with nothing new in them and teach the manager to skim past the one that does; we would move to weekly if the input published weekly or if a vendor breach ever landed mid-month. The second version tells a colleague what you were optimizing for and where the boundary is.

Priya writes two records for the digest. The first covers cadence, monthly rather than weekly, and names the trigger to revisit it. The second covers the review gate: a human approval before the digest is delivered, rather than an automated check only, because the digest goes to a manager who acts on it and a wrong flag costs more than a delayed send. The reversal condition is explicit. If the digest ever becomes internal reference material that nobody acts on directly, the gate can drop to a check.

That second record matters more than it looks. Without it, someone reading the pipeline six months from now sees a manual step in a mostly automated flow and removes it as an inefficiency. With it, the manual step has a reason attached and an owner who knows when to remove it.

## Run the retro on numbers, not memory

The retro has four questions and one rule: no conclusion without a number attached.

What did it cost to build. Hours per phase, from your build notes, split across scope, assembly, hardening, and shipping. If you did not keep notes, reconstruct from your calendar while the memory is still warm and mark the estimate as reconstructed. A cost you guessed is still far more useful than a cost you never estimated, as long as you label it.

What does it return per month. The honest comparison is not "automation versus nothing," it is the manual version's real duration against the automated version's real duration including the review time you still spend. Subtract. That is the monthly return, and the build cost divided by it is your break-even in months. A workflow that saves three hours a month and cost seven hours to build breaks even in month three, which is a defensible thing to say to a manager and a completely different sentence from "it saves time."

What broke, and what it cost. Pull this from the run log rather than recollection. Note the failure, the time it cost, and whether your checks caught it or a human did. A failure caught by a check is evidence the hardening works. A failure caught by a person noticing something odd is a check you have not written yet. [^12]

What carries forward. Two rules, maximum. Not a list of aspirations, two concrete changes you will apply at the start of the next build. Two rules get applied. Ten rules become a document nobody reads.

![Priya's retro ledger for the first month: cost column with hours per capstone phase and library pieces reused, return column with the manual baseline, net saving, break-even and monthly maintenance, and the kept column listing regression folders, recorded decisions and the rules carried forward](</diagrams/capstone-d-ship-document-and-reflect-on-the-full-stack/3-priya-retro-ledger.png>)

The retro is also where you decide whether a post-mortem is owed. Those are different tools. A retro asks what to change in your practice. A post-mortem is a written record of a specific failure: what happened, the impact, the root cause, and the follow-up actions that stop it recurring, written without blame for the people involved. [^5] If the workflow died hard during hardening, write the post-mortem first, because the retro cannot evaluate a build whose failure story is still muddled. [^10]

## Worked example: Priya ships the vendor risk digest

Priya has a hardened workflow that passes six regression folders, blocks a bad draft at the gate, and has produced one real August digest. She has a run log with three entries and build notes on a single page. Shipping takes her an hour, plus twenty minutes for the handover test.

She writes the runbook entry first, and the honest part is what she has to look up. She thought she knew the folder path and gets it wrong on the first attempt. She thought the trigger was "first week of the month" and finds her scheduling note says the second business day. Both errors would have lived in her memory and cost her a broken month later.

Her entry reads, in condensed form: purpose, sends the monthly vendor risk digest to her manager on the second business day. Trigger, the second business day of each month in Eastern time, and if that day is a holiday the run moves to the next business day, checked manually because the scheduler does not know the holiday calendar. Inputs, three files in the risk folder, the exception export and the approved vendor list and the prior month's flagged list, each matching a filename pattern that includes the month in YYYY-MM form. Steps, validate by naming pattern, normalize to the vendor contract shape, draft from the digest template with the house-style instruction loaded, gate on Priya's approval, deliver to the shared drive with the dated filename. Checks, the four definition-of-done checks plus the reconciliation of flagged counts. Failures, a naming pattern mismatch prints the expected pattern and the files found, a count mismatch prints both numbers and stops before delivery, and a gate with no approval after two business days prints a stale review warning. Owner, Priya, reviewed each January, and retired if the vendor process moves into the procurement system.

Then the decision records, two of them, cadence and gate, each about a hundred words. Then she carries the entry to Marcus in accounts payable, who has never seen the workflow. She asks him to run the September cycle from the page alone while she watches without helping.

He gets through validate and stops at the input section, because two folders exist with similar names and the entry names only one. He asks which one is authoritative. Priya's answer takes four seconds and would never have reached a page she had not tested. That stop is the entire value of the handover test: one ambiguity found in twenty minutes instead of on a Tuesday morning in October.

The retro takes her thirty minutes. Her numbers: 1.5 hours scoping, 3 hours assembling, 2.5 hours hardening, 1 hour shipping, 8 hours total. The manual digest took her 3.5 hours a month. The automated version costs her about half an hour of review and a quarter hour of maintenance. Net saving, roughly 2.75 hours a month, and she rounds down to 2.5 to stay honest. Break-even at a bit over three months. Four of her six pieces came from the library rather than being authored fresh, which is the single number that best predicts how fast her next build will go. [^8]

Her two rules for the next build. First, every scope gets a maintenance estimate in hours per month, because she assumed zero and the real answer was fifteen minutes, and a scope without a maintenance line is a scope nobody has run for a year. Second, any step whose failure could pass unnoticed gets a count reconciliation check on day one rather than during hardening, because the missing-file case that passed validation in Capstone C was exactly that kind of step and it cost her most of an afternoon to find. [^12]

She also does the unglamorous thing at the end: she deletes the manual digest checklist from her recurring task list and adds a line to her runbook index pointing at the entry. Two months from now, the workflow will be found by whoever looks for it, and the old manual routine will not quietly compete with it.

![One shipped workflow, three documents: the runbook entry for the person operating it in six months, the decision record for a colleague covering or changing it, and the retro notes for the person designing the next build, each answering a different question about the same pipeline](</diagrams/capstone-d-ship-document-and-reflect-on-the-full-stack/1-three-documents.png>)

Priya's capstone is now a thing that exists outside her head. That is the difference between the four lessons of the capstone and everything before them. She did not learn another pattern. She completed a cycle.

## Try it prompt

Use this with your assistant once your workflow is hardened and has produced at least one real output. Paste your build notes and run log, not your memory of them.

> You are helping me ship a workflow I have already built and hardened. I will paste three things: my scope and definition of done, a description of my pipeline step by step, and my run log from the runs so far. Do not praise the work and do not summarize what I send back to me. First, act as a handover tester: read only what I have given you, then list every question you would have to ask before you could run this workflow yourself for one cycle, ordered by how badly the answer being wrong would hurt. Be specific, quote the phrase in my material that left the question open. Second, draft a runbook entry with seven fields: purpose in one sentence, trigger and cadence including time zone and holiday behavior, inputs with exact locations and naming patterns, ordered steps naming the template or instruction each step loads, the checks that must pass written as statements that can fail, the failure messages I will see with a first action for each, and owner, review date, retirement condition. Where my material does not give you an answer, write UNKNOWN in capitals rather than inventing one. Third, draft up to three decision records in the form context, decision, what would change my mind, choosing only decisions a future reader might otherwise reverse by accident. Fourth, interview me for the retro with four questions only: what it cost per phase, what it returns per month against the manual baseline, what broke and whether a check caught it, and what two rules I should carry into my next build. Push back if any number I give is an estimate rather than a measurement, and tell me which of my claims is currently unsupported.

Then do the work it names, including the handover test with a real person if you can find one. When a colleague can run one cycle from your page, when each of your two or three load-bearing decisions has a reversal condition written down, and when your retro has numbers in every column, the capstone is done and so is the course.

## One pass over the whole arc

Thirty lessons ago you were using AI every day and had no workflow you owned. [^11] The path from there to here was not a list of tools. It was six moves, repeated until they became a habit.

![The ownership cycle: audit, choose, build, harden, ship, reflect, with reflect feeding the next audit and the library shrinking every subsequent build](</diagrams/capstone-d-ship-document-and-reflect-on-the-full-stack/4-ownership-cycle.png>)

You audited your week for the repetitive slice. You chose what deserved a workflow and what deserved to stay manual. You scoped and assembled a pipeline from pieces instead of authoring from scratch. You hardened it with a regression set, a blocking gate, and loud failures. You shipped it with a runbook, decisions, and a handover test. You reflected on measured numbers and carried two rules forward. Then the cycle starts again, and the second pass is faster because your library grew and your retro told you what to fix.

The workflow you shipped this week is not the point. The cycle is the point. It is the thing that survives the next tool change, the next model upgrade, the next role change. [^13] Workflows come and go, and the practice of building one, hardening it, shipping it, and learning from it is what makes the next one cheap.
