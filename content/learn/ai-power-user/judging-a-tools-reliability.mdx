Here is the question nobody asks before it costs them a day: how reliable is this tool, really? Not how reliable the marketing says it is. Not how reliable the vendor claims it will be. How reliable it is right now, the way you would measure it, for the specific workflow you are about to build on it.

Most people skip the question. They pick the biggest name, or the newest feature, or the tool a coworker recommended, and they trust it until it fails. Lesson 24 showed what to do after a workflow dies. This lesson is the judgment you bring before one ever gets built: how to look at a tool, a model, or a platform and form an honest, evidence-backed estimate of how much you can count on it. It is the quiet skill that runs underneath every other lesson in the course, because it decides which workflows are worth building on which foundations in the first place.

The core idea is simple and easy to get wrong. A reliable tool is not one that never fails. No tool never fails. A reliable tool is one whose failure you can predict, tolerate, and recover from. The power user's mental model is not about finding the tool that will never let you down. It is about grading each tool so you know exactly how much you are depending on it, and building your workflow so that the gap between what the tool guarantees and what your workflow needs is covered by you.

## Define the terms

Five terms carry this lesson, and the first two sound interchangeable but are not.

Reliability is the degree to which a tool does what you need it to do, when you need it, for as long as you need it. It is not an abstract quality. It is a measured property, and the people who run large systems for a living have spent decades defining how to measure it. Google's Site Reliability Engineering team, which operates some of the largest systems in the world, treats reliability as something you manage like risk: you decide how much unreliability a service can tolerate, set a target, and hold the service to it. [^1]

Availability is the specific way reliability is most often measured: the fraction of time a service is actually usable. The industry expresses it in "nines": 99.9 percent, 99.99 percent, 99.999 percent. Each additional nine is roughly an order of magnitude closer to perfect. [^1] [^2] To make the numbers human, consider what they mean over a year. A service at 99.9 percent availability can be down for about nine hours across the whole year. At 99.99 percent, that falls to under an hour. At 99.999 percent, it is about five minutes. [^1] These are not marketing slogans. They are commitments with a specific, checkable meaning, and the difference between 99.9 and 99.99 is the difference between a tool that can vanish for most of a workday and one that barely ever goes dark.

![What the nines mean over a year: 99.9 percent is about nine hours down, 99.99 percent is under an hour, and 99.999 percent is about five minutes, each additional nine an order of magnitude closer to perfect.](</diagrams/judging-a-tools-reliability/2-nines-downtime.png>)

You will also meet three acronyms, and Google's SRE team is careful about how it separates them. [^2] An SLI, a service level indicator, is a specific number you can measure, usually error rate or speed or uptime. An SLO, a service level objective, is the target you set for that number, like "successfully serve 99.9 percent of requests." An SLA, a service level agreement, is a contract with a consequence if the target is missed, usually a financial credit. [^2] The way to tell them apart is to ask what happens if the number is missed. If the answer is a payout or a penalty, it is an SLA. If the answer is just an apology, it is an SLO. [^2] Almost everything a vendor advertises as an "SLA" for its API is really a generous SLO, and knowing which one you actually have is the difference between expecting compensation and expecting nothing.

An error budget is the natural partner of an SLO: the acceptable amount of failure built into the target. If a service's SLO is 99.9 percent, then it has a 0.1 percent error budget, its allowed amount of being down or broken before it fails its own promise. [^1] Google's SRE team uses error budgets to manage the tradeoff between reliability and speed, releasing features while budget remains and slowing down when it runs low. [^1] For your purposes, the error budget translates into a plain truth: every tool you use has agreed, implicitly, to be down a certain amount, and that amount is not zero.

A track record is the actual history of how a tool has behaved over time, as opposed to what its vendor promises it will do. This is the load-bearing term of the whole lesson. A promise is what the landing page says. A track record is what you find when you check the status page history, read a post-mortem from a real outage, and notice whether the tool you rely on has changed under you without telling anyone. [^3] Whenever a vendor's promise and its track record disagree, trust the track record, because that is the pattern your future will repeat.

![The reliability vocabulary: reliability and availability are measured properties, an SLI feeds an SLO which becomes an SLA only when a missed target carries a penalty, an error budget is the allowed failure, and a track record is observed history that beats any promise.](</diagrams/judging-a-tools-reliability/1-reliability-vocabulary.png>)

## The worked example: Priya grades two tools before she adopts either

Priya works in marketing operations, and she has been asked to automate the weekly client reporting that her team currently builds by hand. It takes her four hours every Friday afternoon: pull the numbers from three platforms, run a summary through an AI tool to draft the client write-up, format it, and send it before her boss reviews it Monday morning. She wants to build it into the repeatable workflow this course teaches, but she noticed something during Lesson 24's post-mortem on an older workflow that died: she never actually assessed the tools before she adopted them. She picked them because they were recommended, and she was burned when one stopped working mid-stream. [^8] So this time she grades the candidates before she builds.

She has two finalists. The first is a slick new AI writing tool with heavy marketing: the landing page promises "99.9 percent uptime," "enterprise grade," and "planned outages built in." It has great design, a big launch, and a well-known backer. The second is the model her current AI platform offers, which she already uses for other reporting and whose behavior she has learned to predict over several months. [^7] Priya does not assume the flashy one is more reliable because it is flashier. She grades them.

Here is what she finds, and it separates the two like night and day.

![Priya grades two tools: the slick new AI writing tool has marketing but no track record and an SLO wearing an SLA's clothes, while the model she already uses has months of observed behavior, so she builds on the model she can grade.](</diagrams/judging-a-tools-reliability/3-priya-two-tools.png>)

First, she checks whether the new tool has a real track record. Its status page exists, but the history is basically empty: it is under a year old, it has never been through a serious outage, and there are no post-mortems published. [^3] She cannot find a single documented incident to read. That is not proof it is fragile. It is proof she has no evidence either way, which means she would be trusting a promise with nothing behind it. The model she already uses, by contrast, has a status page she has watched for months, a history of real incidents with explanations she was able to read, and a pattern of behavior she has logged in her baseline. Her track record for that model is not the vendor's marketing. It is her own months of lived observation, which is the strongest evidence a power user ever has. [^3] [^6]

Second, she weighs the promises against the track records, the way this lesson teaches. The new tool advertises an "SLA" with a number, but when she reads the fine print, the number is a target, not a contract. Missing it earns an apology, not a credit, which makes it an SLO wearing an SLA's clothes. [^2] More important, she notices a history lesson the whole industry learned the hard way: a big vendor with a good reputation is not immune to failure. In July 2024, a routine software update from the cybersecurity firm CrowdStrike, one of the largest and most trusted companies in its field, crashed nearly 8.5 million Windows devices worldwide and disrupted airlines, hospitals, banks, and broadcasters. [^5] Microsoft, which estimated the 8.5 million figure, pointed out that this was less than one percent of all Windows machines, yet the broad economic and societal impact was enormous. [^5] Priya takes the lesson she needs from this: popularity and industry trust do not grant immunity, and even a small failure in a widely trusted component can be catastrophic when enough workflows depend on it. [^5]

Third, she matches each tool's reliability to what her workflow needs. Her client report is important, but it is not a heart monitor. It runs once a week, and a failure that resolves in a couple of hours on a Friday afternoon, which she would catch and re-run, is tolerable. She does not need a 99.999 percent service, and chasing one would cost her time and budget she does not have. [^1] What she needs is a tool whose failure she can predict, detect, and recover from. The model she already knows wins on every one of those. She can predict its behavior from her months of use. She detects problems with the baseline and smoke-test habit she built in Lessons 9 and 10. [^6] And because she has used it long enough, she has a fallback when it does go down: run the report with a slightly older saved version of her instructions, or do the summary by hand, which she still knows how to do.

So Priya builds her workflow on the model she can grade, not the one with the better advertisement. She keeps the slick tool in mind as a candidate to re-check later, once it has accumulated a real track record. She does not trust it, distrust it, or dismiss it. She grades it honestly, and grades it again in six months, because reliability is not a permanent verdict. [^7]

## The signals that separate a dependable tool from a fragile one

Priya found four signals that separate the two candidates, and they generalize to every tool you will ever evaluate. None of them requires technical expertise. They are reading habits.

![Four signals that separate a dependable tool from a fragile one: a readable track record, a vendor that admits failure in public, honest handling of change, and matching reliability to how long the tool needs to be right.](</diagrams/judging-a-tools-reliability/4-four-signals.png>)

Signal one, does the tool have a track record you can read? A status page is the minimum, and it should show history, not just a green dot for today. Look for documented incidents, and look for whether the vendor explains them. A post-mortem that names what happened and what will change is a sign of a dependable operator. [^3] A tool with a status page and no history is a tool you cannot grade yet, which is a reason to be conservative, not to assume the best.

Signal two, does the vendor admit failure in public? This is the hardest signal to fake and the most informative. A vendor that publishes incident history, post-mortems, and honest notes about what broke is telling you what you can rely on by showing you how it handles being unreliable. [^3] [^5] A vendor that hides its incidents, or only ever announces them as "intermittent" with no follow-up, is giving you its track record in the least useful way possible. The July 2024 CrowdStrike outage was not notable because a big company failed. It was notable because it happened so fast and so broadly, and it reminded every user that you notice reliability most when it disappears. [^5]

Signal three, what happens when the tool changes under you? A vendor that announces changes, gives notice, and lets you see a migration path is dependable. [^7] A vendor that swaps the model behind your workflow without telling you, which Lesson 23 showed happens often, is fragile in exactly the way that quietly kills workflows. [^7] You want the vendor who treats a change as a thing you should know about, not as something you discover the hard way.

Signal four, how long does the tool need to be right? This is the reliability-to-task matching that Priya applied. [^1] The value of a tool's reliability depends entirely on what you are using it for. A tool at 99.9 percent, down nine hours a year, is fine for a weekly report you can re-run or catch. [^1] The same tool is a poor choice for something your whole team waits on synchronously, or something you can never afford to lose. Grade the tool, then grade the gap between its reliability and what your workflow demands, and cover the gap yourself with a fallback, a saved baseline, or a manual path. [^6]

## Weigh the track record, not the promises

The single most important move in this skill is refusing to let a promise stand in for a track record. Vendors are good at promises. The landing page will always be more confident than the tool is. The lesson is to treat every claim about reliability as a hypothesis to test, not a fact to accept, and to test it against the only trustworthy evidence: observed behavior over time. [^2] [^3]

Here is the honest version of that test, and it is the same one Priya ran. First, find the tool's status page and read its incident history, not just its headline number, because headline numbers and real incident history can disagree. [^3] Second, read at least one real post-mortem if the vendor publishes them, and notice whether it explains the root cause or just confirms the outage. [^3] Third, ask what the vendor actively guarantees, in a way that has a consequence, versus what it merely hopes to provide. [^2] Fourth, and cheapest of all, use the tool on real, low-stakes work before you trust it with anything important, and log what you observe in the same baseline-and-changelog system you already built in Lesson 9. [^6] A month of your own careful observation is worth more than any feature list.

When a promise and a track record disagree, the track record wins. When there is no track record yet, you are betting on a promise, and the honest move is to treat it as a bet: keep the stakes low, add a review date to check back later, and never rest a critical workflow on evidence you do not have. [^7] This is not cynicism. It is calibration. The point is not to distrust every vendor. It is to know exactly how much evidence each one has earned, and to size your dependence accordingly.

## Build your own mental model

You have now seen the pieces, and this is the moment to put them together, because the skill this lesson is teaching is not a procedure or a checklist. It is a mental model: a stable way of seeing tools so that the right judgment is the easy one to make.

Your mental model has three parts, and they work together. The first part is a grade for every tool you depend on, based on its track record, its handling of failure, its transparency about change, and how long it needs to be reliable, not on its marketing. [^1] [^3] The second part is an ongoing baseline and changelog that keeps that grade current, because reliability is not a fixed property, it drifts, and only recorded observation can track the drift. [^7] [^6] The third part is a matching habit: for every workflow you build, you ask what reliability it actually needs and whether the tool you chose meets it, and you cover the gap yourself with a fallback and a manual path you still know how to run. [^6]

This is what separates a power user from someone who simply uses AI heavily. A heavy user trusts tools and is surprised when they fail. A power user grades tools, builds on the ones whose reliability is adequate for the job, and is rarely surprised, because the failures that happen were either tolerated, detected, or recoverable. The mental model turns reliability from something you discover in a crisis into something you sized in advance. It is the judgment Lesson 24's post-mortem discipline assumed you had, and it is the skill that makes every workflow you build from here worth building.

## Try it

Give yourself thirty minutes and grade a tool you actually rely on, one that a workflow depends on or one you are about to build on. Pick anything: an AI model, an automation platform, a data connector. You need the tool's status page, if it has one, and your own memory of how it has behaved.

Step one, find the track record. Open the status page and read its incident history, not just today's status. [^3] Step two, read one real incident or post-mortem if the vendor publishes them, and note whether it explains the root cause. [^3] Step three, check your own records from Lesson 9. What have you logged about this tool's behavior? Has your baseline ever failed against it? [^6] Step four, answer the matching question honestly: for the workflow this tool supports, is its reliability adequate, and what is your fallback if it goes down mid-run? [^6]

Below is a try-it prompt that grades the tool rather than flattering it. The trap in this skill is letting a shiny landing page do your judging for you, so the tool should hunt for the places where your evidence is thin.

> You are helping me form an honest, evidence-backed estimate of how reliable a tool is before I trust it with a workflow. I will paste the tool's name, any status page or incident history I can find, and my own notes on how it has behaved for me. Be critical. Check these things: what does the tool's actual track record show versus what its marketing promises, does the vendor publish post-mortems that explain root causes or only confirm outages, how does the vendor handle changes to the model or the tool underneath me, and what is the gap between this tool's reliability and the reliability my workflow actually needs. For each gap, tell me the specific risk of building on this tool as it is now, and the concrete fallback or manual path that would cover it without adding busywork.

Run that once and you will have done the real work of this lesson: you will have replaced a guess about a tool with a grade. The tools you build on tomorrow will be the ones whose reliability you sized today, and that is the difference between a workflow that survives and one that takes you by surprise.
