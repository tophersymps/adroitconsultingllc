Lesson 2 gave you a named task, the frequent, same-shaped, low-risk one that the audit flagged as your best first candidate. That task is the target. This lesson is the bridge between finding it and building on it. It is decomposition: breaking the task you do as one blurry whole into the four parts every workflow needs, so you can see exactly where the setup goes and where your judgment stays.

The reason decomposition comes before building is simple. You cannot build a workflow for a task you cannot name its parts. A workflow is not a magic button. It is a fixed order of named pieces, and until you can say what those pieces are, you are still guessing. Decomposition is how you stop guessing.

## What decomposition is and why it matters

Decomposition means taking a task you currently do as a single undivided action and splitting it into named parts you can see, change, and check. It is the same move an engineer makes before building anything, and it is the same move you make before building a workflow. You take the task, you name its parts, and suddenly the task stops being a blur and becomes a design.

The input-process-output model is a well-established way to describe any workflow, and it is a good place to start. Adobe's explanation of the model puts it plainly: input-process-output, or IPO, is a visual tool used to describe a workflow, the flow of information, or activities within a system. [^1] The model splits a process into three columns. Input is the data, information, or resources that enter the system. Process is the activities or transformations performed on the inputs. Output is the results, products, or outcomes produced by the processes. [^1]

For an AI workflow, that three-part model becomes four parts, because AI adds a step the classic model does not make explicit. The four parts are input, tool, output, and review. The tool is the process step, the thing that does the work. Review is the check you run before the output leaves your hands, and it is the part that makes the workflow safe to rely on. Everything in the rest of this course builds on these four parts, so it is worth getting them clear.

## The four parts, defined

**Input.** What you feed in. The raw material the workflow consumes: the numbers, the notes, the list, the document, the request. Adobe's definition is the one to hold: input is the data, information, or resources that enter the system. [^1] The input is the contract between the workflow and the rest of your work. If you can name what goes in, you know what to gather before you run it, and you know the workflow cannot start until you have it.

**Tool.** The thing that does the work. For a non-technical professional this is usually an AI chat tool with a saved instruction, or a template, or a form. The tool is where the setup lives. It is the part that carries the repeatable work, the fixed order, the formatting, the phrasing. This is the part you will build properly in Lesson 4, and it is the part most people think of first when they imagine a workflow.

**Output.** What comes out. The draft, the summary, the email, the report, the cleaned spreadsheet. A good output is specific and measurable: you know what it should look like before you run the workflow. Adobe's guidance is to start with the desired output and work back, and to make the output thorough, specific, and measurable. [^1] If you cannot describe the output before you run the workflow, you cannot tell a good result from a bad one.

**Review.** The check you run before the output leaves your hands. This is where your judgment stays. Atlassian's guidance on human-in-the-loop systems is direct: treat agent output as untrusted until your existing checks have verified it, and review is validating the output before it ships. [^2] The review is the part that makes the workflow safe, because it keeps a human deciding at the point where the output could still be wrong.

## Why the review is the part that makes it safe

The review is not a formality and it is not a nice-to-have. It is the whole reason the workflow is safe to rely on. Lesson 1 introduced the human-in-the-loop pattern: the tool drafts, the human decides. The review is where that decision happens. Atlassian puts it bluntly: treat agent output as untrusted until your existing checks have verified it. [^2]

The review is also where you catch the failure modes. The tool can be confidently wrong. It can turn a "roughly forty percent" from your notes into a confident "the rollout is over halfway complete." It can drop a metric, or invent a number, or phrase something in a way that would embarrass you in front of the person reading it. The review is the net that catches those. It is not a weakness in the workflow. It is the design.

This is the point that separates a workflow you can trust from a tool you are gambling on. A workflow with a review is a system you can rely on, because the failure is caught before it ships. A workflow without a review is a tool that occasionally sends something wrong, and you will not know which run it was until it is too late. The review is what turns the second into the first.

## A worked example: Marcus decomposes his weekly status email

Marcus from Lesson 2 found his best first candidate: the weekly status email to his director. It is frequent, he runs it every Monday. It is same-shaped, it has the same inputs and the same format every time. It is low-risk, it is a draft he checks before sending. Now he decomposes it into the four parts, and the decomposition gives him a design he can build on.

![The four-part flow Marcus uses for his weekly status email: input feeds the tool, the tool produces the output, and the review checks the output before it ships, with the review feeding back to the next run's input.](</diagrams/decomposing-a-task-into-a-repeatable-flow-input-tool-output-review/1-four-part-flow.png>)

**Input.** The week's numbers from his program tracker, the list of what happened that week, and the saved instruction that says what the email must include and how long it must be. Marcus gathers these on Monday morning. The input is the contract: if he has the numbers and the list, he can run the workflow. If he does not, he cannot, and he knows it before he starts.

**Tool.** His chat tool with the saved instruction. He pastes the numbers and the list, and the tool drafts the email. The tool is where the setup lives. The saved instruction is the part he will build properly in Lesson 4, but for now he can name it, which is enough to design around.

**Output.** A draft status email, under 150 words, with the sections his director expects: what happened this week, what is at risk, and what needs a decision. Marcus knows what the output should look like before he runs the workflow, because he defined it. That is the measurable output Adobe's guidance asks for. [^1]

**Review.** He reads the draft against the numbers he pulled and his own notes. He checks the numbers are right, the tone is right, and nothing urgent is missing. He fixes anything the tool got wrong, and only then sends. This is where his judgment stays, and it is the part that makes the workflow safe. [^2]

![Marcus's weekly status email decomposed into its four parts, with the concrete input, tool, output, and review for his task in each box.](</diagrams/decomposing-a-task-into-a-repeatable-flow-input-tool-output-review/2-marcus-decomposition.png>)

The decomposition gives Marcus something he did not have before: a named contract. He knows exactly what to gather, the input. He knows what to run, the tool. He knows what to expect, the output. He knows what to check, the review. Each repetition after the first is cheaper, because the setup does the re-deciding and the review catches the mistakes. He has not built anything yet. He has designed the thing he will build, which is the whole job of this lesson.

## Where the setup lives and where your judgment stays

The four parts split into two kinds of work, and seeing the split is the point of the whole lesson. The tool carries the repeatable parts: the saved instruction, the fixed order, the formatting, the phrasing. The review carries the judgment: whether the output is right, whether it fits the situation, whether it is safe to send.

![The split between setup and judgment: the tool carries the repeatable work, the saved instruction and fixed order, while the review keeps the human in the loop, deciding whether the output is right before it ships.](</diagrams/decomposing-a-task-into-a-repeatable-flow-input-tool-output-review/3-setup-vs-judgment.png>)

You want the tool to carry as much of the repeatable work as it can, because that is where the time is saved. Every instruction you save, every order you fix, every format you set is a decision you do not have to remake. You want the review to stay with you, because that is where the accountability lives. Lesson 1 made the point about ownership: you answer for the work that leaves your hands. The review is how you answer for it.

A common mistake is to skip the review because the tool is fast and confident. That is exactly when the review matters most. The tool is not wrong often, but when it is wrong, it is wrong confidently, and the cost of a wrong output that ships is higher than the cost of a review that catches it. [^2] The review is not the slow part of the workflow. It is the part that makes the fast part worth trusting.

## Try it

Take the task you flagged in Lesson 2, the frequent, same-shaped, low-risk one, and decompose it into the four parts. This takes about fifteen minutes and produces the design you will build in Lesson 4.

First, on a blank note, write the four parts and fill them in for your task:

1. **Input.** What do you feed in? The numbers, the notes, the list, the document. Name it specifically enough that you know what to gather before you run the workflow.
2. **Tool.** What does the work? For now, name the tool you already use and the saved instruction you would give it. You will build this properly in Lesson 4.
3. **Output.** What comes out? Describe it specifically and measurably: the format, the length, the sections. Adobe's guidance is to start with the output and work back. [^1]
4. **Review.** What do you check before the output leaves your hands? Name the specific checks: the numbers, the tone, the missing items. This is where your judgment stays.

Second, get a second opinion with a tool. Paste this into any chat tool you already use, with your four-part note:

> Here is a task I do repeatedly at work: [your task]. I have decomposed it into four parts: input, tool, output, review. Here is my draft for each: [your four parts]. For each part, tell me what is missing or underspecified. In particular: is my input specific enough to gather every time, is my output described measurably enough that I would know a good result from a bad one, and are my review checks the right ones to catch the mistakes this task is prone to? Do not build the workflow yet. Just sharpen the four parts.

Read the answer the way the AI at Work course taught you. The tool is a helpful colleague with good instincts and no idea how your week actually runs. Its suggestions are a starting point for your own judgment, not a verdict. [^3] What you are walking away with is a sharpened four-part design, the input, tool, output, and review, that Lesson 4 will turn into a custom tool.

## What's next

You have now decomposed your task into the four parts every workflow needs: input, tool, output, and review. You know what to gather, what to run, what to expect, and what to check. The next step is building the part that does the work. Lesson 4 is about building a custom tool: tailored instructions, saved context, your own project. It takes the tool part of your decomposition and turns it into something you control, with instructions written for your task, context saved so you do not re-explain it every time, and a project of your own that holds the whole setup together. [^1]

That is the bridge between decomposing a task and owning it. You cannot build a tool for a task you have not decomposed, and you cannot own a workflow you have not built. Come to Lesson 4 with the four-part design you just sharpened, and you will turn the tool part into something you actually control.

For now, take the fifteen minutes. Fill in the four parts for your task, sharpen them with a second opinion, and write down the review checks you will run. That single act, naming the parts of your own work instead of doing it as one blurry whole, is what turns a task into a flow you can build on. [^1] [^2]

*Note: decomposition is a way of seeing your own work, not a feature of one vendor. Tools and their limits change over time, and your company may have its own data and acceptable-use policies; treat those as the binding rules and verify any tool's current behavior against the vendor's own documentation.*
