Every lesson so far has taught you one part of the pattern: decompose a task, write a template, wire a connector, test a run, document a workflow. Lesson 26 gave you the last piece of that thread, the library that makes every build cheaper than the last. [^15] Now the course changes shape. For the next four lessons you stop practicing on exercises and build one complete workflow of your own, from the first decision to the day it runs on its own. That is the capstone.

This lesson is Capstone A, and its job is narrower than it sounds. You are not building anything yet. You are making the two decisions that decide whether the build succeeds before you touch a tool: which workflow to take on, and what exactly you are committing to. Choose the wrong workflow and you spend four lessons fighting a project that was never going to finish. Choose the right one and skip the scope, and you build something that works but does the wrong thing. Both failures happen before any real work begins, which is why this lesson exists.

## Define the terms

A capstone is a final project that requires you to use everything you have learned, on something real rather than something invented for practice. The word comes from architecture, where the capstone is the final stone placed at the top of a wall that locks all the other stones into place. That is the right image for this course. The capstone does not add a new technique. It makes the techniques you already have hold together, in one workflow that you run.

An end-to-end workflow is a workflow that carries a whole job from its trigger to its finished output, with every step in between defined and connected. You met the shape in Lesson 3, where a repeatable flow breaks into input, tool, output, and review, and again in Lesson 15, where those pieces assemble into a multi-step pipeline. [^4] [^11] The phrase end-to-end matters because it is the difference between a workflow and a fragment. A fragment is a good prompt that does one small thing. An end-to-end workflow starts when the job starts and finishes when the job is done, and nothing you care about falls through the gaps between steps.

![A fragment is a good prompt that does one small thing, with no defined start, no connected steps, and no finished output it owns. An end-to-end workflow carries a whole job from its trigger to its finished output, with every step in between defined and connected, so nothing you care about falls through the gaps between steps.](</diagrams/capstone-a-choose-and-scope-your-own-end-to-end-workflow/5-fragment-vs-end-to-end.png>)

Scope is the boundary of a project: what it includes, what it excludes, and what done looks like. Scoping is the act of writing that boundary down before you build. The word comes from the same root as microscope and telescope, the instrument that lets you see exactly how big (or small) something is. A scoped workflow is one you can see the edges of. A fuzzy workflow is one whose edges keep moving while you build it.

A definition of done is the checklist that tells you a piece of work is actually finished, as opposed to finished enough to stop. The term comes from Scrum, and the 2020 Scrum Guide defines it as a formal description of the state of work when it meets the quality measures required for the product. [^1] The same guide draws the sharp line that matters here: if work does not meet the definition of done, it is not done, and it cannot be presented as done. [^1] For a workflow, your definition of done is the list of checks a run must pass before you trust it. You will write one in this lesson, and Capstone B will build against it.

## Why choosing and scoping come first

There is a temptation at the start of any final project to open a tool and start building, because building feels like progress. The lesson from every failed automation you can name is that the failure was decided before the first prompt. Two failure patterns cover almost all of them.

The first is aiming at the wrong target. You pick a task because it is visible, or because a colleague mentioned it, or because it sounds impressive to automate, and only later discover that it changes shape every run, or that you do not actually control its inputs, or that it happens twice a year and the setup will never pay back. Lesson 2 warned you about this at the start of the course: memory flatters, and the tasks worth automating are the quiet, same-shaped, recurring ones, not the visible ones. [^3] Lesson 8 made the opposite half of the point: some things are genuinely better off done by hand, as one-offs, and forcing them into a workflow is how you get a slow, fragile version of a quick manual task. [^7] The choice of target is not a minor pre-step. It is the main decision of the capstone.

The second failure pattern is building the right target with no boundary. You know what the workflow should do in your head, so you build, and then the edges drift: the manager asks for one more column, you decide to also send the file to finance, you add a second source that arrives in a different format, and the workflow that was supposed to save a day now costs a weekend. This is scope creep without the word scope ever being spoken. A written scope stops it because a boundary that exists on paper is a boundary you can honor, and a boundary you can point to when someone asks for one more thing.

![Two failure patterns decide a capstone before you build: aiming at the wrong target (a task that changes shape, has inputs you do not control, or recurs too rarely to pay back) and building the right target with no boundary (edges that drift as the manager asks for one more column, the file goes to finance, or a second source arrives). The five selection tests guard the first; a one-page seven-part scope guards the second.](</diagrams/capstone-a-choose-and-scope-your-own-end-to-end-workflow/1-two-failure-patterns.png>)

Here is the honest framing. Choosing and scoping feel like the slow part, and they are, and that is exactly why they are worth it. A good scope turns "automate my monthly reporting" into "when the export lands in the folder, normalize it, draft the digest, and flag what needs my eyes," which is a sentence you can build from. The definition of done turns "it works" into a checklist you can test, which is the finish line Capstone B needs and the quality bar you learned to set in Lesson 18. [^12]

## The five selection tests

You are looking for one workflow to take through the rest of the capstone. Run every candidate through five tests. A candidate that fails even one of the first four is out. The fifth is a tiebreaker.

![The five selection tests screen a capstone candidate in order: it is real (run at least monthly), it is same-shaped (same inputs, steps, and output every run), it is worth the build (the setup pays back), and it is medium (three to six connected steps, real input and output contracts, a human review gate). Failing any of the first four is out. The fifth, you care, is a tiebreaker between candidates that pass.](</diagrams/capstone-a-choose-and-scope-your-own-end-to-end-workflow/2-five-selection-tests.png>)

**Test one: it is real.** You actually run this task, on a real schedule, for real work, at least monthly. Not a task you might do someday. Not a hypothetical improvement. A capstone workflow must survive the course, and a task you do not actually have will not. This test is what separates the capstone from every earlier exercise: the lesson exercises taught you the pattern on invented jobs, and the capstone demands a job that is already yours.

**Test two: it is same-shaped.** The task has the same inputs, the same steps, and the same output format every time it runs, with only the details varying. This is the audit test from Lesson 2 restated: same-shaped tasks are where a saved setup pays for itself, because every run after the first is cheaper. [^3] A task where the shape itself changes, where you decide each time what to include and how to frame it, is a judgment task, and the course has been honest since Lesson 8 that judgment tasks are the last ones to automate, not the first. [^7]

**Test three: it is worth the build.** The task recurs often enough, or takes long enough per run, that the setup pays back. Rough math is fine: if the task costs you two hours a month and the build will take six hours of your attention across the capstone, the payback is three months, which is real. If the task costs you ten minutes a month, the payback is years, and the workflow will be retired before it pays for itself. The cost and limits lesson made the same point about token budgets and rate limits, that you spend to get back more than you spend. [^14] A capstone workflow should earn its keep on a schedule you can point to.

**Test four: it is medium.** This is the capstone-specific test. The workflow must be small enough to finish within the remaining lessons, and large enough to exercise several pieces of your library. A one-prompt job, a single call to a tool that drafts an email, is too small: there is nothing to assemble, and you will not practice the pattern. A workflow that needs data from four systems, two approvals, and a forecast that your manager will present to the board is too big: it will not finish, and the stakes make it the wrong place to learn. Medium is three to six connected steps, at least one real input contract and one real output contract, and at least one human review gate. You should be able to say "I assemble this from pieces I already own" and mean most of it.

**Test five: you care.** This one is a tiebreaker, but it is not soft. Between two candidates that pass the first four tests, pick the one you would actually want to run next month. The mental-model lesson said it plainly: you only own the workflows you are willing to maintain, and maintenance is the price of ownership. [^14] A workflow you chose because it matters to you will get the fixes, the versioning, and the runbook entries that keep it alive. A workflow you chose because it was impressive will be a post-mortem waiting for a month when you are busy.

Here is the same set of tests phrased as the questions to ask while you screen candidates. Is this a real job I actually run? Does it come in the same shape every time? Does the setup pay back on a real schedule? Can I finish it in the capstone, with assembly instead of heroics? And do I actually want to be running this next quarter? Four yeses and one strong feeling, and you have your capstone.

## The seven-part scope

Once the target survives the tests, you write the scope. Keep it to one page. If it will not fit on one page, the workflow is too big, which is test four failing late. Seven sections, in this order.

![The seven-part scope, written in order on one page: the one-sentence job, the trigger, the inputs, the steps, the output, the boundaries, and the definition of done. About three hundred words, the cheapest insurance the capstone offers.](</diagrams/capstone-a-choose-and-scope-your-own-end-to-end-workflow/3-seven-part-scope.png>)

**One: the one-sentence job.** What the workflow does, in one sentence with the trigger and the output in it. "When the month closes, produce the vendor risk digest for my manager." If you cannot write this sentence, you do not yet know what you are building. The SMART criteria, the goal-setting framework from project management, make the same demand of objectives generally: specific and measurable beats vague and ambitious. [^2]

**Two: the trigger.** What starts a run. A schedule, a file landing in a folder, a manual start you click, an event from another tool. Lesson 12 covered the options: cadence, batching, queues, event hooks. [^9] Name exactly one trigger for the first version. More than one trigger is a scope increase, and you can add it after the workflow ships.

**Three: the inputs.** Everything the workflow consumes, and the exact form it arrives in. A file name, a set of columns, a folder, a source system. The structured-output lesson is the mirror image of this: your inputs are someone else's outputs, and the cleaner the contract on both sides, the fewer surprises in the middle. [^5] If an input is not same-shaped, the workflow is not same-shaped, and you are back to test two.

**Four: the steps.** The ordered pipeline, in the input, tool, output, review shape from Lesson 3, assembled the way Lesson 15 showed. [^4] [^11] Name each step in one line, and mark which steps are tool steps and which are human steps. The human steps are your review gates from Lesson 13, the checkpoints you build in, not bolt on. [^10]

**Five: the output.** The finished artifact, in its exact shape. A document, a message, a spreadsheet, a set of rows, all with the format spelled out. This is your output contract, the structured-output discipline applied to the whole workflow. [^5] If you can describe the output precisely, you can test the workflow precisely, which is what test four of the quality-bar lesson demands. [^12]

**Six: the boundaries.** What is explicitly out of scope. No auto-sending to other people. No pulling from a second source yet. No changing the data, only reporting it. Boundaries are the section that prevents scope creep, and they are also the section that keeps the workflow honest about what it does not do, which is the sunsetting discipline from Lesson 20 applied at the start instead of the end. [^17]

**Seven: the definition of done.** The checklist a completed run must pass. Every input validated. Every source row accounted for. Every flag from the source present in the output. The review gate signed off. The output saved where the trigger says. The runbook updated if anything changed. Lesson 10's testing discipline gives you the frame: before the first real run, a dry run must pass against a fake or past input. [^8] Write that into the definition of done too.

A scope in seven parts looks like a lot of writing. It is about three hundred words. That is the cheapest insurance the capstone offers, because every wrong guess you catch on paper costs minutes, and every wrong guess you catch in the build costs hours.

## Worked example: Priya chooses and scopes her capstone

Priya is back from Lesson 26, where she extracted her library from four workflows: the weekly vendor summary, a client-facing report, a meeting-notes digest, and a research brief. [^15] Her library holds a structured-output contract, two parameterized templates, a shared house-style instruction, and the tested connectors, all proven by workflows that run. She arrives at the capstone with three candidates and screens them.

Her first idea is a one-prompt job: "summarize my inbox every morning and tell me what needs me." It fails test four instantly. There is one step and no assembly, no input contract, no review gate worth the name. It would be a fine fragment to reuse, but it is not a capstone.

Her second idea is a quarterly forecast pack: pull the last four quarters of spend, delivery, and staffing data from four systems, produce a twelve-page forecast with scenarios for the leadership team. It fails test four from the other side. It needs senior judgment she does not fully control, the stakes are real (the numbers go to the board), and finishing it inside the capstone would require heroics, which is exactly what test four forbids. [^7]

Her third idea passes. Every month, after the vendor summary runs, Priya hand-builds a one-page vendor risk digest for her manager: which vendors are slipping on delivery, which have open quality flags, and which three deserve attention this month. She has done it by hand for a year. It is real, it recurs monthly, and it is the same shape every time: same sources, same table, same three priority notes. The build will assemble from pieces she already owns: the vendor data contract, the house style, the review habits she built in Lesson 13. [^10] [^5] And she cares, because month-end currently eats her evening. Four yeses and one strong feeling.

So Priya writes the scope. Here it is, all seven parts, exactly as it would sit on one page.

Job. When the month closes, produce the one-page vendor risk digest for my manager, in the shared drive, on the second business day.

Trigger. The second business day of the month, at 9am. Manual start allowed, because the calendar should never be the bottleneck for a job that can wait a day.

Inputs. The month's vendor delivery export (CSV, columns: vendor, on-time percent, late deliveries, notes), the quality-flag file from the support queue (CSV, columns: vendor, issue, opened date, status), and the accepted vendor list (CSV, column: vendor name). All three are dropped into the vendor-digest-monthly folder before the trigger fires.

Steps. One, validate: all three files present, and the flag file's vendor names all appear on the accepted list. Two, normalize: map both sources into the vendor contract shape from her library. [^5] Three, draft: run the parameterized digest template with the month filled in, applying the house style. [^6] Four, review (human): Priya reads the draft, spot-checks every flagged vendor against the source file, and edits the priority notes. [^10] Five, deliver: save the one-pager to the shared drive with the dated filename and send the link to her manager.

Output. A one-page markdown digest: a two-line summary, a table of flagged vendors (vendor, issue, severity, status), at most three priority notes, and a review date. No columns beyond the contract. [^5]

Boundaries. No auto-send to anyone besides the manager link. No pulling from the vendor portal API; the manual export stays for now. No forecasting. No changes to the accepted vendor list; updates to that list stay a manual process. No remediation actions, only flags.

Definition of done. All three files validated and every vendor on the flag file matched to the accepted list. The count of flagged rows in the digest equals the count in the source. Priya reviewed and approved the draft. The digest is saved with the dated filename in the shared drive. The runbook entry for the workflow exists or is updated. [^13] And a dry run against last month's files passed before the first real run. [^8]

![Priya's vendor risk digest runs five steps: validate (all three files present, flag vendors on the accepted list), normalize (map both sources into the vendor contract shape), draft (run the parameterized template with the month filled in), review (a human gate where Priya spot-checks flagged vendors and edits priority notes), and deliver (save the one-pager with the dated filename and send the link). Three silent failures each die on a check in the definition of done: a stale file on validate and the dated filename, a dropped flag on the row-count check, and a threshold typo on Priya's review step.](</diagrams/capstone-a-choose-and-scope-your-own-end-to-end-workflow/4-priya-vendor-digest.png>)

Then Priya does one more pass that does not fit in a template: she asks what could look fine and still be wrong. The silent-error lesson taught her the failure class where everything reports success and the output is quietly wrong. [^16] For this workflow, three silent failures come to mind. A stale file: last month's export drops in the folder by accident, and the digest reports on the wrong month. A dropped flag: one flagged vendor from the source never makes it into the table, and nobody notices because nothing errors. A threshold typo: the template is filled with the wrong month or the wrong severity cut, and the digest quietly reframes the whole month. Notice what each one maps to in her definition of done. The stale file dies on validate and on the dated filename. The dropped flag dies on the row-count check. The threshold typo dies on Priya's review step, because she reads the priority notes against the source every time. That is the whole point of writing the failure test down at the scoping stage: it turns "let's be careful" into three specific checks, and it costs nothing to write.

## Try it

Give yourself an hour and do what Priya did. You should have your library from Lesson 26 and at least two candidate ideas, because Lesson 26 told you to come here with ideas. [^15] If you only have one candidate, that is fine; the tests will tell you whether it earns the capstone or whether you need to dig for a better one.

Run the screening first: take each candidate through the five tests and write a verdict sentence for each one. Then write the scope for the one that survives, all seven parts, one page. Then run the failure pass: list three ways the completed workflow could look fine and still be wrong, and check that your definition of done catches each one. If a failure has no check, add the check.

Below is a try-it prompt that runs the whole exercise with you. The trap in this lesson is choosing an impressive workflow over an owned one, so point the tool at that specific risk and make it argue with you.

> You are helping me choose and scope my capstone workflow. I will describe two or three candidate tasks from my real work, plus the library of reusable pieces I have already built (templates, output contracts, house style, connectors). Be critical. First, screen each candidate against five tests: is it a real task I actually run at least monthly; does it have the same shape every time; does the setup pay back on a real schedule; is it medium sized, with three to six connected steps, a real input contract, an output contract, and a human review gate, rather than a one-prompt job or a project too big to finish; and is it a workflow I actually want to own and maintain. Tell me which candidate wins and why, and push back hard if I am choosing an impressive workflow over one I will actually run. Second, for the winning candidate, draft the seven-part scope: one-sentence job, trigger, inputs in their exact form, the ordered steps with tool steps and human review steps marked, the output in its exact shape, explicit boundaries of what is out of scope, and a definition of done checklist. Third, attack my scope: hunt for anything ambiguous, any missing input, any out-of-scope task that crept back in, and three ways the finished workflow could look fine and still be wrong, making sure my definition of done catches each one. Finish by showing me the final one-page scope I can build from.

Run that, read the scope out loud, and ask the one question that closes the lesson: would I trust this page to tell someone else exactly what to build? If the answer is yes, you have finished Capstone A, because the point of choosing and scoping is to make the build a matter of assembly rather than a matter of guesswork.

## What's next

You have made the two decisions that decide whether the capstone succeeds: the workflow you will own, and the scope that draws its edges. The scope is not homework. It is the specification that everything from here on builds against.

The next lesson is Capstone B: build it (design and assemble). Where this lesson chose the target and wrote the boundary, Lesson 28 takes your scope and turns it into a running workflow. You will design the pipeline from your seven-part scope, wire the steps together, assemble the build from the library pieces you extracted in Lesson 26, and run the first dry test against your definition of done.
