You finished Capstone A with a one-page scope and a definition of done. That page is the specification for everything in this lesson. The trap now is to drop it and start building from memory, the way most builds start and the way most of them drift. Lesson 27 warned you that the two decisions that decide a capstone happen before you build. [^16] This lesson makes good on that: you design the workflow from the scope, pull the pieces from your library, and assemble them so the build is a matter of fitting known parts into a known frame, not improvising.

This is Capstone B, and its job is to take your written scope and turn it into a workflow that runs end to end, at least once, against a fake or past input. You are not hardening it yet. You are not wiring it into a schedule yet. You are proving that the pieces can carry the job from trigger to output and pass the checks in your definition of done. Capstone C will take what you build here and make it durable. You cannot harden a workflow that does not exist, so the whole point of this lesson is to get yours assembled and moving.

## Define the terms

Assembly is the act of putting an automation together from pieces that already exist, rather than building it from scratch each time. The word comes from manufacturing, where a product is assembled from parts that were made separately and are known to fit together. That is the right image for a capstone. Your library from Lesson 26 holds the reusable blocks: parameterized templates, structured-output contracts, house-style instructions, tested connectors. [^14] Assembly means you spend the build wiring those pieces into your workflow instead of re-authoring them. Every piece you reuse is a piece you have already tested, which is why assembly is faster and more reliable than authoring.

A pipeline is the ordered sequence of steps that carries the job from input to output, where each step's output becomes the next step's input. Lesson 15 showed you how individual pieces connect into a multi-step pipeline. [^10] Your scope already names these steps in order. Your job in this lesson is to fill in the details that make them connect: what exactly passes between them, and in what shape. The pipeline is the skeleton; the pieces are the muscle; the structured-output contract is the connective tissue that keeps one step's output readable by the next. [^4]

A dry run is a test of the workflow against a fake or past input, before you trust it with real data. Lesson 10 established this as the discipline: you prove a workflow before it runs on real information. [^6] For the capstone, the dry run is the moment of truth for assembly. It answers the one question that matters: does the assembled workflow carry a real job from trigger to output without a piece failing or a wire coming loose? The dry test uses your definition of done as the checklist, exactly as a sample that leaves the factory floor is checked against the specification before it is shipped.

## Why assembly comes before hardening

There is a strong pull to build the workflow and immediately start polishing it: add the error handling, wire the calendar, make the output prettier, handle every edge case you can imagine. That pull is backwards. You cannot meaningfully harden a workflow that has never produced one output. The reliability lessons in this course, the review gates in Lesson 13, the testing discipline in Lesson 10, the silent-error awareness in Lesson 11, all of them act on a running pipeline. [^9] [^6] [^8] Build something that runs first, then make it strong. Capstone C is where the hardening happens, and it is only possible because Capstone B produced a working assembly.

The other reason assembly comes first is that it is where you discover what your scope missed. When you write a scope on paper, you describe the steps at the level of intention: "validate the files," "normalize the sources." The moment you actually connect the pieces you discover the texture of those intentions. The flag file arrives with an extra column you did not note. The house-style instruction expects a vendor name, but one source spells it "vendorname." The template wants a month, and you realize the file names carry the month but the data inside does not. None of these are failures. They are the normal friction of turning a plan into a working thing, and you want to find them in assembly, in the dry run, where they are cheap, not in the first real run, where they are not. [^6]

Here is the simplifying fact that makes the build tractable. You already decided, in Capstone A, which workflow to build and how big it should be: three to six connected steps, real input and output contracts, one human review gate, built from pieces you mostly own. [^16] The medium test was not an excuse to keep the capstone small. It was the decision that makes this lesson finishable. A medium workflow assembles in an hour. A huge one does not fit in a course, which is exactly why test four exists.

## Design the pipeline from the scope

Start with the scope's steps section. It already names the ordered pipeline. Your job is to turn each named step into a concrete, executable instruction: what it does, what it consumes, what it produces, and which piece from your library it uses. Do this on paper or in a note before you touch a tool. This is the design step, and it is where you catch most of the scope's unstated assumptions cheaply.

Work the steps in order, and for each one write three lines. First, the input: exactly what this step receives from the previous step, in its shape. Second, the action: what the step does to that input. Third, the output: exactly what this step passes to the next step, in its shape. The output of one step is the input of the next, so writing them down together is how you catch mismatches before you wire anything. A step whose input does not match the previous step's output is a seam you will discover now rather than in a broken run.

![Each step gets three lines, input, action, and output, and the seam between steps is where a mismatch between one step's output and the next step's input surfaces on paper before you wire anything](</diagrams/capstone-b-build-it-design-and-assemble/1-three-line-design-method.png>)

Use your structured-output contract to pin the shapes. Lesson 6 defined the contract: getting data back in a format downstream tools can consume. [^4] In assembly, the contract does double duty. It tells each tool step the exact field names and shapes to emit, and it tells you, when a run fails, whether the failure is in the logic of a step or in the seam between steps. The clearest signal of a wiring problem is a step that receives data it cannot read. If you design the seams up front, that signal is reliable.

The human review gate is a step like any other, with one difference: the action is human judgment, not a tool call. Lesson 13 defined this as a checkpoint built into the workflow, not bolted on after. [^9] Design it as a deliberate pause between the draft step and the deliver step, with a specific thing the human checks. "Priya reads the draft and checks the flagged vendors" is a review gate. "Priya glances at it" is a habit. Name the check, because the definition of done will test it.

## Assemble from the library

Now you build. Assemble in dependency order, not in the order the workflow runs. First the pieces that need no other piece: the structured-output contract, the house-style instruction. Then the parameterized template, which depends on both, because the template fills slots that the contract names. Then the connector steps, which move data between the pieces. Wire the smallest meaningful slice first, ideally the first two steps, and run them against real input before you connect the rest. This is the vertical-slice discipline from Lesson 15: get one complete thread working end to end, then expand. [^10]

![Assemble in dependency order, not run order: build the contract and house-style instruction first, then the template that depends on them, then the connectors, and wire the smallest slice before expanding](</diagrams/capstone-b-build-it-design-and-assemble/2-assembly-dependency-order.png>)

You are looking for one complete path from trigger to output that passes, then you add coverage. The fastest way to build a workflow is to get a thin but complete version running first: every step present, every seam connected, one real input through the whole thing, and an output at the end. Once that moves, every further change is an improvement to working machinery instead of a change to a half-built machine, which is far easier to reason about and far less likely to scatter breakage.

Here is what reusing pieces actually looks like, and why it is not the same as rebuilding them. The template from your library has slots for vendor name, month, severity, and status. Your workflow fills those slots with data from the normalize step. You do not rewrite the template; you map the connector output into its input shape. The house-style instruction sits in the draft step's context so every draft matches your voice without you re-pasting the rules. The structured-output contract you extract in Lesson 6 becomes the schema the draft step produces and the review step reads. [^14] [^4] The build is the act of mapping inputs to slots, not re-authoring the pieces.

## Worked example: Priya assembles her vendor risk digest

Priya is building the workflow she scoped in Capstone A: the one-page vendor risk digest she delivers to her manager on the second business day of each month. [^16] Her scope named five steps in order: validate the three input files, normalize both sources into the vendor contract shape, draft the digest with the parameterized template and house style, review the draft (human), and deliver the one-pager to the shared drive with the dated filename. Her definition of done has five checks: all three files validated, every flagged vendor matched to the accepted list, the flagged-row count in the digest equals the count in the source, Priya reviewed and approved the draft, and a dry run against last month's files passed before the first real run. [^16]

![Priya's five-step vendor risk digest runs validate, normalize, draft, review as a human gate, and deliver, and the definition of done checks all five outcomes before the workflow is trusted with real data](</diagrams/capstone-b-build-it-design-and-assemble/3-priya-five-step-pipeline.png>)

Priya starts with design, writing the three lines for each step in a note. For validate, the input is three files in the vendor-digest-monthly folder, the action is "check all three present, and every flag-file vendor appears on the accepted list," and the output is "a pass signal and the three file paths." For normalize, the input is the three file paths, the action is "map the delivery export and the flag file into the vendor contract shape," and the output is "one normalized table with the contract's fields." For draft, the input is the normalized table, the action is "fill the parameterized template with the month and run it with the house style in context," and the output is a draft markdown digest. For review, the input is the draft, the action is "spot-check every flagged vendor against the source and edit the priority notes," and the output is an approved draft. For deliver, the input is the approved draft, the action is "save with the dated filename to the shared drive and send the manager the link," and the output is the saved file and sent link.

Writing these five blocks out reveals the design decisions she had not made in the scope. The normalize step must handle the fact that the delivery export and the flag file spell vendor names differently, one uses "Vendor Inc" and the other uses "VendorInc," so the mapping is not a plain copy. The draft step needs the month as a fill value, and the scope told her the month is available in the trigger and the file names, but not that it is absent from the data itself, so she notes to pass it as a parameter. The review step needs her to compare the draft to a source, which means the source paths must survive through normalization, not be discarded. None of these appear in the scope. All of them surface in the design pass, and she fixes all of them on paper before she touches a tool, which is precisely the point of design.

Then Priya assembles in dependency order. She pulls her vendor structured-output contract and sets up the normalize step to emit exactly those fields. She opens her parameterized digest template and confirms the slot names match the contract fields, adjusting the mapping where the flag file calls a field "issue" and her template calls it "description." She puts the house-style instruction into the draft step's context so the digest keeps her voice. She wires validate to normalize first, runs it against last month's real files using the connector she proved in Lesson 26, and reads the normalized output to confirm the vendor names match and every flagged vendor survived. [^14] Only then does she connect draft, review, and deliver.

The dry run is where the assembly proves itself. Priya runs the whole workflow against last month's files, the fake-or-past input her definition of done requires. [^6] In the first dry run, the deliver step fails: the dated filename uses the file's last-modified date instead of the current month, because she had configured the filename date from the input folder rather than the run date. It is a one-line fix in the deliver step's instruction, and she makes it and re-runs. The second dry run passes every check in her definition of done: all three files validated, every flagged vendor matched, the flagged row count in the digest equals the count in the source, her review gate saw her spot-check the priority notes, and the dry run itself passed. [^16]

![The dry run is where assembly proves itself: the first run fails on the dated filename, a one-line fix in the deliver step, and the second run passes every definition-of-done check](</diagrams/capstone-b-build-it-design-and-assemble/4-dry-run-catches-wiring-mistake.png>)

Priya has now done the thing Capstone B exists to do. She has a workflow that carries the whole job from trigger to output, assembled from pieces she already owned and trusted, passing the checks she wrote before she built. It has no schedule yet, no error handling worth the name, and she is not sure what happens if a source file is missing on a real morning. All of that is Capstone C's job. What she has is a running pipeline, which is the only thing you can harden.

## Try it

Give yourself an hour and do what Priya did, in the three passes that kept her build from stalling.

First, design from your scope. Take the steps section of the page you wrote in Capstone A and write, for each step, the three lines: the input, the action, the output. Work in order, and the moment you see a step whose output does not match the next step's input, fix it on paper then, because that is where it is cheap. Write at least one unstated assumption you caught into a note, because finding them is the whole value of this pass.

Second, assemble from your library. Pull your reusable pieces from Lesson 26: the structured-output contract, the parameterized templates, the house-style instruction, the tested connectors. [^14] Wire the seams between them first, run the smallest meaningful slice against real input, and expand. Every time you are tempted to rewrite a piece instead of reusing it, ask whether you are improving the piece or just re-authoring it from memory. Reuse is the point.

Third, run the dry test. Run the assembled workflow against a fake or a past input, and check the output against every line of your definition of done. Do not declare success because it produced an output. Declare success when the output passes the checks. Fix what fails, re-run, and repeat until the definition of done is satisfied or you have hit a real design problem, in which case you fix the design (rarely) or you have learned the workflow was bigger than you thought (common, and fine, Capstone C helps there).

Below is a try-it prompt that runs the whole build with you. The trap in this lesson is treating a persuasive-looking output as proof, so point the tool at that specific risk and make it check your work against your own checklist.

> You are helping me assemble the capstone workflow I scoped. I will paste my one-page scope, including the steps and the definition of done I wrote in Capstone A, and I will describe the reusable pieces in my automation library (my parameterized templates, structured-output contract, house-style instruction, and tested connectors). Be rigorous, not encouraging. First, take my scope's steps section and turn each step into an input, an action, and an output, working in order. Stop me on every seam where one step's output will not cleanly feed the next step's input, and surface at least three unstated assumptions my scope made that will bite me during assembly. Second, tell me which library piece does the work in each step, and where I can reuse a piece instead of re-authoring it. Third, before I run any tool, list the five most likely places this assembled workflow will fail on its first dry run, and what the definition of done check would catch or miss for each. Do not let me call it done because it looks like it worked. Only call it done when every one of my definition-of-done checks passes.

Run that, then build. When the dry test passes every check in your definition of done, stop. Do not start polishing, do not start wiring the calendar, do not add edge cases. You have built the pipeline, and that is the entire deliverable of Capstone B. The polishing is next.
