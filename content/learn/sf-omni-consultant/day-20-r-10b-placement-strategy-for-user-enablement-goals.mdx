You can build a FlexCard that is genuinely beautiful and still have it be useless. It pulls the right data, renders the right fields, offers the right actions, and nobody touches it, because it sits somewhere the people it was built for never look. That is the failure this lesson exists to stop. Requirement r-10b from the FlexCards domain, worth 18% of the Salesforce OmniStudio Consultant exam, asks you to prescribe the optimal placement and methods for embedding FlexCards within Salesforce, such as a component, a tab, or a console view, to meet strategic user enablement goals. The exam is not checking whether you can drag a card onto a page. It is checking whether you can reason about placement as a strategy: given a goal about what a user should be able to do more easily, where should the card live so that goal is actually met.

Here is the core idea, and it is worth holding onto for the whole lesson. Placement starts with the enablement goal, not with the card. The question you answer first is what the user should be able to do more easily. The placement is the answer to where that happens. A card placed where the user does not work, or one that does not support the goal, is wasted no matter how well it is built. That single sentence is the whole objective, and it is the lens every placement decision in this lesson should be read through.

## Deep Dive

### Placement is a strategy, not a technical step

Most consultants treat placement as the last mechanical step in building a card. You finish the layout, then you drop the card somewhere and call it done. The exam wants you to invert that order. Placement is a strategic decision that should be made with the enablement goal in mind, and it often should be made before you build the card at all, because the placement constrains what the card can be.

Think about what a FlexCard actually is. A FlexCard shows contextual information at a glance and provides access to relevant tasks for the displayed data.[^1] That definition has two halves: the information and the tasks. Both halves only make sense relative to a context. The information is contextual because it is about a specific record or a specific situation. The tasks are relevant because they are the actions a user would take on that data. If the card is placed somewhere that strips away the context, both halves collapse. The information has nothing to anchor to, and the tasks have no reason to be there.

So the enablement goal is the thing that gives placement its meaning. An enablement goal is a statement about what a user should be able to do more easily after the card exists. It is not "show a card on the account page." It is "let a service agent see the customer's open cases and launch a follow-up without leaving the case they are working." The first is a placement without a purpose. The second is a purpose that dictates a placement. The exam rewards the consultant who can state the goal first and then justify the placement as the answer to where that goal is achieved.

### The trade-off between context and reach

The placement decision is fundamentally a trade-off between context and reach. A record-page placement gives the card a strong record anchor but limits it to one record at a time. The card sits on a specific record's page, so it always knows which record it is about, and it can pull data for that record and offer actions on that record. The cost is that the card only ever sees one record at a time. It cannot give you a view across many records, because it is bound to the single record whose page it lives on.

A tab or dashboard placement reaches across records but loses the anchor. A card on a tab or a dashboard is not tied to any particular record, so it can show a list of many records, a summary across the org, or a trend. The cost is that the card has no record context handed to it. It must source its own context, which usually means the user selects a record, or the card queries broadly and lets the user filter. The card trades the certainty of a record anchor for the breadth of a cross-record view.

The goal determines which trade-off is correct. If the goal is to speed up a high-frequency task that happens on a specific record, such as resolving a case or updating an account, then the record anchor is worth more than the reach, and a record-page placement is right. If the goal is to give visibility across the org, such as showing all open opportunities or all pending approvals, then the reach is worth more than the anchor, and a tab or dashboard placement is right. There is no universally correct placement. There is only the placement that matches the goal.

### Enablement goals fall into patterns

The exam tends to describe enablement goals in recognizable patterns, and recognizing the pattern points you to the placement. Three patterns cover most scenarios.

The first pattern is a goal to speed up a high-frequency task. The user does the same task many times a day, and the card should make that task faster. This points to a console or record-page placement where the task happens. A service agent resolving cases all day works in the console, so a card that follows the selected case and offers the resolution actions belongs in the console. A sales rep updating account details works on the account record page, so a card that shows the key account data and offers the update action belongs there. The placement is where the task happens, because that is where the speed matters.

The second pattern is a goal to give visibility across the org. The user needs to see a broad picture, not work on one record. This points to a tab or dashboard placement. A manager who needs to see all open cases by priority, or all deals at risk this quarter, is not anchored to a single record. The card belongs on a tab or dashboard where it can show the cross-record view. The reach of the placement is the whole point.

The third pattern is a goal to guide a multi-step process. The user needs to be walked through a sequence of steps, which in OmniStudio means an OmniScript. The placement's job is to launch that OmniScript from the right context. A FlexCard can be the beginning point for a customer transaction, launching a guided process from the card.[^1] The card belongs where the user is when they decide to start the process, and it must carry the context the process needs. A card on a record page that launches an OmniScript to update that record is the classic example: the card is the entry point, and the placement supplies the record context the OmniScript needs to do its work.

### The card's data source and actions must match the placement's context

A placement that does not match the card's data needs produces a card that cannot do its job. This is the second major exam theme, and it is where a lot of consultants go wrong.

A record-page card relies on the record anchor for its data. The card's data source, often a Data Mapper or a SOQL query, uses the record ID as an input parameter to return data related to that record.[^2] If you take that same card and put it on a tab with no record anchor, the data source has nothing to query against. The card either shows empty data or fails outright, because the context it was built to consume is not there.

A tab card must source its own context. Because there is no record anchor, the card's data source has to be self-contained, or the user has to provide the context through a filter or a selection. A card built to show a single record's details will not work on a tab unless you rebuild its data source to handle the missing anchor.

A console card must fit a compact, task-focused space. The console is where an agent works through a queue of records, so the card has to be dense enough to be useful at a glance and light enough not to slow the agent down. A card designed for a wide dashboard, with charts and large tables, is the wrong shape for a console utility panel.

The lesson is that the placement and the card's data needs have to be consistent. When you choose a placement, you are also choosing what context the card can rely on, and the card's data source and actions have to be built for that context. Salesforce's own guidance on setting up a data source on a FlexCard makes this explicit: you configure how to retrieve the data the card shows, and you can set different data sources for child and parent FlexCards, then choose where to publish the card.[^2] The data source and the placement are decided together, not independently.

### Multiple placements can serve one goal

The same card definition can appear in more than one place, and that is often the right answer. A card that helps a service agent resolve cases can live on the record page for when the agent is deep in a single case, and in the console for when the agent is working through a queue. Both placements serve the same enablement goal, and the card reaches the user in both contexts.

When you do this, you must verify the card behaves correctly in each context, because the data and actions may need to adapt to the different placements. The record-page version can rely on the record anchor. The console version may need to follow the selected record instead, which is a different way of getting context. The actions may need to differ, because an action that makes sense on a record page might not make sense in a compact console panel. Salesforce's FlexCard capabilities describe exactly this flexibility: FlexCards are viewable on any device or channel, and you can deploy them from the Lightning App Builder to a Lightning page or from the Experience Builder to a community or portal page.[^1] The same card can be published to multiple targets, but each target is a context that has to be tested.

The exam tests this as a judgment call. If a scenario says a card should help agents both when they are on a record and when they are in the console, the right answer is to place the card in both contexts and verify it works in each, not to pick one and ignore the other. The card is a single definition, but its behavior is per-placement.

### Validate the placement against the goal after rollout

The last piece of the strategy is the review. Placement is a hypothesis about where the user acts, and it should be validated against the enablement outcome after rollout. A card placed to speed up case resolution should measurably help agents resolve cases. If it does not, the placement or the card needs adjustment.

This is easy to skip, because the card shipped and the project moved on. But the exam treats it as part of the job. The consultant does not just place the card and walk away. The consultant returns to the goal, checks whether the card is actually enabling the user, and adjusts the placement or the card if it is not. Maybe the card is in the right place but shows the wrong data, so the card needs work. Maybe the card is fine but the agents never see it because it is buried, so the placement needs work. Either way, the review is what turns placement from a guess into a validated strategy.

The quiz tests this directly. One question describes a card placed to speed up case resolution that is not helping agents after rollout, and asks what the consultant should do. The correct answer is to review whether the placement and the card support the goal, and adjust them, because placement is a hypothesis about where the user acts. Leaving it and assuming it works ignores the evidence, removing all actions and adding more data do not address the placement problem at all.

## Config Walkthrough

Apply the placement strategy with these four steps.

1. State the enablement goal in terms of the user's task. Write down what the user should be able to do more easily, and where that task happens. Be specific. "Help agents resolve cases faster" is a start, but "let an agent see the customer's open cases and launch a follow-up without leaving the case they are working" is a goal you can place against. The where in the goal is the seed of the placement.

2. Choose the placement that matches the goal. For a high-frequency task, pick a console or record-page placement where the task happens. For cross-record visibility, pick a tab or dashboard. For a guided multi-step process, pick a context that launches the OmniScript from the right place. Match the pattern of the goal to the placement that serves it.

3. Verify the card's data source and actions are consistent with the placement's context. If the placement provides a record anchor, confirm the data source uses it. If the placement has no anchor, confirm the card sources its own context. Adjust the source or the card where the placement changes what is available, and confirm the actions still make sense in the space.

4. After rollout, review whether the card supports the goal in practice. Watch how the user actually works with the card. If the card is not helping the task, adjust the placement or the card. Treat the placement as a hypothesis and validate it against the enablement outcome.

## Exam Traps

Three traps account for most misses on this objective.

First, choosing a placement before defining the enablement goal. Without a goal, the placement is arbitrary, and the card may end up where the user does not work. The exam will describe a consultant who picks a placement because it looks good or because it is easy, and the correct answer is always to define the goal first, then place the card where the task happens. The quiz tests this directly: a consultant placing a card who has not yet defined the enablement goal should first define the goal in terms of the user's task, then place the card where that task happens. Choosing the placement that looks best, placing the card in every context, and skipping the goal entirely are all wrong because they ignore the strategic purpose.

Second, placing a card in a context that does not match its data needs. A record-bound card in a tab, or a cross-record card on a single record page, cannot do its job. The exam will describe a card that needs a record anchor and ask which placement is consistent with that need. The answer is a record-page component, because it provides the anchor. A tab with no record anchor, a dashboard with no context, and a utility panel all force the card to source its own context, which a record-bound card is not built to do. Match the placement to the card's data requirements.

Third, rolling out a placement without validating it against the goal. Placement is a hypothesis about where the user acts, and if the card does not help the task, the placement or the card needs adjustment. The exam will describe a card that shipped but is not working, and the correct answer is to review whether the placement and the card support the goal and adjust them. Do not assume it works, and do not reach for a fix that does not address the placement, like removing actions or adding data.
