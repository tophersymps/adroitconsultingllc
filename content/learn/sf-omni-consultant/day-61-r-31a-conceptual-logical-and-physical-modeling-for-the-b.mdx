A team starts a new solution and jumps straight into building objects and fields. They build a beautiful data model, and it is exactly wrong, because the business never agreed on what it needed. They built the wrong thing correctly. That is the failure this lesson exists to prevent.

Requirement r-31a, part of the Best Fit Solutions domain worth 15% of the Salesforce OmniStudio Consultant exam, asks you to guide the creation of a Conceptual Model, Logical Model, and Physical Model to support the business solution, including recommending optimal object design to support OmniStudio artifacts. The exam wants you to understand that the three models are three levels of abstraction of the same solution, and that each answers a different question.

## Deep Dive

### The three models are three levels of abstraction of the same solution

The three models are three levels of abstraction of the same solution, and each answers a different question. The Conceptual Model answers what the business needs: the entities, the relationships, and the business rules, in business language with no technology. The Logical Model answers how the data is structured: the entities become records, the relationships become keys, and the rules become constraints, still independent of the platform. The Physical Model answers how it is built in Salesforce: the objects, the fields, the relationships, and the indexes that actually exist.

Think of it as zooming in. The Conceptual Model is the wide view, the business view, the view that a stakeholder can read and agree to. The Logical Model is the middle view, the data view, where the structure is decided. The Physical Model is the close view, the platform view, where the structure becomes real Salesforce objects and fields. Same solution, three levels of detail, three different questions answered.

The exam wants you to keep the three straight. The Conceptual Model is business language, no technology. The Logical Model is data structure, still no platform. The Physical Model is the Salesforce implementation. Mixing them up is a common failure, and the exam tests the distinction directly.

### The Conceptual Model is the contract with the business

The Conceptual Model is the contract with the business. It should be built with the stakeholders and expressed in their terms: a customer, an order, a claim, a policy. The architect should not jump to objects and fields at this stage, because doing so forces technology decisions before the business has agreed on what it needs. A clear conceptual model is what prevents the team from building the wrong thing correctly.

The quiz tests this directly. One question describes an architect starting a new solution where the business has not yet agreed on what it needs, and asks what should be built first. The answer is the conceptual model in business language with the stakeholders. The conceptual model is the contract with the business and should be built first in business language, because jumping to objects and fields forces technology decisions before the business agrees on what it needs. The physical model, OmniScript, and Integration Procedure all depend on that agreement.

Picture the meeting. The architect sits with the business stakeholders and asks what they need. They talk about customers, orders, and claims, in the words the business uses every day. The architect writes it down in those same words. No object names, no field types, no relationship diagrams. Just the entities, the relationships, and the business rules, agreed to by the people who own the problem. That agreement is the contract.

The trap is jumping ahead. The architect is comfortable with objects and fields, so the instinct is to start there. But that forces technology decisions before the business has agreed on what it needs. The business says "customer," and the architect decides it is an Account, and that decision is made before anyone agreed that a customer is what the solution is about. The conceptual model keeps the conversation in business language until the business has agreed on the what, and only then does the team move to the how.

### The Logical Model is where the data structure is decided

The Logical Model is where the data structure is decided. The architect takes the conceptual entities and decides which become records, how they relate (one-to-many, many-to-many), and what the key fields are. This is the stage to resolve ambiguities: is a customer the same as a contact, is an address part of an account or its own entity, is a product a single record or a hierarchy. Decisions made here determine how well the physical model supports the OmniStudio artifacts.

The quiz tests this directly. One question asks in which model the architect decides whether an address is its own entity or part of an account. The answer is the Logical Model. The Logical Model is where the data structure is decided, including whether an address is its own entity or part of an account. The Conceptual Model stays in business language without that structure. The Physical Model implements the decision already made. It is a data-structure decision, not only a business one.

The address question is a perfect example of what the Logical Model is for. In the conceptual model, the business says the solution tracks customers and their addresses. That is business language, and it is fine. In the logical model, the architect decides the structure: is an address a field on the customer record, or is it its own entity with its own record? That is a data-structure decision, and it has real consequences. If the address is its own entity, the solution can track multiple addresses per customer and history. If it is a field, it is simpler but limited. The logical model is where that decision gets made, deliberately, before the platform is involved.

The logical model is also where the relationships get decided. One-to-many, many-to-many, what the key fields are. A customer has many orders, that is one-to-many. A product can be part of many orders and an order can contain many products, that is many-to-many. These decisions determine how the data is structured, and they determine how well the physical model supports the OmniStudio artifacts that will read and write it.

### The Physical Model is the Salesforce implementation

The Physical Model is the Salesforce implementation. The architect maps the logical entities to standard or custom objects, defines the fields and their types, sets up the relationships (lookup, master-detail), and considers indexes and record types. This is where the model meets the platform's constraints: governor limits, sharing, and the capabilities of the objects chosen. The physical model must be validated against the OmniScripts and Integration Procedures that will read and write it.

This is where the abstraction becomes real. The logical entity "customer" becomes the standard Account object.[^1] The logical entity "order" becomes a custom object with fields for the order number, the date, and the total. The one-to-many relationship between them becomes a lookup or master-detail relationship field.[^2][^3] The fields get types, the relationships get set up, and the model meets the platform.

The platform has constraints, and the physical model is where they surface. Governor limits cap how much data a transaction can touch. Sharing controls who can see what. The objects chosen have capabilities and limits. A field count limit, a relationship depth limit, a record volume limit, all of these are physical-model concerns. The architect designs within them, and when a constraint is discovered, it gets raised back up to the logical and conceptual levels.

The physical model must be validated against the artifacts. An OmniScript reads and writes the physical model, an Integration Procedure transforms data between the logical and physical shapes, and a FlexCard displays the physical model's data. If the physical model is poorly designed, every artifact built on it inherits the problem. The architect should design the models before building the artifacts, because retrofitting a data model is far more expensive than designing it.

### The three models must stay aligned

The three models must stay aligned. A change in the business (conceptual) must flow through the logical and physical models, and a constraint discovered in the physical model (a field limit, a relationship limit) must be raised back to the logical and conceptual levels. The architect should treat the models as a connected set, not three separate documents, and keep them in sync as the solution evolves.

The quiz tests this directly. One question describes a platform constraint, such as a relationship limit, discovered while building the physical model, and asks what the architect should do. The answer is to raise it back to the logical and conceptual levels and adjust the design. A platform constraint discovered in the physical model must be raised back to the logical and conceptual levels so the design can adjust, keeping the models aligned. Ignoring it ships a broken model. Documenting it only in the physical model leaves the other models out of sync. A custom setting is not a substitute for a relationship.

The flow goes both directions. A business change flows down. The business decides that a customer can now have multiple addresses, so the conceptual model changes, the logical model changes the address from a field to an entity, and the physical model adds the object and relationship. A platform constraint flows up. The physical model hits a relationship limit, so the architect raises it back to the logical model, which reconsiders the structure, and to the conceptual model, which reconsiders the business rule. The models are a connected set, and they stay in sync.

The trap is treating them as three separate documents. If a business change is not flowed through the models, or a platform constraint is not raised back, the models drift out of sync with the solution. The conceptual model says one thing, the physical model does another, and the team is building on a contradiction. Keep them connected, and the solution stays coherent.

### The models are the foundation for the OmniStudio artifacts

The models are the foundation for the OmniStudio artifacts. An OmniScript reads and writes the physical model, an Integration Procedure transforms data between the logical and physical shapes, and a FlexCard displays the physical model's data. If the physical model is poorly designed, every artifact built on it inherits the problem. The architect should design the models before building the artifacts, because retrofitting a data model is far more expensive than designing it.

The order matters. Design the models first, then build the artifacts. An OmniScript that writes to an object needs the object's fields to match the script's data. An Integration Procedure that transforms data needs the object's relationships to be present. If the model is designed first, the artifacts are built on a solid foundation. If the artifacts are built first, the model is retrofitted to fit them, and retrofitting is expensive.

The exam wants you to see the models as the foundation, not an afterthought. A poorly designed physical model means every artifact built on it inherits the problem. An OmniScript that queries a non-indexed field runs slowly. An Integration Procedure that needs a relationship the model lacks cannot do its job. The foundation determines what the artifacts can do, so the foundation comes first.

## Config Walkthrough

Build the three models in four steps.

1. Build the Conceptual Model with the business. Identify the entities, relationships, and business rules in business language, with no technology terms, and get stakeholder agreement before proceeding. Sit with the stakeholders, write down what they need in their words, and get their sign-off. This is the contract with the business.

2. Resolve the data structure in the Logical Model. Decide which entities become records, how they relate, and what the key fields are, and resolve ambiguities like whether an address is its own entity. This is where the data structure is decided, still independent of the platform.

3. Map the Logical Model to the Physical Model. Choose standard or custom objects, define fields and types, set up relationships, and validate against governor limits and the OmniScripts and IPs that will use it. This is where the model meets the platform's constraints.

4. Keep the three models in sync. Flow business changes down through logical and physical, and raise platform constraints back up, so the models never drift apart. Treat the models as a connected set, not three separate documents.

## Exam Traps

Three traps dominate this objective.

First, jumping straight to objects and fields without a conceptual model. The team builds the wrong thing correctly because the business never agreed on what it needs. Build the conceptual model in business language with the stakeholders first, and get their agreement before touching objects and fields.

Second, treating the three models as separate documents. A business change that is not flowed through the models, or a platform constraint that is not raised back, leaves the models out of sync with the solution. Treat the models as a connected set and keep them in sync as the solution evolves.

Third, designing the physical model without validating it against the OmniStudio artifacts. An OmniScript that queries a non-indexed field or an IP that needs a relationship the model lacks inherits a design flaw that is expensive to fix later. Design the models before building the artifacts, and validate the physical model against the artifacts that will read and write it.
