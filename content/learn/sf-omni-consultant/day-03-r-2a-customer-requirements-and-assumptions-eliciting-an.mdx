The OmniStudio Consultant exam opens with a domain that looks like common sense and punishes candidates who treat it that way. OmniStudio Fundamentals asks you to determine a project's needs by evaluating a customer's requirements, assumptions, risks, and constraints. This lesson covers the first half of that sentence, eliciting and validating requirements and assumptions. The exam hands you a customer story and a stated requirement, then hides the real difficulty inside the assumptions the customer never says out loud. The candidate who accepts the requirement at face value picks the answer that looks right. The candidate who probes the assumption underneath it picks the answer that survives production. This lesson teaches you to do the second thing, and it gives you a repeatable method for turning a vague wish into a requirements baseline you can design against.

## Deep Dive

### Start with the business outcome, not the tool

The most common mistake in requirements work is reaching for a component before you understand the work. A customer says they need an OmniScript, or a FlexCard, or an Integration Procedure, and the temptation is to start sketching steps. That is backwards. The architect's job is to learn what the customer's users actually do, how often they do it, and what a successful step looks like, and only then decide whether an OmniScript, a FlexCard, or an Integration Procedure is even the right answer.

Consider the difference between two requests that sound identical. A customer says, "We need a form for our agents to open a new account." That could be a guided OmniScript if the account opening has several ordered steps that a person completes. It could be a FlexCard if the real need is to show an existing account summary and let the agent act on it. It could be an Integration Procedure if the work happens server-side with no user in front of it. The word "form" tells you nothing about which one fits. The business outcome tells you. If the outcome is "an agent walks a new customer through five ordered steps and the order matters," you need an OmniScript. If the outcome is "an agent sees the account context and takes one action," you need a FlexCard. The tool follows the outcome, never the other way around.

The exam tests this by giving you a requirement phrased in tool language and asking which component actually serves the need. The correct answer is the one that matches the process, not the one that matches the word the customer used. Hold that distinction and you will read scenario questions correctly.

### What elicitation actually means

Elicitation is the act of drawing requirements out of people who have not articulated them. It is not the same as collecting a list. A list is what you get when you ask someone to write down what they want. Elicitation is what you get when you walk through real work and observe what actually happens. The difference matters because users rarely think in terms of requirements. They think in terms of their job, their screens, and their frustrations.

The practical technique is the walkthrough. You sit with the customer and walk one real end-to-end scenario from start to finish. You ask what happens first, what system they touch, what data they need at each step, and what they do when something goes wrong. You capture the steps, the systems touched, and the data used at each step. That walkthrough produces a picture of the process that a wish list never will, because it forces the customer to describe what they do rather than what they imagine.

Elicitation also means asking about frequency and volume. How many users run this process? How often? Is it a one-time migration or an ongoing daily flow? Those answers are not decoration. They determine whether a component that works for ten users a day also works for ten thousand, and whether the process is worth automating at all. A process that runs twice a year for three people may not justify an OmniScript. A process that runs a thousand times a day absolutely does. The exam rewards the candidate who asks these questions, because the answers change the design.

### Every requirement carries hidden assumptions

A stated requirement is never complete. Underneath it sit assumptions the customer has not examined, and the architect's job is to surface them. The curriculum names the recurring ones, and they appear on the exam in nearly every scenario.

User volume is an assumption. The customer says the flow must handle their users, but they have not said how many, whether they all run it at once, or whether the number will grow. Response-time tolerance is an assumption. The customer says the flow must be fast, but they have not said what "fast" means in seconds or which step has the tightest budget. Source-system data quality is an assumption. The customer says the billing system returns pricing, but they have not said whether the data is clean, complete, or reachable. Whether the process is one-time or ongoing is an assumption. A one-time data migration and a recurring daily flow are different designs.

The danger is that an unvalidated assumption silently becomes a design constraint. The curriculum gives the sharpest example: the architect assumes the billing system returns pricing in under a second, designs the flow around that speed, and the assumption fails in production when the real system takes four seconds. The assumption was never a fact. It was a guess that got baked into the design. The exam tests exactly this, and the correct answer is always the one that validates the assumption before it becomes a decision.

### Assumptions must be written down and confirmed

An assumption you hold in your head is not an assumption you can validate. The discipline is to write every assumption down and confirm it with the customer in writing. This is not bureaucracy. It is the difference between a design measured against a real baseline and a design measured against a guess.

Take the billing system example. The architect writes down, "Assumption: the billing system returns pricing in under one second." The customer reviews it and either confirms it or corrects it. If they confirm it, the architect can design to that budget with confidence. If they correct it and say the real response is three to five seconds, the architect designs differently, with a loading state, a progress indicator, or a server-side Integration Procedure that does not block the user. Either way, the written confirmation turns a guess into a fact the design can stand on.

The exam frames this as a hard rule. An unvalidated assumption about source-system speed or data quality is the most common silent cause of a production failure. The candidate who writes assumptions down and gets them confirmed in writing avoids that failure. The candidate who keeps them in notes and proceeds does not.

### Separate what the customer wants from what the process needs

Users describe the solution they imagine, not the problem they have. This is the single most important insight in requirements elicitation, and the exam builds questions around it. A customer describes the exact screens they want, the exact fields, the exact layout. The architect who builds those screens may solve the wrong problem, because the customer described a solution they invented rather than the process they actually run.

The way to separate wants from needs is to walk through real scenarios with sample data. Instead of asking the customer to describe the screens, ask them to show you a real transaction. What data comes in? What does the user do with it? What is the successful outcome? Sample data makes the process concrete. It forces the customer to confront the actual inputs and outputs instead of the imagined interface.

A worked example makes this concrete. A customer asks for a quote flow with a specific three-screen layout they sketched on a whiteboard. The architect does not build the sketch. The architect walks the customer through a real quote: the agent opens the account, pulls the customer's plan and usage, and the pricing engine returns a number. The walkthrough reveals that the real need is not the three screens but a fast, accurate price returned from the billing system, with the screens as a thin wrapper around it. The architect designs for the need, the price, and the screens follow. The exam tests this by offering the described screens as a tempting wrong answer and the process need as the correct one.

### Validation is iterative

Elicitation does not end when the walkthrough is done. Validation is iterative. The architect confirms each requirement against a concrete scenario, checks that the data needed to satisfy it exists and is reachable, and flags any requirement that depends on a system or field that is not yet available.

The key word is reachable. A requirement may be perfectly stated and still fail because the data it needs does not exist, or exists in a system the flow cannot call, or lives in a field that is not populated. The architect checks each requirement against the actual data that exists. If the requirement needs a customer's credit score and the credit bureau integration is not built yet, that requirement is flagged as dependent on an unavailable system. It is not assumed away. It is recorded as a risk until the dependency resolves.

Validation also means confirming the acceptance criterion with the customer. For each requirement, the architect writes a concrete, measurable criterion. Not "the flow is fast," but "the flow completes in under five seconds for a returning customer." The customer agrees to that number or pushes back with a different one. Either way, the requirement now has a testable definition, and the design can be measured against it.

### The requirements baseline

The output of elicitation is a requirements baseline. This is a document that names the process, the actors, the data inputs and outputs, and the acceptance criteria. It is the contract the OmniStudio design is measured against. Every component you build, every Data Mapper you configure, every Integration Procedure you orchestrate is validated against this baseline. If the design satisfies the baseline, it satisfies the need. If it does not, it fails regardless of how elegant it is.

The baseline is not a wish list. It is a signed agreement. The customer reviews it, confirms the assumptions, agrees to the acceptance criteria, and signs off before design begins. That sign-off is what makes the baseline durable. Without it, the design is measured against an unstated and possibly wrong baseline, and the customer can change the goal after the build starts. With it, both sides know what success means.

The exam treats the baseline as the correct output of needs determination. A list of questions with no decisions is not a baseline. A copy of the customer's RFP is input, not output. A fixed architecture chosen up front is not a baseline either. The baseline is the documented, agreed set of requirements, assumptions, and acceptance criteria that the design is measured against.

### Worked scenario: the quote flow

A full scenario pulls the method together. A financial services customer asks for a quote flow. The stated requirement is, "Agents need a new quote flow that returns pricing instantly." Read at face value, that is an OmniScript with a pricing step. The exam wants you to read past it.

The architect starts with the business outcome. What do agents actually do? They open an account, pull the customer's plan and usage, and get a price from the billing system. The walkthrough reveals the process: three data inputs, one external call, one result. The architect then surfaces the assumptions. "Instantly" is not a number. The architect asks what the business will tolerate and learns the billing system actually takes three to five seconds. User volume is a few hundred agents running quotes all day. The data quality of the billing system is uneven, with some accounts missing usage records.

Each finding reshapes the design. Because the pricing call is slow and the user should not wait, the architect plans an Integration Procedure that runs the call server-side and returns a result, with a loading state in the OmniScript. Because the billing data is uneven, the architect plans validation and a fallback message when usage is missing. The architect writes down every assumption, confirms the response-time budget with the customer in writing, and produces a baseline that names the process, the actors, the data inputs and outputs, and the acceptance criterion: the quote completes in under five seconds for a returning customer with complete data.

That baseline is the deliverable. The exam question asks what the architect should do with the "instantly" requirement, and the correct answer is to probe the underlying assumption, the source system's actual response time and the latency the business will tolerate, and validate it before it becomes a design constraint. Accepting it unexamined risks a design that fails in production. Ignoring it because it is not measurable is wrong, because timing is measurable and important. Assuming the billing system can always respond in under a second is exactly the unvalidated assumption that causes failures.

## Configuration Walkthrough

Requirements elicitation happens before you open the designer, but it follows a repeatable sequence you can run in any discovery session.

1. Run a discovery session where you walk the customer through one real end-to-end scenario. Capture the steps, the systems touched, and the data used at each step. Ask about frequency and volume, and ask what a successful step looks like.
2. List every assumption you hear, such as user volume, response-time tolerance, and source-system data quality. Mark each assumption as confirmed, unconfirmed, or at risk, and write down the ones you still need to validate.
3. For each requirement, write a concrete acceptance criterion, for example the flow completes in under five seconds for a returning customer. Confirm the customer agrees with the criterion, and revise it if they push back.
4. Produce a requirements baseline document that names the process, the actors, the data inputs and outputs, and the acceptance criteria. Have the customer sign off on it before design begins.

## Exam Traps

- **Accepting a requirement at face value without probing the underlying assumption.** A customer says the flow must return data instantly, and the architect designs for the fastest possible response. The unexamined assumption, that the source system returns data instantly, becomes a design constraint that fails in production. Probe the assumption, the source system's actual response time and the latency the business will tolerate, and validate it before it becomes a decision.
- **Designing the solution the customer describes instead of the process they need.** Users often describe the imagined solution rather than the underlying problem. Building the exact screens the customer sketched may solve the wrong problem. Walk through real scenarios and sample data to separate what the customer wants from what the process actually needs.
- **Skipping written sign-off on assumptions and acceptance criteria.** Keeping the requirements in the architect's notes, or sharing them verbally in a meeting, leaves the design measured against an unstated and possibly wrong baseline. Write the assumptions down, confirm them with the customer in writing, and produce a requirements baseline with acceptance criteria that the customer signs off on before design begins.

## Exam Tip

Write every assumption down and get it confirmed in writing. An unvalidated assumption about source-system speed or data quality is the most common silent cause of a production failure, and the exam rewards the candidate who validates assumptions before they become design constraints. When a scenario gives you a vague word like "instantly" or "fast," treat it as a signal that an assumption is hiding underneath, and probe it.

## Try It

Take a real process from your own work and run it through the four-step walkthrough. Pick a flow you know well, such as a customer onboarding or a service request. Walk one end-to-end scenario and write down the steps, the systems touched, and the data used at each step. Then list every assumption you are making, including user volume, response-time tolerance, and source-system data quality, and mark each as confirmed, unconfirmed, or at risk. For each requirement, write a concrete acceptance criterion with a number in it, such as the flow completes in under five seconds for a returning customer. End by producing a one-page requirements baseline that names the process, the actors, the data inputs and outputs, and the acceptance criteria. That baseline is the deliverable the exam's requirements questions are asking for, and the discipline of writing it down is the skill this lesson teaches.
