The OmniStudio Consultant exam does not test whether you can build a FlexCard. It tests whether you can decide which FlexCard to build, and that decision lives in the project's risks and constraints. This requirement asks you to determine a project's needs by evaluating a customer's requirements, assumptions, risks, and constraints. The exam hands you a customer story and expects you to separate the hard boundaries from the may-or-may-not conditions, then design a solution that survives both. The candidate who treats every stated fact as a requirement picks the flashiest component. The candidate who sorts each fact into the right bucket picks the component that actually works in that customer's environment. This lesson makes that sorting automatic.

## Deep Dive

### Constraint: a hard boundary the design must respect

A constraint is a boundary the solution cannot cross. It is not a preference and it is not a hope. It is a fixed limit that the design must respect, and the architect identifies constraints early because they decide which OmniStudio tools and patterns are even viable.

The curriculum names the recurring kinds. A fixed budget is a constraint because it caps how much configuration and integration work the project can absorb. A mandated integration target is a constraint because the solution must talk to that specific system, not a nicer one the architect prefers. A data-privacy regulation is a constraint because it dictates where data can live, who can see it, and how it must be handled. A go-live date is a constraint because the design must be complete and stable by that date, not whenever it happens to be ready.

The word that separates a constraint from everything else is "must." A constraint is something the design must respect. If the customer says the quote flow must return in under two seconds, that is a constraint. If the customer says the flow should be fast, that is a preference, and preferences are negotiable in a way constraints are not. The exam rewards the candidate who reads "must" and treats it as a wall rather than a suggestion.

Why does identifying constraints early matter for OmniStudio specifically? Because constraints change the tool choice. A strict response-time SLA pushes you toward a Turbo Extract over a standard Extract, because the Turbo variant reads only the fields you name and skips the overhead of a full query. A data-privacy regulation that forbids storing sensitive values in a log pushes you toward scrubbing the response before it reaches an Integration Procedure's debug output. A go-live date that leaves no room for a custom Apex build pushes you toward a purely declarative OmniScript that can ship without a code deployment. The constraint does not just limit the design. It selects the design.

### Risk: a condition that may or may not occur

A risk is the opposite of a constraint in one crucial way. A constraint is certain. A risk is a condition that may or may not occur, and the architect's job is to assess its likelihood and impact and plan a mitigation rather than assume it away.

The curriculum names the recurring risks. A source system being unavailable is a risk, because the outage may or may not happen. A data-quality gap is a risk, because the source data may or may not be clean enough to load. A key stakeholder changing requirements is a risk, because the scope may or may not shift mid-project. Each of these is a condition, not a boundary. The design does not have to respect them as walls. The design has to survive them if they happen.

The critical discipline is that a risk is handled with a mitigation, not with hope. The exam phrasing is blunt about this. You do not assume a risk away because the customer is confident. You rank risks by likelihood and impact, and you give the highest-ranked ones a concrete mitigation. A fallback data path is a mitigation for a source system that might be unavailable. A data-quality cleanup step is a mitigation for source data that might be dirty. A phased rollout is a mitigation for a scope that might grow. Each mitigation is a specific, designed response, not a generic "we will monitor it."

### The same fact can be a constraint in one org and a risk in another

The exam's favorite trick is that classification is not fixed. The same requirement can be a constraint in one customer's environment and a risk in another, so the architect evaluates each fact against the customer's actual situation rather than applying a label by rote.

The curriculum gives the cleanest example. A strict response-time SLA is a constraint, because it is a hard boundary the design must respect. The source system's reliability behind that SLA is a risk, because the source may or may not be available when the flow needs it. The SLA is the wall. The reliability is the condition that could break the wall.

Now flip the environment. In a different org, the customer has no formal SLA, but the source system is known to be flaky. There, the reliability is still a risk, but there is no SLA constraint to protect, so the mitigation changes. You might add a retry and a cached fallback rather than engineer for a hard two-second ceiling. The same two facts produce different designs in different orgs because the constraint set differs. The exam tests whether you classify against the customer's environment, not against a memorized table.

### Constraints often conflict, and the conflict must be surfaced

A single constraint is easy to respect. Two constraints that pull in opposite directions are where projects fail, and the exam wants you to know the correct response to a conflict.

The curriculum names the classic case: a tight timeline against a large integration scope. The timeline says the project must ship soon. The scope says the project must integrate a lot. Both are hard boundaries. You cannot satisfy both at full strength, and the architect's job is to surface the conflict and force a decision, because silently trading one off against the other produces a design that fails one of them.

Silently cutting integration scope to hit the timeline fails the integration requirement. Silently letting the timeline slip to build the full scope fails the schedule constraint. Either silent trade produces a design that violates a stated boundary. The correct move is to put the conflict in front of the customer and force a decision: reduce the scope, move the date, or accept a phased delivery that lands the critical integrations first and the rest later. The decision is the customer's to make, because only the customer can weigh their own constraints. The architect's job is to make the conflict visible and force the choice, not to make the trade quietly.

### Risks are ranked, and the highest-ranked get a concrete mitigation

Not every risk deserves the same treatment. The exam wants you to rank risks by likelihood and impact, then spend your mitigation effort where it matters.

Likelihood is how probable the risk is. Impact is how much damage it does if it occurs. A risk that is very likely and very damaging gets a full mitigation. A risk that is unlikely and minor gets a lighter treatment, or a documented acceptance. The ranking is what keeps the register honest, because a register that lists every conceivable risk at equal weight is a register that protects nothing.

The curriculum is explicit that the highest-ranked risks get a concrete mitigation, not a generic promise. A fallback data path is concrete. A data-quality cleanup step is concrete. A phased rollout is concrete. Each names a specific action the project will take. A register that says "we will monitor the source system" is not a mitigation, because monitoring does not prevent the failure. The exam phrasing is blunt: a register that only documents problems does nothing to prevent them.

### The output: a documented risk-and-constraint register

All of this work converges on a single deliverable, a risk-and-constraint register. The register names each item, its type, its impact on the OmniStudio design, and the agreed mitigation. It is the artifact the architect uses to defend design decisions and to re-evaluate when the project changes.

The register has a specific shape. Each row names the item, labels it as a constraint or a risk, states its impact on the design, and records the agreed mitigation. A constraint row might read: "Response-time SLA of two seconds; constraint; forces a Turbo Extract and a lean response shape; no mitigation, it is a boundary." A risk row might read: "Source billing system outage; risk, high likelihood, high impact; could break the SLA; mitigation is a cached fallback and a retry with a controlled error state."

The register is not a static document. It is a living artifact that the architect revisits whenever the project scope changes. When the customer adds an integration, the register gains a row. When the go-live date moves, the timeline constraint changes and the risks behind it are re-ranked. The register is how the architect defends a design decision in a review, because it shows the reasoning behind every choice. It is also how the architect catches a problem early, because a change that invalidates a mitigation shows up as a row that no longer holds.

### Worked example: a quote flow with a flaky source

Working one scenario end to end makes the method concrete. A customer asks for a quote flow that captures a customer's plan and usage, calls a billing system to price it, and shows the quote on the account record. Read for the surface requirement and you see an OmniScript for the guided capture, an Integration Procedure for the pricing call, and a FlexCard for the quote display. That reading is correct as far as it goes.

Now sort the facts. The customer states a strict response-time SLA of two seconds. That is a constraint, a hard boundary the design must respect. The billing system is known to be unreliable. That is a risk, a condition that may or may not occur, and it is the risk that could break the SLA constraint. The two facts are not the same kind of thing, and the exam's first question on this requirement tests exactly that distinction.

The design follows from the classification. The SLA constraint pushes the pricing call toward a lean Integration Procedure with a Turbo Extract that reads only the fields the quote needs. The reliability risk pushes the same call toward a retry and a cached fallback, so that when the billing system is down, the flow returns a cached quote or a controlled error instead of hanging past the SLA. The register records both: the SLA as a constraint with no mitigation, and the reliability as a high-likelihood, high-impact risk with a concrete fallback mitigation.

Now add a conflict. The customer also wants the full integration scope, every product line priced, delivered in three weeks. The timeline and the scope are two constraints that conflict. The architect surfaces the conflict and forces a decision. The customer chooses a phased rollout: the top product lines ship in three weeks, the rest follow in a second phase. That decision is recorded in the register, and the design is built to the agreed phase. Nothing is traded silently, and neither constraint is failed outright.

## Configuration Walkthrough

Needs determination happens before you open the designer, but it follows a repeatable sequence you can apply to any customer story.

1. List every hard boundary the customer states. Budget, go-live date, integration target, and regulation all qualify. Label each one as a constraint, and write it as a "must" statement so the boundary is explicit.
2. For each constraint, identify the risk that could break it. A source system outage sits behind a response-time SLA. A data-quality gap sits behind a load requirement. Rate each risk's likelihood and impact on a simple high, medium, or low scale.
3. For the highest-ranked risks, write a concrete mitigation. A fallback data path, a data-quality cleanup step, or a phased rollout all qualify. Confirm each mitigation with the customer, because a mitigation the customer will not fund is not a mitigation.
4. Produce a risk-and-constraint register that names each item, its type, its impact on the design, and the agreed mitigation. Revisit the register whenever the project scope changes, and re-rank the risks when a constraint moves.

## Exam Traps

- **Treating a constraint as a risk, or vice versa.** A constraint is a hard boundary the design must respect. A risk is a condition that may or may not occur. Confusing the two leads to designing around something that is actually a wall, or assuming away something that will actually break the design. When a fact is stated as a "must," it is a constraint. When it is a condition that might happen, it is a risk.
- **Silently trading off conflicting constraints.** A tight timeline against a large integration scope is a conflict between two hard boundaries. Silently cutting scope to hit the date fails the integration requirement, and silently slipping the date to build the full scope fails the schedule. The correct move is to surface the conflict and force a decision, not to make the trade quietly.
- **Listing risks without mitigations.** A register that names problems but records no mitigation documents the risk and does nothing to prevent it. Every high-ranked risk needs a concrete mitigation, such as a fallback data path, a cleanup step, or a phased rollout. A generic "we will monitor it" is not a mitigation.

## Exam Tip

Keep a risk-and-constraint register with a concrete mitigation for every high-ranked risk. A register that only lists problems does not protect the design. When a scenario question asks how to handle a risk, the correct answer always includes a specific mitigation, never a promise to watch the problem.

## Try It

Take a customer requirement from your own work and run it through the four-step walkthrough. Write down every hard boundary the customer states and label each as a constraint. For each constraint, identify the risk that could break it and rate its likelihood and impact. For the highest-ranked risks, write a concrete mitigation and confirm it with the customer. Then produce a one-page register that names each item, its type, its impact on the design, and the agreed mitigation. Finally, find two constraints that conflict and write the decision you would force the customer to make. That register is the deliverable the exam's "determine a project's needs" questions are asking for.
