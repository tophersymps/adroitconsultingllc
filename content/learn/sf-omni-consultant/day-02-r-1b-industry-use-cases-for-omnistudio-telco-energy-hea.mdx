The first exam domain, OmniStudio Fundamentals (16% of the exam), rewards the candidate who can describe the toolkit in action, not just name it. Requirement R-1B asks you to describe the OmniStudio solution and the common customer use case scenarios across industries such as telco, energy, healthcare, and the public sector. The exam does not probe your memory of marketing slides. It hands you a scenario, names an industry and a business process, and asks you to pick the pattern and the tool that fits. That is why this lesson is built around mapping a business process to a pattern, and a pattern to a tool, for each industry the exam names.

The core claim to internalize first: every vertical the exam mentions reuses the same toolkit. There is no telco-specific OmniScript, no healthcare edition of the FlexCard, and no energy-only Integration Procedure. The four tools that do the work, the OmniScript, the FlexCard, the Integration Procedure (IP), and the Data Mapper (formerly DataRaptor), recur in every industry because the underlying work is the same shape. A high-volume, form-heavy, multi-step process that must be guided, consistent, and integrated with existing systems appears in every vertical. Once you see that shared shape, the industry name becomes atmosphere and the tool choice becomes obvious.

## Deep Dive

### The four recurring tools and the rule that picks them

Before mapping industries, fix the four tools and the rule that selects each one, because the exam question at the top of the domain always reduces to that rule.

- **OmniScript.** A guided, multi-step user journey. A user moves through a sequence of screens, validates input as they go, and finishes by writing data back to Salesforce or to an external system. The OmniScript is the tool for anything a person completes step by step, where the order of those steps and the guidance between them matter.
- **FlexCard.** A contextual display component. It shows data about the current context, typically the record on screen, with states, drill-down flyouts, and actions. A FlexCard summarizes what is relevant and lets the user expand or act without navigating away.
- **Integration Procedure (IP).** Server-side orchestration. Where an OmniScript runs in the browser and walks a person through steps, an IP runs on the server and chains together data operations, external callouts, and business logic in a single call, with no user in front of it.
- **Data Mapper.** Extract, transform, and load of data. A Data Mapper reads from Salesforce, reshapes JSON, and writes records back, and it is the tool you reach for when data has to move or change shape between steps.

The rule that picks among them is the verb in the requirement. A guided journey is an OmniScript. A summary to display is a FlexCard. A system-to-system call is an Integration Procedure. A payload transform is a Data Mapper. Hold that mapping, because this lesson's central teaching is the architect's job: map the customer's business process to the right tool rather than force a tool onto a process.

### Telecommunications: order and service management

The recurring capture in telco is order and service management. A customer wants a new line, or an existing customer wants to add a device, change a plan, or tack on an add-on like an international calling package. The business handles that as a guided journey: the rep selects a plan, configures the device and add-ons, checks eligibility, sees the price, and hands the order off to a quote or to billing.

Map each step of that journey to a tool. The overall shape is a guided, multi-step flow, so an OmniScript drives it. Inside the flow, the steps that check the billing system for whether this customer is eligible for a promotion, or pull the current price for a device and plan bundle, are lookups that belong in Integration Procedures. The OmniScript invokes those IPs at the moment it needs the answer, and the IP reaches the billing system. The customer's eligibility result and the computed price come back into the flow, and the rep confirms and submits the order.

Why this two-part split matters: keep the guidance in the OmniScript and keep the system access in the IP. If you dragged the billing callout into every step, you would rebuild the same integration repeatedly and create a maintenance burden. By isolating the lookup in one IP and invoking it from the OmniScript, you write the integration once and reuse it.

**Worked example.** A rep opens an order-capture OmniScript. Step one presents plan options and the rep selects the Premium plan. Step two prompts for the device and two add-ons. On reaching step three, the OmniScript invokes an IP named Get-Pricing-Eligibility. That IP runs a Data Mapper Extract against the billing system data, calls an HTTP Action to check current prices, and returns a JSON payload with an eligible flag and a total. The OmniScript renders that total on the confirmation step, the rep records the quote, and the order hands off for provisioning. Scoring the tools: the OmniScript is the primary fit because the process is a guided journey; the IPs carry the eligibility and pricing lookups; a Data Mapper serves the extract inside those IPs.

### Energy and utilities: field and customer operations

In energy and utilities, the pattern centers on field and customer operations rather than order capture. The recurring journeys are service requests, outage reporting, and meter or connection changes. A customer reports an outage, or a field worker logs a meter swap, and the organization must capture what happened in a structured way and hand it to the outage or work-order system.

The guided capture belongs in an OmniScript. The customer, or the field tech, answers a few structured questions about the situation: location, what is affected, how long it has been happening. Because the report feeds a downstream system that expects a specific payload, a Data Mapper normalizes the data before it moves on. In current OmniStudio language that Data Mapper is called a Data Mapper, and in older materials you will still see the name DataRaptor; the exam uses the two interchangeably, so recognize both names for the same tool.

**Worked example.** A customer opens an outage-reporting OmniScript from a portal. They select their address, report that power is out, and pick the affected equipment from a list. When they submit, the OmniScript invokes an Integration Procedure. Inside that IP, a Data Mapper takes the friendly fields the user entered and reshapes them into the outage system's expected request shape, then an HTTP Action posts the normalized payload to the outage API and returns a ticket number. The OmniScript displays that ticket number and the expected response time. Here the rule is vivid: the OmniScript guides the report, and the Data Mapper normalizes the data, which is the closest thing to a full-fledged Data Mapper pattern this requirement demonstrates.

### Healthcare: patient and member journeys with privacy in mind

Healthcare is where the exam tests your sensitivity to data privacy more than your tool selection. The pattern is patient and member journeys: intake, eligibility verification, and benefits explanation. A patient arrives for a new practice, a member enrolls in a plan, or a provider verifies coverage and explains benefits. Each is a guided flow, so an OmniScript is the natural capture tool, but the data it touches is protected.

The architectural consideration outranks every tool decision here. Because intake involves sensitive eligibility and benefits data, the guided flow must respect the platform's security model and field-level access rather than build custom logic to bypass it. Field-level security governs which profiles and roles can see which fields. Sharing rules govern which users can see a given record. The OmniScript runs under the standard platform security model, and the architect's job is to confirm the profiles and field-level settings allow exactly the right roles to see the sensitive fields before the flow is built. If you build custom logic that reads all fields regardless of role, you have created a privacy violation no matter how convenient the flow.

**Worked example.** A member-intake OmniScript collects name, date of birth, policy number, and eligibility status. Date of birth and policy number are flagged as sensitive. Before building the journey, the architect confirms that the intake profile can read the eligibility fields but that the call-center profile looking at the same record page later can see only the non-sensitive summary in a FlexCard. The eligibility check at submit runs as an Integration Procedure that calls the payer's verification service, and the benefits explanation is rendered as a FlexCard whose drill-down data is restricted by field-level access. The rule to remember: field-level security is the access model, not an obstacle, and the flow that ignores it fails the exam question regardless of how clean the tool mapping looks.

### Public sector: applications and case intake with auditability

In the public sector, the pattern is applications and case intake: permits, licenses, benefits, and service requests. A citizen applies for a building permit, renews a license, or submits a benefits claim. The value OmniStudio delivers is threefold: consistent guided steps so every application collects the same information the same way, auditability of who submitted what and when, and integration with the legacy case or records systems the agency already runs.

Auditability is the exam concept here, and it is measured in the data, not in the tool. To be auditable, a submission must be captured in a record with clear metadata about who submitted it, what was in it, and when it arrived, so a reviewer can trace the case end to end. That rules out storing submissions only in browser cache, allowing anyone to edit a filed submission, or deleting history to save storage, because all three destroy the trail the process requires.

**Worked example.** A permit-applications OmniScript collects applicant identity, the permit type, the property address, and supporting documents with signatures. On submit it writes a custom object record, say Permit_Application__c, and stores submission metadata, the applicant ID, the submitter, and a timestamp, alongside it. The record and its metadata live in Salesforce, tied to the applicant, so the case is traceable from submission through review. A legacy system holds the final decision, reached through an Integration Procedure call, while the application record stays in Salesforce where the agency can audit it. The mapping: the OmniScript guides the application, and the durable record with submission metadata is what makes the flow auditable.

### The shared thread across industries

Assemble the four patterns and the common thread is obvious. Telco order capture, energy outage reporting, healthcare member intake, and public-sector permits all share the same shape: a high-volume, form-heavy, multi-step process that must be guided, consistent, and integrated with existing systems. That is why the same tools recur in every vertical instead of industry-specific variants. The exam is not expecting you to memorize a toolbox per industry; it expects you to recognize a process shape and name the tool that fits it.

The architect's judgment closes the lesson. Your job is to map the customer's business process to the right tool, not to force a tool onto a process. A simple lookup belongs in a FlexCard, because there is no multi-step journey to guide and a full OmniScript would add screens and maintenance for no benefit. A guided multi-step journey belongs in an OmniScript, because a FlexCard cannot hold the step-by-step control the process needs. A system-to-system call belongs in an Integration Procedure, and a payload transform belongs in a Data Mapper. Pick by the verb and the process, not by habit.

## Configuration Walkthrough

Walk through the same reasoning you would apply in the OmniStudio builders when a use case arrives.

1. **Sketch a telco quote-to-order flow and map every step to a tool.** Write down the journey: plan selection, device selection, add-ons, eligibility check, pricing, and quote handoff. Mark which steps the rep completes in the OmniScript (plan, device, add-ons, confirmation) and which are lookups to mark as Integration Procedure calls (eligibility and pricing against the billing system). Every guided step is an OmniScript element; every system lookup is an IP invoked from inside the flow.
2. **For energy outage reporting, split captured fields from enriched fields.** List the fields a customer must provide: address, affected equipment, and description. List what the outage system already knows: this account's outage status, outage history, and estimated restoration. Decide that the customer-provided fields are captured directly in the OmniScript, while the outage-system fields are enriched by an Integration Procedure that calls the outage API and returns them into the flow. Keep the normalization in a Data Mapper.
3. **For healthcare intake, secure the sensitive fields first.** Identify which fields are sensitive: date of birth, policy number, and clinical details if any. Before building the guided flow, confirm the profile and field-level security allow the right roles to see them and deny the rest. Verify that any FlexCard on the record page is limited by the same field-level settings. Do not build the flow until the access model is confirmed, because the flow inherits that model.
4. **For a public-sector permit, plan the audit trail.** Confirm the submission needs an audit trail and decide where the record and its submission metadata live. Choose a Salesforce custom object so the application record and its who, what, and when metadata are stored together and traceable. Note that the legacy decision system is reached by Integration Procedure, while the durable record stays in Salesforce for auditing.

## Exam Traps

- **Reusing one industry's flow template verbatim in another.** The tools are the same across telco, energy, healthcare, and the public sector, but the business rules, data-privacy constraints, and integration targets differ. Copying an order-capture flow into a healthcare intake ignores field-level security, and reusing an outage flow as a permit application loses the audit trail. Mapping per industry is required, not optional.
- **Ignoring data-privacy rules by building custom logic that bypasses field-level security.** In healthcare especially, a custom handler that reads all fields regardless of role defeats the platform's security model and is a privacy violation. The guided flow must respect the same access model as the rest of the org. Ethically and on the exam, the flow inherits field-level security; do not route around it.
- **Choosing a tool by habit rather than by process.** Forcing a simple lookup into a full OmniScript adds screens and maintenance for no benefit, while squeezing a guided journey into a FlexCard loses the step-by-step control the process needs. Pick by the verb and process shape, not by whatever tool you opened last.
- **Confusing DataRaptor and Data Mapper.** They are the same tool under an older and newer name. The exam uses both, so recognize either one as the extract, transform, and load tool rather than treating DataRaptor as something separate.

## Exam Tip

Map the customer's business process to the tool before opening any builder. A lookup is a FlexCard. A guided journey is an OmniScript. A system call is an Integration Procedure. A payload transform is a Data Mapper. Almost every R-1B item reduces to which of those four verbs the scenario uses, so read the scenario, underline the verb, and answer from the mapping rather than from the industry name.

## Try It

Open a sandbox with OmniStudio enabled and build two small artifacts. First, a telco order-capture OmniScript with three steps: plan selection, device and add-ons, and confirmation. For the pricing step, define a simple placeholder Integration Procedure that returns a total based on the selected plan, and invoke it from the confirmation step. Run it end to end and confirm the total appears. Second, build a healthcare intake OmniScript with two fields you mark sensitive, and before running it confirm the field-level security on those fields: assign the fields so one profile can read them and another cannot, then log in as each profile and confirm the flow respects the difference. That exercise proves the lesson's two core claims: the OmniScript guides and the IP looks up, and the flow inherits the platform's field-level access model rather than overriding it.
