A card that tries to show everything becomes a card that shows nothing useful. This is the tension Lesson 16 asks you to solve. The requirement, R-8b, sits in the FlexCards domain (worth 18 percent of the OmniStudio Consultant exam) and it asks you to recommend the implementation of necessary actions, including child FlexCards, on Flexcards to drive user workflows and business processes. The earlier action types from Lesson 15, links, modals, OmniScript launches, and Integration Procedure calls, all take the user somewhere or run some process. A child FlexCard does something different: it composes. It embeds one card inside another so that a summary can keep its focus while a smaller, reusable card carries the detail or hosts the nested workflow.[^1]

This is the composition lesson. Where Lesson 15 taught the five action types and how to match each to the user's next step, this lesson goes deeper into the one type that reuses the card machinery itself as the destination. You will learn what makes a card a child, how parent and child exchange data, how a modal and a child card combine into a drill-down pattern, and why shallow nesting is a design rule rather than a preference. These decisions show up directly on the exam, and the traps are about getting the data contract and the nesting depth right, not about knowing which button to click.

## Deep Dive

### What a child FlexCard is

A child FlexCard is a FlexCard that is marked as embeddable and then embedded inside another FlexCard, the parent. To mark a card as a child, you enable the Is Child FlexCard setting on the card definition. Once it is marked and activated, the card can be dropped onto the canvas of another card, and the card you dropped it into becomes its parent.[^1][^2]

The point of the split is focus. Instead of building one monolithic card that holds every field, every table, every action, and every rule for a record, you build a parent that shows the overview and one or more children that each own a slice of the detail. The parent hosts the child, and the child can be bound to its own data source or to data the parent passes down. Each card stays small, readable, and responsible for one thing, and the composition of those cards produces the full view.[^2]

That last clause, "bound to its own data source or to data the parent passes down," is the architect's lever. A child card can fetch its own data, independent of the parent. That independence is what lets you show data from many sources on a single screen: the parent pulls the record header, one child pulls a related list, another child pulls data from an integration. There is no limit on the number of child cards a parent can hold. The design freedom is real, and it is exactly the freedom you have to manage carefully.[^1]

So when the exam asks why someone would use a child FlexCard, the honest answers are the architectural ones: reusability, multiple data sources on one card, and flyouts. A child card built once can be embedded in multiple parents, saving the work of rebuilding or cloning the same view for every place it is needed. That is the payoff of composition, and it is the reason a well-built child card is designed to be context-agnostic rather than wired to one record.[^1]

### Why child cards are the composition mechanism for nested action flows

The child FlexCard is not just a way to show more data. It is the composition mechanism for nested action flows, which is why the requirement lists it as an action type alongside the more obvious buttons. A parent card shows a summary, and a child card shows the detail or the action surface for a specific part of that summary. The user reads the overview, then drills into the piece they need to act on, and the action they take lives in the child card rather than being crammed into the parent.

This mirrors how a person actually works. You do not decide everything from one screen full of everything. You get a sense of the situation, then focus on the part that needs your attention, then act on that part. A parent card with a child card mirrors that rhythm: see the overview, then drill into the piece you need to act on. The child is where the focused action happens, and the parent is what keeps the whole thing in context.

That is why the requirement frames child FlexCards as part of "actions that drive user workflows." A child card is a workflow driver because it gives the user an in-context place to act on a slice of the data without leaving the record, the page, or the workflow. The nesting is not decoration. It is the structure that lets a summary card stay a summary while the detail and its actions live one level down, close enough to reach and focused enough to use.

### Data flows between parent and child in two directions

The core of child-card design, and the place most scenarios go wrong, is the data contract: how the parent and child exchange data. Data flows two ways, and each direction has a purpose.

Parent to child: the parent passes context or data down to the child. In the FlexCard Designer you do this by binding a data node on the child FlexCard element to a node in the parent. You select the child card you are embedding, then choose which data node feeds it. The child receives exactly what the parent decides to hand it, whether that is the record, a related list, a merge field, or a slice of the parent's data.[^1][^2]

Child to parent: the child communicates its result or raises an event that the parent reacts to. When a child card runs an action, changes state, or completes a step, the parent needs a way to know. Events and the card's refresh behavior are how the parent stays in sync with what happened in the child. If the child runs a nested workflow that modifies data, the parent must reflect the new state, or the user is looking at stale information inside a card they trust.

Getting this contract right is the load-bearing design decision. The child should receive exactly what it needs and no more, and it should communicate its outcome back clearly enough that the parent can react. A child that receives the whole parent payload when it needs one field is sloppy; a child that receives nothing outside its own fetch and never tells the parent what happened is broken. Both are data-contract failures, and both make the child hard to reuse because the contract is implicit rather than explicit.

Here is the phrasing to hold for the exam: the child should receive exactly what it needs and communicate its outcome back clearly. That sentence is the design goal of every child card you build, and it is the standard the exam uses to judge a good child-card design from a bad one.

### Child cards inside modals: the drill-down pattern

The most common pattern that combines everything so far is the child card hosted inside a modal. A parent card opens a modal, and the modal hosts a child FlexCard that captures the input or shows the detail. The user stays on the page, the modal opens in context, and the child card inside it does the real work of showing the detail or collecting the action.

This pattern is worth naming because it is on the exam and it keeps coming up in real designs. The drill-down flow works like this. The parent shows a summary with a button or a row action. When the user clicks it, a modal opens. Inside the modal, a child FlexCard renders the detail for the specific item the user clicked, or hosts a small form for a quick capture. When the user is done, the modal closes and the parent card can refresh to reflect what happened.

The combination is stronger than either piece alone. A modal alone gives you an in-context dialog, but it is just a container. A child card alone gives you a reusable view, but it needs a home. Together, a modal that hosts a child FlexCard gives you an in-context, reusable detail or capture surface. This is exactly the right answer to the exam scenario where a parent card should show a summary and let the user drill into detail without leaving the page. The link takes them away, a static text element shows no detail, and a child with no data contract has nothing to display. The modal with a child card keeps them in context and shows real detail.[^3]

A flyout is the same idea in a different container. Instead of a modal, the child card appears in a popover or window triggered by a flyout action. The reuse principle is identical: the child is built once and surfaced where the user needs it, near the data that justifies it. Whether you land on a modal or a flyout depends on how much room the interaction needs and whether it should cover the page or float beside it.

![Drill-down pattern: a parent summary with a button opens a modal that hosts a child FlexCard showing detail or capturing input, then the modal closes and the parent refreshes, with a flyout as the alternative container](</diagrams/day-16-r-8b-child-flexcards-and-nested-action-flows/2-drill-down-modal.png>)

### Nesting depth is a design constraint

Here is the discipline that separates a good architect from a careless one. The child FlexCard machinery supports deep nesting, and the platform documentation is explicit that cards support multiple levels of embedded cards, down to recursive nesting. Just because you can nest five levels deep does not mean you should.[^3][^4]

Deep nesting multiplies two bad things. First, it multiplies data fetches. Each level of child that fetches its own data adds a round trip per child, and the fetched data accumulates across the tree. A parent with children, grandchildren, and great-grandchildren becomes a series of staggered network calls that the user waits on, and a fast card degrades into a slow one.

Second, deep nesting complicates the data contract. As the tree grows, tracing where a value came from and how it reached a deep child becomes genuinely hard. The parent passed something to the child, which passed it to a grandchild, which combined it with its own fetch, and by the time you are debugging a wrong value you are tracing through four layers of handoff. Every extra level is another place for the contract to be unclear.

The rule is shallow nesting. Prefer a parent with one or two levels of children, each with a single, clear responsibility. If you find yourself nesting a third level deep, stop and ask what the middle level is actually for. Very often it is just passing data down, and the whole tree can be flattened so the leaf child receives its context directly from the parent.

There is also a mechanical reason to keep cards small, and it reinforces shallow nesting from a different angle. When OmniStudio compiles a FlexCard, it generates a Lightning Web Component, and each generated component file has a size ceiling. Because child Flexcards are separate LWCs, splitting a big card into smaller child cards is not just a design nicety, it is a practical way to stay under the compilation size limit. Multiple smaller cards beat one giant card by construction, which is a concrete, non-theoretical reason the composition approach scales.[^4]

![Shallow versus deep nesting: one or two levels of children, each receiving context directly from the parent, avoids the multiplied round trips and hard-to-trace data handoffs of deep nesting](</diagrams/day-16-r-8b-child-flexcards-and-nested-action-flows/3-shallow-vs-deep-nesting.png>)

### Design child cards to be context-agnostic

Because child cards are reusable, they should be designed to be context-agnostic: they receive their data and context from the parent rather than assuming a fixed record. A child card that hard-codes a record, a specific ID, or a specific account cannot be embedded anywhere useful. It is welded to one situation, and the moment you want to reuse it in another parent, it is useless.

The payoff of composition only exists if the child is portable. So you design the child's data contract to be explicit and self-contained: the child declares what it needs, the parent supplies it through the data node, and the child renders from the data it is given rather than from an assumption baked into the card. A well-designed child card can be reused across parents that meet its contract, which is exactly the reusability the exam values.

Make the contrast explicit, because it is an exam trap. A reusable child card with an explicit, self-contained data contract that receives its context from the parent is the correct answer. A child card with a hard-coded record is the wrong answer, because it cannot be embedded in multiple parents and it defeats the entire purpose of composition.

![Reusable versus hard-coded child: a context-agnostic child that receives context from the parent through an explicit data contract embeds in multiple parents, while a child with a hard-coded record renders the wrong data everywhere](</diagrams/day-16-r-8b-child-flexcards-and-nested-action-flows/4-reusable-vs-hardcoded.png>)

## Worked Example: the account summary card

Put it together on one card so the pieces become concrete. A sales agent opens an account record that renders a parent FlexCard. The parent shows the account header, the key metric, and a small set of actions. It does not try to show every contact, every open case, every recent order, and every billing detail, because that would be the monolithic card this lesson warns against. Instead, the parent hosts children.

- A **Contacts child card** is embedded directly in the parent below the header. It has its own data source that pulls the account's contacts, and it renders a compact list. The parent hands it the account context so it knows which account to show, and the child does the rest. This child is reusable: the same contact card can be embedded in a case record page or a contract record page wherever contacts are relevant.[^1]

- An **Order history child card** is embedded inside a modal. The parent shows a summary line, "3 open orders, $41,200 total," and a View Orders button. The button opens a modal, and the modal hosts the order history child card. The user sees the detail without leaving the account page, and when they close the modal they are back where they started. No navigation, no lost context.[^3]

- The **Contacts card** runs a nested action flow. Each contact row has a "send email" action that launches a short OmniScript. The contact card hands the OmniScript the contact ID, so the flow opens pre-filled and pointed at the right person. The parent never grows a single email action of its own; the action lives where the data lives, in the child.

Now make the deliberate mistakes and watch each one break the design. Nest the order history three levels deep, with a middle card that does nothing but pass data down: the page makes extra fetches, the latency climbs, and debugging where the order total came from means tracing three layers. Give the contacts card a hard-coded record ID: it renders the same contacts everywhere, so embedding it in a case page shows the wrong data and the reuse story collapses. Embed the contacts card without a data contract: it receives nothing meaningful, so it renders empty or stale, and the parent cannot react to anything the child does because the child never reported back. Each failure looks small in isolation and each one breaks the composition.

## Configuration Walkthrough

Configuring a child FlexCard follows the same shape regardless of whether it sits directly in the parent or inside a modal. Work in the FlexCard Designer.

1. **Activate the child, then embed it.** Mark the child card as a child by enabling Is Child FlexCard, and activate it. In the parent FlexCard editor, drag the Flexcard element onto the canvas and open its properties. Use the Flexcard Name field to select the child card definition to embed.[^1]

2. **Define the data contract.** Decide what the child needs from the parent, and bind the child Flexcard element's data node to that data in the parent. The parent passes the context or data node the child needs. Then configure how the child communicates back: how the child's actions report their result and how the parent refreshes to reflect it. This step is not optional. Without the data node the child renders blind, and without the return path the parent cannot react.[^2]

3. **For a drill-down, host the child inside a modal.** If the child is a detail view or a quick capture, add an action on the parent that opens a modal, and place the child FlexCard inside that modal. The user stays in context while viewing the child's detail. Confirm the modal closes after the interaction and that the parent updates afterward, so the in-context benefit is not lost to a modal that leaves the parent stale.[^3]

4. **Keep nesting shallow.** For each child, verify it has a single, clear responsibility, and count the total data fetches across the card tree. If the tree makes more fetches than the page can absorb, or a middle level exists only to pass data down, flatten it. Prefer one or two levels of children, each receiving its context directly from the parent.

## Exam Traps

Three traps recur, and each is a design judgment rather than a syntax detail.

- **Deeply nested child cards that each fetch their own data.** The multiplied fetches slow the page, and the data contract becomes hard to trace as a value travels through several handoffs. Shallow nesting is the answer: a parent with one or two levels of children, each receiving what it needs from the parent instead of re-fetching everything itself.

- **A child card that assumes a fixed record.** A child designed with a hard-coded record instead of receiving its context from the parent is unusable in other parents. That defeats the purpose of composition. Design the child's data contract to be explicit and self-contained so the child can be embedded wherever its contract is met.

- **Embedding a child without a return contract.** If a child card is embedded but you never define how it communicates its result back to the parent, the parent cannot react to the child's outcome, and the workflow stalls in silence. The click succeeds, the child does its thing, and the parent stays unaware. Define the return contract as part of the design, not as an afterthought.

## Exam Tip

Compose with shallow nesting. Each child has one clear responsibility, receives its context from the parent, and communicates its result back explicitly. Read every scenario with that standard in hand: what does the child need to do its job (its data contract), and how does the parent learn what it did (its return contract)? A child that satisfies both is reusable and safe to nest. One that ignores either will look fine on the canvas and break in production.

Open a sandbox with OmniStudio enabled and rebuild the account summary card from this lesson. Create a child FlexCard that lists an account's contacts, mark it as a child, and activate it. Embed it in a parent card, bind the parent's account context to the child's data node, and confirm the child renders the right contacts. Then build the drill-down: add an order history child card, put it inside a modal opened by a button on the parent, and verify the user sees the detail without navigating away. Give the contacts child a row action that launches a short OmniScript with the contact ID, and confirm the flow opens pre-filled. Then break it. Nest the order history three levels deep and watch the latency and the tracing pain multiply. Each failure teaches the same lesson you need for the exam: the data contract and the nesting depth decide whether a child card is reusable or a liability.
