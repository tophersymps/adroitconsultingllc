A team creates a custom object named Customer because it wants full control. It works, and the team is happy, until it needs standard reporting and integration, and discovers that the data is split between the custom object and the standard Account object, with two sources of truth and no clean way to reconcile them. The custom object duplicated a standard one, and the team paid for it.

That is the failure this lesson exists to prevent. Requirement r-31b, part of the Best Fit Solutions domain worth 15% of the Salesforce OmniStudio Consultant exam, asks you to recommend optimal object design, standard vs custom, to support OmniStudio artifacts. The exam wants you to understand that the standard-versus-custom decision is a trade-off between fit and control.

## Deep Dive

### The standard-versus-custom decision is a trade-off between fit and control

The standard-versus-custom decision is a trade-off between fit and control. A standard object (Account, Contact, Opportunity, Case) brings built-in behavior, reporting, and integration with the rest of the platform, but it also brings constraints and baggage. A custom object brings full control over fields and behavior but none of the standard object's ecosystem. The architect should choose standard where the standard object's behavior matches the business need, and custom where it does not.

Think about what each side actually gives you. A standard object comes with the platform. It has reporting, it integrates with other features, and users are familiar with it. But it also comes with constraints: required fields, record types, sharing behavior, all of which are part of the standard object's design. A custom object gives you full control over fields and behavior, but you build the ecosystem yourself. No built-in reporting, no integration with the platform's features, no user familiarity. You trade the ecosystem for control.

The exam wants you to make this trade-off deliberately. Standard where the standard object's behavior matches the business need, custom where it does not. The decision is not about which is better in the abstract. It is about which fits the specific business concept and the artifacts that will use it.

### Standard objects are the right choice when the business concept matches the object's purpose

Standard objects are the right choice when the business concept matches the object's purpose. A customer is an Account,[^1] a person is a Contact, a sales opportunity is an Opportunity, a support issue is a Case. Using the standard object means the solution inherits the object's reporting, its integration with other features, and its familiarity to users. The architect should not create a custom object that duplicates a standard one, because that splits data and breaks the ecosystem.

The quiz tests this directly. One question describes a business concept that is a customer, where the solution needs the standard reporting and integration that come with it, and asks what the best object choice is. The answer is the standard Account object. A customer maps to the standard Account object, which brings the built-in reporting, integration, and familiarity the solution needs. A custom object duplicates the standard one and splits the data. A custom setting and a custom metadata type are for configuration, not for customer records.

The mapping is the key. When the business concept matches the object's purpose, use the standard object. A customer is an Account. A person is a Contact. A sales opportunity is an Opportunity. A support issue is a Case. The match means the solution inherits everything the standard object brings: reporting, integration, familiarity. The users already know how to work with an Account. The platform already knows how to report on it. The integration with other features already exists.

The trap is creating a custom object that duplicates a standard one. The team creates a custom Customer object because it wants control, and the data is split. Some customer data lives in the custom object, some in the standard Account, and there are two sources of truth. The standard object's reporting and integration are lost, and the team maintains two sources of truth. The custom object did not add value; it split the data and broke the ecosystem.

### Custom objects are the right choice when the business concept has no standard home

Custom objects are the right choice when the business concept has no standard home or when the standard object's behavior would fight the requirement. A product configuration, a claim, a policy, or a specialized document record often has no clean standard object. The architect should also consider custom when the standard object's required fields, record types, or sharing behavior would force compromises. A custom object gives the team the shape it needs without the baggage.

The quiz tests this directly. One question describes a business concept, such as a product configuration, that has no clean standard object and where the standard objects would force compromises, and asks what the best choice is. The answer is to create a custom object that matches the requirement. When a concept has no clean standard home and standard objects would force compromises, a custom object gives the team the shape it needs without the baggage. Forcing it into a standard object creates workarounds. A custom label and a custom setting are for configuration, not for transactional records.

The trigger is the absence of a standard home. A product configuration, a claim, a policy, a specialized document record, these often have no clean standard object. There is no standard object for a product configuration, so the team builds a custom one that matches the requirement. The custom object gives the team the shape it needs, without the baggage of a standard object that does not fit.

The other trigger is a standard object whose behavior would fight the requirement. The standard object's required fields, record types, or sharing behavior would force compromises. The team would spend more effort working around the standard object than it would have spent building a custom one. In that case, custom is the better choice. The custom object gives the team the shape it needs without the baggage.

### The decision must consider the OmniStudio artifacts that will use the object

The decision must consider the OmniStudio artifacts that will use the object. An OmniScript that writes to an object needs the object's fields to match the script's data, and an Integration Procedure that transforms data needs the object's relationships to be present. The architect should validate the object design against the artifacts' fetch, transform, and save requirements before finalizing it, because an object that does not support the artifacts forces rework.

The artifacts are the consumers of the object, and the object must serve them. An OmniScript that writes to an object needs the object's fields to match the script's data. If the script collects a field the object does not have, the save fails or the data is lost. An Integration Procedure that transforms data needs the object's relationships to be present. If the IP needs to relate records and the relationship does not exist, the transform cannot do its job. A FlexCard that displays data needs the sharing model to work for its display.

The validation is the step that prevents rework. The architect checks the object design against the artifacts' fetch, transform, and save requirements before finalizing it. The fields match the OmniScript's data. The relationships support the Integration Procedure's transforms. The sharing model works for the FlexCard's display. If the object does not support the artifacts, the design is reworked before the artifacts are built, not after.

The trap is designing the object without validating it against the artifacts. An object that does not support the OmniScript's data or the IP's relationships forces rework after the artifacts are built. The artifacts are built on an object that cannot serve them, and the team has to go back and change the object or the artifacts. Validate the design against the artifacts before finalizing it, and the rework is avoided.

### Relationships are part of the object design

Relationships are part of the object design. The architect must decide whether a relationship is a lookup or a master-detail,[^2] considering the cascade behavior, the sharing implications, and the reporting. A master-detail relationship cascades deletes and controls access, while a lookup is more flexible. The choice affects how the OmniScript and Integration Procedure read and write the related data, so it should be made deliberately, not by default.

The quiz tests this directly. One question describes an architect choosing between a lookup and a master-detail relationship for related records, and asks what should drive the decision. The answer is the cascade, sharing, and reporting needs of the relationship. The choice between lookup and master-detail should be driven by the cascade, sharing, and reporting needs, because master-detail cascades deletes and controls access while a lookup is more flexible. The default is not always master-detail. Field count and object name do not determine the relationship type.

The two relationship types behave differently. A master-detail relationship is a parent-child relationship where the master object controls the detail, and it cascades deletes and controls access.[^3] When the master is deleted, the detail records are deleted too, and the master controls who can view the detail's data. A lookup relationship links two objects but has no effect on deletion or security, and it is more flexible.[^3] The detail in a lookup can stand alone.

The choice is driven by the needs. If the related records should be deleted when the parent is deleted, and the parent should control access, master-detail is the choice. If the related records should stand alone and the relationship should be flexible, lookup is the choice. The cascade, sharing, and reporting needs drive the decision. The default is not always master-detail, and field count and object name do not determine the relationship type.

The choice affects the artifacts. An OmniScript and an Integration Procedure read and write the related data, and the relationship type changes how they do it. A master-detail relationship controls access and cascades deletes, so the artifacts inherit that behavior. A lookup is more flexible, so the artifacts have more freedom. The choice should be made deliberately, not by default, because it shapes what the artifacts can do.

### The object design should be validated against the platform's limits and the solution's growth

The object design should be validated against the platform's limits and the solution's growth. Field count, relationship depth, and record volume all have limits in the current release, and an object design that is fine for a demo can fail at scale. The architect should design the object with headroom, avoid over-normalizing into many small objects that complicate the OmniScript, and avoid under-normalizing into a few wide objects that hit field limits.

The limits are real, and they change. Field count, relationship depth, and record volume all have limits in the current release. An object design that is fine for a demo can fail at scale, when the record volume grows or the field count is exhausted. The architect should check the current release limits and design with headroom, so the object does not hit a wall as the solution grows.

The design should also avoid the two extremes. Over-normalizing into many small objects complicates the OmniScript, because the script has to navigate many objects and relationships to do its work. Under-normalizing into a few wide objects hits field limits, because a single object can only hold so many fields. The architect should find the balance: enough objects to model the data cleanly, not so many that the artifacts are complicated, and not so few that the objects are too wide.

## Config Walkthrough

Design the objects in four steps.

1. For each logical entity, decide standard or custom. Choose a standard object when the business concept matches its purpose, and a custom object when there is no standard home or the standard behavior would fight the requirement. A customer is an Account. A product configuration is a custom object. Make the trade-off between fit and control deliberately.

2. Validate the object design against the artifacts. Confirm the fields match the OmniScript's data, the relationships support the Integration Procedure's transforms, and the sharing model works for the FlexCard's display. An object that does not support the artifacts forces rework, so validate before finalizing.

3. Choose relationships deliberately. Decide lookup versus master-detail based on cascade, sharing, and reporting needs, not by default. Master-detail cascades deletes and controls access. Lookup is more flexible. Let the needs of the relationship drive the choice.

4. Design with headroom. Check field count, relationship depth, and record volume against the current release limits, and avoid both over-normalization and under-normalization. Design the object so it does not fail at scale.

## Exam Traps

Three traps dominate this objective.

First, creating a custom object that duplicates a standard one. The data is split, the standard object's reporting and integration are lost, and the team maintains two sources of truth. When the business concept matches a standard object's purpose, use the standard object. Do not create a custom object that duplicates it.

Second, choosing a standard object whose required fields and behavior fight the requirement. The team spends more effort working around the standard object than it would have spent building a custom one. When the standard object's behavior would force compromises, choose a custom object that matches the requirement.

Third, designing the object without validating it against the OmniStudio artifacts. An object that does not support the OmniScript's data or the IP's relationships forces rework after the artifacts are built. Validate the object design against the artifacts' fetch, transform, and save requirements before finalizing it.
