# Lesson 89: Your 90-day roadmap: hands-on practice plan, what to study next

Lesson 88 rehearsed the performance: the preparation clock, the four artifacts, a presentation running order, and the questions waiting in the last forty minutes. Rehearsing once is not preparation. Preparation is a schedule that produces a scored run every week and tells you the date to stop.

The part most candidates handle badly is the calendar. The program sets it before you do. Candidates must be ready to appear for the evaluation two to four weeks after they register, and a request to push that date means the Architect Evaluation will be canceled and the candidate will need to re-register when they are prepared. [^2] Pass, and the Technical Architect team schedules the Review Board Exam with a pre-meeting in the next two to four months. Fail, and feedback arrives with prescriptive next steps plus a six month wait before re-registering. [^2] Nothing in that sequence rewards optimism.

So a 90-day plan has three jobs. Find out what you cannot yet do. Practice doing it under the exam clock. Arrive at a registration decision holding evidence instead of a feeling.

## The fixed points your calendar has to respect

Seven program rules constrain the shape of any roadmap, and most of them are dates rather than study topics. Read them once, then decide which ones your plan bends around.

| Fixed point | Program rule | What it forces in the plan |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prerequisite credentials | Current System Architect and Application Architect certifications are required to register, and the Architect Maintenance module has to be completed to stay current [^1] [^2] | Confirm both credentials and the maintenance module before anything else goes on the calendar |
| CTA601 timing | Students should attend this course at least four months before they plan to register for the Architect Evaluation [^4] | If the course is not behind you, registration moves out, not the course |
| Registration window | A candidate must be ready to appear for the evaluation two to four weeks after registering [^2] | The registration date is a commitment, so the plan works backwards from it |
| Evaluation format | Online only, with 60 minutes to read, solution, design and justify, 30 minutes to present, 30 minutes for questions and 45 minutes of feedback [^1] | Practice runs should use the evaluation clock before they use the board clock |
| After a pass | An evaluation pass makes you eligible for the Review Board Exam, scheduled with a pre-meeting within two to four months [^2] | Keep the following quarter flexible |
| After a fail | Feedback with prescriptive next steps and a six month wait before re-registering [^2] | A rushed registration can cost half a year, which is the real price of guessing |
| Fees | 1,500 dollars for the evaluation with a 750 dollar retake, and 4,500 dollars for the Review Board Exam with a 2,250 dollar retake [^1] | Budget for a retake on the evaluation and treat it as part of the plan |

Two rows change behavior more than the rest. The four month course rule means CTA601 is a prerequisite in practice even where it is only recommended in writing. The two to four week readiness window means registration is not the start of your preparation; it is the end of it. [^2] [^4]

There is a third constraint worth naming because it is easy to skip. The credential is two steps, and the first one runs on a compressed clock. The Architect Review Board Evaluation gives you 60 minutes to read, solution, design and justify a detailed architectural solution, 30 minutes to present recommendations, 30 for question and answer, and 45 minutes of feedback and discussion. [^1] Practice on the short clock as well as the long one, because the evaluation is the gate you meet first.

## Define the terms

**Diagnostic run.** The first timed practice of the plan, taken before any studying, scored the same way a mock board is scored. Its only purpose is to replace your guess about your weak areas with a number.

**Rep.** One scored artifact produced inside its time budget: a data model, a system landscape, an integration flow, or an authorization flow design. Those four are the artifact types the CTA601 workshop teaches, which is a reasonable definition of what the presentation has to carry. [^4]

**Constraint table.** Every number, named system, population, regulation and date pulled out of the scenario, in one place. It is the artifact that proves your solution came from the scenario.

**Practice run.** A timed block that produces the full artifact set for one scenario, scored afterwards. No presentation, no questions.

**Mock board.** A practice run followed by a 45 minute presentation and 40 minutes of questions, delivered to someone who will not help you, then scored. The number of these you complete is the honest measure of the plan.

**Score log.** One row per rep, per practice run and per mock board, with the date, the scenario, the criteria scored, and the single weakest thing observed. The log is what turns four weeks of practice into a study list.

**Readiness evidence.** The three scored outputs that justify registering: a complete constraint table on an unseen scenario, a full artifact set produced inside 180 minutes, and a mock board that cleared the scoring gate.

**Study block.** A 90 minute unit aimed at one weak signal from the log, ending with a one page note: the pattern, the alternative you rejected, and the failure path.

## Days 1 to 30: diagnose before you choose what to study

Set aside three hours and take a scenario you have never seen. Community sample scenarios exist for this, and the Architect Trailblazer Community is where Salesforce points candidates for them. [^1] [^3] The one rule that matters on day one is that you do not open a study guide first.

Salesforce's own account of why candidates struggle is blunt: experienced architects fail mock boards not because they lack knowledge, but because their communication lacks clarity, and the mental model that connects the pieces is missing. [^5] A diagnostic run exposes exactly that, and it is the cheapest measurement you will ever take, because the alternative measurement is a registration you were not ready for.

1. **Pick the scenario and set the clock.** Use 120 minutes for the first run if 180 feels impossible, and write down the clock you used so the next run can be compared.
2. **Produce the artifacts you would present.** Data model, system landscape, integration flow, and the authorization flows. Rough is fine. Missing is the point.
3. **Score it with the criteria from lesson 88.** Constraint coverage, decision log depth, numbers sheet arithmetic, whether the recommendation appears in the first minute, whether answers open with a sentence, and whether you named anything you decided not to solve.
4. **Map every miss to an outline area.** The reviewed areas are System Architecture, Security, Data, Solution Architecture, Integration, Development Lifecycle and Deployment Planning, and Communication. [^3]
5. **Write the log row and stop.** No studying on the same day. The first pass is a measurement, not a lesson.

Then translate the misses into a plan with this table.

| Signal from the diagnostic | Outline area it belongs to | What weeks 2 to 4 should do about it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Constraints that never made it out of the scenario | Communication and System Architecture [^3] | Two constraint-extraction drills a week on unseen scenarios, table only, 30 minutes each |
| A diagram nobody else could read | Communication [^3] | Draw the in-scope data models from memory, then check them against the Data Model Gallery [^5] |
| Volume figures quoted but never used in arithmetic | Data [^3] | Size one operation per rep: which query, which load, which API ceiling |
| Sharing or visibility answered in a sentence | Security [^3] | Work the sharing model questions from Fundamentals, then write the four layer answer for one portal |
| Integration named as a product | Integration [^3] | Rebuild the same answer with a named pattern and one rejected alternative [^5] |
| No failure path anywhere in the solution | Solution Architecture and Integration [^3] | Add the degradation sentence to every answer before the time runs out |
| No test strategy mentioned at all | Development Lifecycle and Deployment Planning [^3] | Write the test strategy as five lines: what is tested, by whom, at which environment, and what blocks a release [^3] |

Notice that only one row is a knowledge problem in the narrow sense. The rest are production problems, and they get fixed by producing under the clock.

## Days 31 to 60: produce instead of reading

The middle thirty days run the same week five times. Two study blocks, two timed sprints, one constraint drill, and a written log entry each time.

1. **Two study blocks, 90 minutes each.** One weak area per block, chosen from the log rather than from the outline in order. Each block ends with the one page note.
2. **Two artifact sprints, 45 minutes each.** Take one scenario and produce one artifact pair: for example a data model with a system landscape, or an integration flow with its authorization flow. The pairing matters, because the judges score the seams.
3. **One constraint drill, 30 minutes.** Read a fresh scenario and produce nothing but the constraint table. This is the cheapest rep you can run and the one most likely to move your score.
4. **One defense rep, 20 minutes.** Answer three questions out loud in the decision, reason, trade-off shape, then check whether each answer opened with a sentence.
5. **One log entry.** Date, scenario, artifact, score, weakest observation.

Six weeks of that produces the rotation below. Adjust the order to your diagnostic, not to taste.

| Week | Focus | Artifacts produced | Scored test at the end |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5 | Data, second weak area | Data model plus large volume sizing | Every figure in the scenario appears in arithmetic |
| 6 | Security, first weak area | Sharing model plus authorization flows | Four layer answer, declarative mechanism named first |
| 7 | Integration | Integration flow plus pattern selection | One named pattern, one rejected alternative, one failure path |
| 8 | Mix, all seven areas | One full practice run at 180 minutes | Full artifact set produced inside the clock |
| 9 | Communication | Presentation skeleton and a five minute delivery | Recommendation stated in the first 60 seconds |
| 10 | Repair of the two worst signals | Re-work of the two weakest artifacts | Both artifacts clear their scoring criteria |

How much is enough depends on hours, and hours decide mock boards rather than reading.

| Hours per week | Weekly composition | Mock boards you can fit in sixty days |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 8 | 4 study blocks, 2 sprints, 1 constraint drill | 2, one in week 8 and one in week 12 |
| 12 | 6 study blocks, 3 sprints, 1 constraint drill | 3, adding a mid-plan board in week 6 |
| 20 | 8 study blocks, 4 sprints, 1 constraint drill, 1 mock board | 4 or more, with the extra hours going to presentation and defense reps |

The arithmetic is the argument. At eight hours a week you have two mock boards before registering, and a mock board costs about five hours once you count preparation, presentation, questions and scoring. If a plan needs more evidence than that, the answer is more weeks, not fewer hours of sleep. Nobody should register on a single mock board.

One more rule for this block. Every fake scenario you practice on should be read twice, once for constraints and once for the sentence that tells you which system is authoritative. Candidates who miss that sentence produce designs that sound right and answer the wrong question.

## Days 61 to 90: simulate, then commit

The final thirty days are for simulation and paperwork, in that order.

**Mock board two, around day 63.** Full format: 180 minutes, 45 minutes of presentation, 40 minutes of questions. Use a person who has never seen the scenario and will not fill your silences. Salesforce recommends presenting part of a solution to an audience unfamiliar with it, which is the same thing at smaller scale. [^5]

**Mock board three, around day 78.** Same format, different scenario, ideally a different person. Score it yourself against the same criteria and compare the two logs. Progress on the constraint table and on the first sixty seconds of the presentation is the signal that the plan is working.

**Defense drills between the boards.** Present nothing and answer everything. Six questions, each answered in one sentence plus a reason. The 40 minutes of questions is where an answer shape wins or loses points, so it deserves its own reps rather than whatever is left after the presentation.

**The readiness gate.** The readiness gate is a scored mock board in which a person who will not help you sat through the presentation and put the questions to you, plus a complete constraint table on an unseen scenario and a full artifact set produced inside the clock. If all three are not in the log, registration waits.

**The stop rule.** Write the rule down before the last mock, not after it. Something like: if the third mock board still fails the constraint coverage criterion, registration moves out a month and the constraint drill becomes the daily block. A stop rule written in advance costs you a month. A registration surrendered to the program costs six. [^2]

**Registration logistics, in the last week.** Confirm both prerequisite credentials and the maintenance module, register through Webassessor, budget the evaluation fee and the retake, and clear the two to four week window in your work calendar. [^1] [^2] Note the formats while you are there: the evaluation is online only, and the Review Board Exam offers online and in-person options. [^1]

**Do not add a new domain in the last two weeks.** New material in the final stretch costs presentation reps, and presentation reps are what the judges score.

## What to study next, and in what order

Order beats effort here. Four rules cover most decisions.

**Fix domains that appear in every scenario before domains that appear in one.** Integration, security and data carry the heaviest weight in most scenarios, so a weak signal in any of them outranks a missing feature elsewhere. [^3]

**Pair every weak domain with the artifact where it shows up.** A sharing model weakness shows up in the authorization flow. A data weakness shows up in the sizing sheet. Practicing the domain without its artifact produces knowledge you cannot present.

**After each mock board, go back to the Fundamentals, not forward to new features.** Salesforce names this as the way to use both resources: take the feedback from a mock board to find where the reasoning was weakest, then use Fundamentals to deepen it. Candidates report topics feeling straightforward before the first mock that take on new importance after the third. [^5]

**Compress the decisions, then test the whole solution.** Decision guides exist to speed up decision point identification and to make the justification shorter and clearer. [^5] The Well-Architected Framework then tests the assembled design: use each pillar as a question set, and use the framework's trade-off pairs, such as Trust against Resource and Cost Optimization, to say out loud which quality you spent to buy another. [^6] It is a living document with a change log, so check it rather than trusting a downloaded copy. [^6]

| Weak signal | Domain | Artifact to produce | Resource to work | Weekly test |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Volume with no operation attached | Data | Sizing sheet | Large data volume guidance over the scenario's own numbers [^3] | Every figure used in arithmetic |
| Access answered with layouts | Security | Authorization flows | Sharing and visibility fundamentals, then the four layer answer [^5] | Declarative mechanism named first |
| Pattern chosen by habit | Integration | Integration flow | Integration patterns and the decision factors behind them [^5] | One rejected alternative per answer |
| Solution reads as separate answers | All seven | One landscape diagram | Well-Architected pillars as a review checklist [^6] | Two pillar trade-offs named in the presentation |

## Exam traps

**Registering because a slot was open.** The two to four week readiness window turns a convenience into a deadline, and cancelling it means re-registering from scratch. [^2]

**Reading more and producing less.** Notes do not appear in the log. If a week produced no artifact, it produced no evidence.

**Practicing with tools the exam does not give you.** The permitted kit is fixed, short, and does not include templates. [^3] Practice inside that box, or you will spend the first twenty minutes of the real thing rebuilding muscle memory you should already have.

**Practicing without a clock.** Untimed reps teach a shape that does not survive contact with 180 minutes. The timings of all three blocks are published, so there is no excuse to guess. [^1] [^3]

**A mock board run by someone who helps you.** Questions from a friendly colleague are not the questions. The 40 minute block is designed to be adversarial about your decisions. [^3]

**Treating the presentation as the whole exam.** For the Review Board, 45 minutes of presentation is followed by 40 minutes of questions, and defending a recommendation is part of the graded work rather than an appendix to it. [^3]

**Skipping the short clock.** The evaluation gives 60 minutes to solution and design and 30 minutes to present, and it is the step you meet first. Practicing only the 180 minute board leaves you unprepared for the gate you actually walk through. [^1]

**No log.** Without dated scores you cannot tell progress from familiarity, and familiarity is what makes candidates register too early.

**No stop rule.** A plan without a written abort condition is a plan that registers on schedule regardless of evidence.

## Try it

Block 60 minutes today and build the calendar. Do not perfect it, finish it.

1. **Write the two candidate windows.** One for registration, one for the evaluation two to four weeks after it, and check that CTA601 sits at least four months before the registration date. [^2] [^4]
2. **Schedule the diagnostic run** in the next seven days, with a scenario you have not read and a hard 120 minute clock.
3. **Name three mock boards** with dates and people, and confirm each person will not help you.
4. **Build the weekly template.** Blocks, sprints, constraint drill, defense rep. Write the times of day, not the intentions.
5. **Create the score log.** Columns: date, scenario, artifact, criteria scored, weakest observation, next action.
6. **Write the stop rule** as one sentence with a consequence and a date.
7. **Write the study list from the outline, not from a syllabus.** Seven areas, ranked by how often you missed them in the diagnostic, and nothing else. [^3]

Then run week one as written. The most useful thing a 90-day roadmap can do in its first seven days is prove that you will actually run week two.

## What's next

Lesson 90 closes the series. It is the capstone synthesis: **General systems architecture: building your own enterprise AI**. The method you have been rehearsing for the last ninety days, naming the authoritative system, choosing a pattern, stating the trade-off, naming the failure path, and pricing the maintenance load, is the same method an enterprise AI platform demands. Lesson 90 applies it end to end to a platform you would build yourself, drawing together the data, integration, security, latency and governance material from earlier lessons into one design, which makes it the right place to see how the whole series fits together before you go and use it on a real scenario.
