# Lesson 88: Exam prep: CTA review and scenario practice

Lesson 87 read the published exam guides as scope documents and turned them into a study plan: weights, scenario stems, exclusions, weaknesses. That plan gets you into the room. This lesson is about the room.

The Architect Review Board Exam is a performance. The candidate reviews and solves a hypothetical scenario, presents the solution to a panel of CTA judges, and then answers their questions about it. [^1] You get 180 minutes to review and prepare, 45 minutes to present, and 40 minutes in question and answer. [^1] Everything you bring out of the preparation block has to fit in the materials you are handed: the scenario itself as a Google Doc, a Google Slide deck, a Google Sheet, a blank Lucidchart with access to the Salesforce Shape Library, and an onscreen timer. No other materials are allowed, and Lucidchart templates are not permitted for any part of the certification. [^1]

Two lines in that exam guide decide how you should practice. The first sets the format. The second says that other products and apps can be referenced in a solution, but the candidate must be able to defend any recommendation appropriately. [^1] Take that literally. A design gets its points in the answer, not in the choice, and the answer has to survive a room of people who have run these designs in production.

## What the day actually looks like

| Block | Time | What it produces |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scenario review and solution preparation | 180 minutes | A solution, a design and the justification for both, expressed in the four artifacts |
| Presentation | 45 minutes | A recommendation the judges can follow without asking who owns what |
| Questions and answers | 40 minutes | The defense of every decision you just presented |

The scenario arrives as a Google Doc, so work in it. Highlight every number, every named system, every regulator, every user population and every date. Those highlights become the constraint table you present from, and the constraint table is the difference between an answer that came from the scenario and an answer that came from habit.

Before any of that, there is a gate. The CTA credential is earned in two steps: the Architect Review Board Evaluation, then the Architect Review Board Exam, and both require current Application Architect and System Architect certifications. [^2] The evaluation runs on a compressed clock, with 60 minutes to read, solution, design and justify a detailed architectural solution, 30 minutes to present recommendations, 30 minutes for question and answer, and 45 minutes of additional feedback and discussion. [^2] It costs $1,500 with a $750 retake, against $4,500 for the Review Board Exam with a $2,250 retake. [^2]

Read those two price tags together and the evaluation looks like what it is: the cheaper dress rehearsal for the real thing, on a shorter clock, with feedback attached. The catch is timing. Candidates must be ready to sit the evaluation within two to four weeks of registering, and asking to push the date cancels the registration. Pass, and the Technical Architect team schedules the Review Board Exam and a pre-meeting within the next two to four months. Fail, and you receive feedback with prescriptive next steps, then wait six months before re-registering. [^3] Practice happens before you register, not after. The same FAQ sets the reschedule windows at 72 hours for the evaluation and 30 days for the Review Board Exam, and a no-show is charged the full exam fee. [^3]

## Define the terms

**Scenario.** The hypothetical customer you are handed. It is deliberately incomplete, and the incompleteness is the exam. Every version contains numbers, named systems, populations, regulations and dates, and those are the constraints your decisions have to trace back to.

**Solution.** The set of components and interactions that satisfy the requirements: which system is authoritative for which fact, what data lives on the platform, how systems exchange it, who can see what, and how the whole thing gets built and released.

**Design.** The detail that shows the solution works. Objects, relationships, volumes, interfaces, identity paths, environments. In the exam it is what goes into the data model and the landscape diagram.

**Justification.** The sentence that names why your choice beat the alternative you rejected. Every recommendation needs one, because the exam guide requires the candidate to defend any recommendation made in a solution. [^1]

**Decision log.** A table you keep during the 180 minutes with one row per decision: the decision, the options considered, the mechanism chosen, the scenario constraint that drove it, the trade-off accepted, and what happens when it fails. It is your presentation outline and your answer sheet at the same time.

**Recommended solution, not options.** Guidance published to candidates is explicit about the presentation: present your recommended solutions and not options, justify why the solution should be used, keep the solutions relevant to the scenario, and practice delivering concise answers that name the risks and considerations. [^6]

**Mock board.** A timed run of the full format in front of people who will ask the hard questions, recorded so you can watch yourself spend four minutes on a decision that needed one.

**Deliberate exclusion.** Something real in the scenario that you have chosen not to solve in this design, with the reason stated out loud. Naming it is scored. Leaving it unmentioned invites the judge to find it for you.

## The 180 minutes, split into four decisions

Most candidates lose the Review Board to the clock rather than to knowledge. Budget the three hours before you spend them.

| Minutes | Work | Output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 to 30 | Read for the story, then again for constraints | An annotated scenario with every number, system, population, regulator and date marked |
| 30 to 55 | Name the decisions | A decision log with 10 to 15 rows: authority, systems, org strategy, identity, record access, data model, volumes, retention, integration patterns, licensing, environments, release, testing, risk |
| 55 to 110 | Design the two artifacts the judges read | Data model and system landscape diagram |
| 110 to 150 | Build the narrative | The slide deck, written in the order you will present it |
| 150 to 172 | Do the arithmetic | Volumes, API consumption, storage, license counts and the latency budget on the sheet |
| 172 to 180 | Rehearse out loud | Your opening two minutes and the three answers you trust least |

The order matters more than the numbers. The diagram is the artifact that eats time, so it gets a hard stop. A data model built from the standard objects you can already reproduce is faster and more credible than one invented from scratch, and the published data model gallery is a legitimate starting point for it. [^4] The deck is transcription once the decision log exists, which is why the log comes before the slides rather than after. The arithmetic block is not bookkeeping: if the scenario hands you a call limit and a peak volume, the sheet is where you show you noticed both.

Rehearsing the opening matters because the first two minutes fix the judges' model of the scenario. Restate the constraints in the customer's language, name the decisions you are about to present, then start. Candidates who open with an architecture diagram make the panel assemble the problem themselves, and panels that are assembling are not yet convinced.

## Reading a scenario under the clock

Work on a full-sized scenario rather than a fragment. Here is one.

> **Ridgeline Freight** moves 28 million shipments a year under two acquired brands, each of which brought its own Salesforce org. Rating and billing stay in an on-premise ERP that the client owns, and the ERP exposes roughly 500 API calls a day. 3,200 internal users work in the CRM. 900 partner carriers log in to a portal to see and update their own shipments, and turnover in that carrier population runs at about 20 percent a year. Field technicians use a mobile app, often with no coverage. A regulator requires shipment documents and their audit history to be retained for seven years. The chief operating officer wants an executive dashboard that answers in under three seconds. The CIO has asked whether AI can triage the 40,000 inbound case emails a month. Go-live is nine months away, and the security team will not approve a design it has not reviewed.

Two passes produce the table that carries the rest of the exam.

| Constraint in the scenario | The decision it forces |
| --- | --- |
| Rating and billing authoritative in the ERP, 500 calls a day | Which integration pattern, and the volume arithmetic that proves it fits |
| Two brands, two orgs, shared carriers | Single or multi-org, and who owns identity in each |
| 900 external carriers with 20 percent turnover | License type, portal authentication, provisioning method, external sharing mechanism |
| Seven-year retention with audit history | Data lifecycle, archiving target and immutable audit records |
| Three-second dashboard | A reporting layer separated from the operational objects |
| Field technicians offline | Sync scope and conflict handling |
| 40,000 case emails a month, AI triage question | Scope control, and an honest answer about measurement before automation |
| Nine months, security review before approval | Delivery lifecycle and governance, which are scored domains |

That table takes about 30 minutes and gives you most of a presentation. Everything after it is deciding rather than discovering.

**The weak answer.** "We integrate the ERP with Salesforce using real-time callouts so billing is always current, and we connect the two orgs through a shared integration layer." It names technologies, contradicts the 500-call limit it was just given, and leaves the org question open while the schedule burns.

**The scoring answer.** "Billing stays authoritative in the ERP. Rating and invoice status are reference data with an hourly tolerance, so I synchronize them into a read-only object on a scheduled job and batched reconciliation rather than making the CRM wait on an ERP call, which keeps daily consumption inside the 500-call ceiling. Two orgs is the wrong default: the brands share carriers, share the ERP and share one security team, so the answer is one org with brand-specific record types and a shared role hierarchy, and the acquisition that needs its own org is the one with a regulator of its own. Carriers authenticate through their own identity provider with just-in-time provisioning, because manual account creation does not survive 20 percent turnover, and their record visibility comes from a sharing set scoped to their carrier account rather than from opening the object. Field technicians synchronize only their assigned shipments, with a stated conflict rule and a queue for exceptions, because syncing the full shipment list to a device turns a mobile requirement into a data volume problem. On the AI question, I would fund a pilot on a single queue with a measured precision target before any automatic routing, and keep customer-visible actions out of the first phase."

Both answers take the same amount of time to say. One of them contains the authority sentence, the pattern, the arithmetic, a rejected alternative, a failure rule and a boundary on optional scope.

## The 45-minute presentation

The presentation is not the solution read aloud. Salesforce's guidance to candidates is to present recommended solutions rather than options and to justify each one, which changes the deck: every section leads with a decision. [^6]

| Slides | Minutes | Content |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 to 2 | 3 | The scenario restated as constraints, in the customer's language |
| 3 | 5 | The picture: systems, what is authoritative where, and what sits on and off the platform |
| 4 to 6 | 12 | Data and integration: model, volumes, patterns, failure paths |
| 7 to 8 | 8 | Security and identity: the two populations, the access model, the license types |
| 9 | 5 | Delivery and lifecycle: environments, release plan, testing, who approves production |
| 10 | 4 | Risks, assumptions and deliberate exclusions |
| 11 | 4 | The recommendation restated as decisions with their trade-offs |

That is 41 minutes of content in a 45-minute slot, and the remaining four always disappear into transitions and questions from the panel during the presentation itself. The blog post Salesforce publishes for candidates puts the pressure plainly: 45 minutes goes fast, and the right diagram can make or break the presentation. [^4] Borrow the landscape picture from the Reference Architecture Gallery and the model from the Data Model Gallery instead of drawing either from first principles, then simplify what you borrow until every box on the slide is a component you mention. [^4]

Two habits keep the presentation honest. Say the recommendation first, then the constraint that drove it, then the trade-off you accepted, and stop. If a slide has three recommendations, it has two too many for 90 seconds of talking. And keep the decision log beside the deck: when a judge interrupts, you answer from the log rather than from memory, and the log is already in decision, reason, trade-off shape.

## The 40 minutes of questions

The questions have patterns, and knowing them removes most of the surprise. Expect the simpler option, the failure case, the cost, the exclusion, the sequencing and the three-times-volume case.

- Why not the simpler option?
- What happens when the external system is unavailable?
- What does this cost, and who pays for it?
- What did you decide not to solve?
- How would you sequence this if the budget were halved?
- Where does this design break first at three times the volume?

Five rules make the answers score.

1. **Answer in one sentence first.** A 20-second question that draws a 90-second answer reads as uncertainty. The sentence is the decision, the rest is the reason.
2. **Trace every answer to a scenario constraint.** Judges are testing whether the decision came from the scenario or from habit, and the constraint is your evidence.
3. **Concede and re-decide when the judge is right.** "That is a better answer, and here is what changes" costs less than defending a decision you cannot support. The same applies when you misstate a limit: correct it in the next sentence.
4. **Never invent a number.** If you do not know a governor limit or a published ceiling, say how you would size it, name the operation it constrains, and say where you would verify it. The exam guide's communication objectives include handling unexpected roadblocks and determining the appropriate next steps, which is exactly this behavior. [^1]
5. **Keep the artifacts in front of you.** The sheet answers the volume question and the log answers the decision question. Reaching for either is faster and more accurate than reconstructing it out loud.

## Mock boards: the loop that moves the needle

One rehearsal is not practice. A loop is.

1. **Run the format alone and on the clock**, all 180, 45 and 40 minutes, and record it. You will find that the diagram ate an hour and the sheet is empty.
2. **Run a mock in front of two or three architects**, one of whom is instructed to disagree with your most expensive decision and keep disagreeing.
3. **Score the run against the Well-Architected Framework pillars**, using the published pattern and anti-pattern lists as a checklist: identity and access under Trust, scalability and capacity under Reliability, DevOps and observability under Operational Excellence, code quality and license optimization under Resource and Cost Optimization, accessibility under Fairness. [^5] Each gap is a named weakness instead of a feeling.
4. **Convert each gap into one source.** Salesforce's own guidance for candidates is to identify the weakest part of the solutioning with Fundamentals and Decision Guides rather than adding general reading. [^4] Decision guides exist to speed up decision points and sharpen justification, which are the two skills the board scores. [^4]
5. **Present one section to someone who does not work in Salesforce.** If the recommendation only lands with internal shorthand, the judges will spend their 40 minutes decoding you instead of testing you. [^4]

Salesforce publishes a practice scenario for the evaluation inside the Architect Trailblazers community, with instructions for using it: set a timer for the preparation time and for the presentation, present recommended solutions and not options, justify the choice, keep the solution relevant to the scenario, and practice concise delivery that names risks and considerations. [^6] That scenario is a legitimate input to the loop above, and the loop is where the loop's value lands.

## Exam traps

**Presenting options instead of a recommendation.** The candidate's published guidance says it directly: present recommended solutions and not options. [^6] A menu reads as an unresolved design.

**A recommendation with no rejected alternative.** "We chose a scheduled synchronization" scores less than the same sentence plus "we rejected per-record callouts because the scenario caps us at 500 calls a day."

**Drawing before deciding.** The diagram is scaffolding, not the answer. Candidates who open Lucidchart at minute 15 spend the preparation time on boxes and arrive at the presentation without justifications.

**No numbers.** Volumes, call limits, retention periods, latency targets and user populations appear in scenarios because they belong in answers.

**Restating the scenario instead of narrowing it.** Reading the scenario back for five minutes spends a tenth of the presentation telling the judges what they already know.

**Leaving the delivery lifecycle unresolved.** Environments, release plan, testing and who approves a production change are scored domains, and they are the ones most likely to be missing from a design-focused preparation. [^1]

**Treating the evaluation as a formality.** It runs on a shorter clock with the same format, and passing it is a prerequisite for the Review Board Exam. [^2]

**Reading the scenario once.** The constraints that change your design are usually on the second or third page, which is why the first 30 minutes are two passes rather than one.

**Defending a decision the judge has already beaten.** Take the point, state what changes, and finish. Refighting it costs time you need for the remaining questions.

## Try it

Take a full scenario, either the practice scenario Salesforce publishes or one you have written with a named external system of record, two user populations, a volume figure and a retention requirement. Then run the whole format once, in one sitting.

1. **Start a 180-minute timer.** Annotate the scenario, build the constraint table, write the decision log, then produce a data model, a landscape diagram, a slide deck and a numbers sheet. Stop when the timer stops, whatever state the artifacts are in.
2. **Present for 45 minutes** to one person who will sit through it without interrupting, working from the deck you built in that window.
3. **Ask them to put the six questions** from the previous section to you, and answer each one starting with a single sentence.
4. **Score the run** against the criteria below.

| Criterion | Pass condition |
| --- | --- |
| Constraint table | Every number in the scenario appears on it |
| Decision log | 10 or more rows, each with a trade-off and a failure path |
| Numbers sheet | Volume, API and latency arithmetic present, with the operation each constrains |
| Presentation | The recommendation is stated in the first 60 seconds |
| Answers | Every answer opens with a sentence rather than a preamble |
| Exclusions | At least three things you decided not to solve, named out loud |

Score it honestly. A thin constraint table is a reading problem, and no amount of diagram practice fixes a reading problem.
