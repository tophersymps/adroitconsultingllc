# Lesson 59: The Architecture Review Board: Presenting, Defending Tradeoffs, and Documenting Decisions

Earlier in this course you learned the *discipline* of review: design review as a forum, not a bottleneck, the anatomy of an architectural decision record, and the constructive no. This lesson is about the *institution* that makes that discipline durable. The architecture review board, the ARB, is the standing body that decides whether a proposed design lives. It is also the room where your architecture gets defended in public, under questions that will not stop until they are answered fairly. Learning to present a design there, to defend its tradeoffs without getting defensive, and to walk out with a documented decision is one of the highest-leverage skills a Salesforce System Architect can build, because a platform shaped only by whoever asked last is a platform with no direction at all.

There is an important distinction up front. Design review (lesson 22) is the *activity*: a structured session examining a proposed solution before it is built. The architecture review board is the *body*: a cross-disciplinary team with a charter, a cadence, and a documented decision process that runs those sessions at platform scale. An org can run an ad hoc design review with no board at all. The board is what you get when review stops being occasional and becomes institutional. [^1]

The board exists for a simple reason. The hardest problem in architecture is not designing good systems. It is getting a diverse group of humans to agree on one, and to agree on the record so the decision survives the people who made it. AWS's architecture guidance is blunt about what an ARB protects you from: without a formal process, differences in priorities across a team lead to prolonged debates and stalled projects. [^1] The board is the structured answer to that stall.

## Define the terms

- **Architecture review board (ARB)**: a multi-disciplinary team that reviews solution architectures to make sure they comply with enterprise guidelines, best practices, and supportability requirements. Members typically come from Security, Development, Enterprise Architecture, Infrastructure, and Operations. [^1]
- **Review checkpoint**: a defined point in the delivery lifecycle where an architecture is formally examined. AWS names two that matter most: after design and before a build or purchase decision, and again before deployment to confirm the reviewed architecture matches what was actually built. [^1]
- **Gate**: a checkpoint that carries authority. When the board's review is a gate, a design cannot proceed to implementation or purchase without its sign-off. [^1]
- **Executive sponsor**: a leader on the senior team who champions the board, draws in the right stakeholders, and makes the review a real gate rather than a suggestion. [^1]
- **Review shepherd**: the person who keeps the review process moving and understood across the org, liaises with sponsors on exceptions, and champions continuous improvement. The shepherd is not the single decision maker. [^1]
- **Tradeoff**: the acceptance that choosing option A gives up something option B offered. Every real architecture decision is a tradeoff; a design with no acknowledged tradeoffs has not actually been thought through.
- **Exception**: a time-boxed, documented departure from an enterprise guideline or policy, granted through a defined process and set to expire on a fixed date rather than running forever. [^1]
- **Decision documentation**: the record a board leaves behind, including approvals, reasons for recycling a design, exceptions required, and follow-ups owed, kept with the project's lifecycle documentation. [^1]

## Why a formal board beats hallway consensus

Any architect can point to the failure mode of "consensus by whoever talks loudest." What makes it hard to fix is that the failure is not technical. It is a negotiation problem among people who all want the org to succeed but define success differently. AWS describes the cast of characters that shows up when review is informal: the person who distrusts anything not built in-house, the reviewer who waits until the last minute to give feedback, the gatekeeper who insists every decision go through them, the designer who picks complexity for its own sake, the perfectionist who delays every decision, and the long-tenured historian whose memory is invaluable but who can keep the org looking backward. [^1]

Nothing about these personas is malicious. They are each a reasonable person defending a reasonable interest. But left unmanaged, the mix produces decisions by stalemate, or by whoever has the most stamina in the meeting. The board does not remove the humans. It structures how their disagreements get resolved: one design at a time, against a shared set of guidelines and best practices, on the record.

The real payoff is measurable. AWS lists what an effective board returns: improved compliance with enterprise standards and regulations, reduced technical debt because bad designs get caught in the design phase, operational efficiency from standardizing practices across the org, architectures built with supportability in mind, and, above all, security embedded from the outset rather than bolted on later. [^1] Note what is not on that list. "Fastest time to danger," "most clever solution," and "what one powerful stakeholder wanted" are absent, deliberately.

## Where review sits in the lifecycle

A board that meets at the wrong time is theater. The sequence matters. AWS's model places architecture review after the design phase and before a build or purchase decision, with an optional second review before deployment to confirm the built solution matches the reviewed one. [^1] The reason for the placement is economic: a design error corrected on a diagram costs hours; the same error corrected after implementation, or after purchase, costs rework that hits the schedule and the budget. [^1]

This is exactly the checkpoint model a Salesforce architect recognizes. Before solution design (lesson 55) turns into build, the board reviews the architecture. Before a package is bought or a big integration is built, the board signs off. And because governance in Salesforce horns against a real platform with real guardrails, the review answers against the Well-Architected capabilities: is this solution Trusted, Easy, and Adaptable? [^2]

![The two review checkpoints in the delivery lifecycle: after design and before build or purchase, then again before deployment to confirm the built solution matches what was reviewed](</diagrams/architecture-review-board-presenting-defending-tradeoffs-documenting-decisions/1-lifecycle-checkpoints.png>)

One pattern worth naming is the second checkpoint. The deploy-time review is easy to skip because the design was already approved, so this review looks purely ceremonial. It is not. AWS frames it as validating that what was reviewed and approved is what will actually ship. [^1] Build drifts from design: a team swaps a Platform Event for a direct callout mid-sprint, or wires data through a field nobody approved. The deploy review catches drift while it is still the same project, not a post-incident surprise.

## Presenting your design: what you are really asking for

Now the part that makes most architects uncomfortable. You have designed something. You believe in it. You have to stand in front of the board and win approval. The mistake people make is treating the presentation as a demonstration of how good the design is. It is not. It is a request for a decision, and the board's job is to test whether you have thought through the implications.

Reframe the session before you open your mouth. You are not there to prove your design is perfect. You are there to give the board enough to make an informed decision, and to show that the decision is worth making now. That reframe changes everything about how you prepare, because it tells you what the board actually needs from you: the context, the options you weighed, the recommendation, the tradeoffs, and the specific thing you are asking them to approve.

Your presentation should move in that order. Lay out the problem the design solves and the constraints that matter (time, budget, regulatory, data residency). Name at least two alternatives you considered for every major choice, and be ready to explain why you rejected each one. [^3] State your recommended approach and the reasoning behind it. Then name the tradeoffs you accepted, openly, before anyone has to drag them out of you. Close by stating the decision you want: "I am asking the board to approve a Platform Event integration with this replay strategy, in place of the synchronous callout, accepting eventual consistency within the analytics use case."

![The five-part presentation order: problem and constraints, at least two alternatives, recommendation and reasoning, tradeoffs named openly, then the specific decision you want the board to approve](</diagrams/architecture-review-board-presenting-defending-tradeoffs-documenting-decisions/2-presentation-flow.png>)

That last sentence matters more than the first ten minutes of slides. A board cannot approve a design it is not sure what you are asking it to approve. When the ask is crisp, the board can say yes quickly or no specifically, and either outcome is useful. A vague ask produces a vague session and an undocumented decision, which is no decision at all.

Supporting the presentation, Salesforce's guidance on intentional communication is the right frame: an architect is not an encyclopedia reciting answers but a detective asking the right questions, and responding too quickly risks misreading the need. [^4] That applies on both sides of the table. The presenter who listens to the question underneath a hard challenge answers the real concern instead of the surface one.

## Defending tradeoffs: the prepared mind

The board's questions will find your soft spots, and they should. That is the point. What separates a good defense from a collapse is not having a perfect design. It is having thought about the tradeoffs before the question lands.

The survival-guide frame is direct: prepared slides are not a prepared mind. For every major decision in your design you should already know at least two alternatives, why you rejected them, and what you gave up to pick the one you did. [^3] When the board asks "why event sourcing here, that is a debugging nightmare," the presenter who can say "accepted, it adds replay complexity, and here is what we traded for it" is in a fundamentally different position from the presenter who improvises. Preparing the mind means running the hard questions before the meeting: What happens at volumes you did not test? Where is the single point of failure? Who owns this when you leave? What does this cost to fix in two years?

Two disciplines keep the defense honest.

**Separate fact from judgment.** Some of your claims are facts you can point to: the platform limit, the API contract, the measured latency, the license cost. Some are judgment: this is the right pattern for our situation. When a claim is a fact, say so and show the source. When it is judgment, own that too instead of dressing it up as fact. The board respects an architect who knows which is which, because it tells them what to probe and what to accept.

**Change your mind on the record.** Defending a tradeoff does not mean defending the decision you walked in with. It means defending the reasoning. If a board member surfaces a true cost you underweighted, the strong response is not to argue it down; it is to weigh it honestly and, if it flips the balance, say so and revise. A design that survives the meeting unchanged because you never actually engaged with the objection is a design that was not reviewed, it was rubber-stamped by your own stubbornness.

The trap is the reverse. Defensiveness reads as weakness to a board that has seen a thousand pitches. When you go rigid the moment a design is challenged, you signal that you are protecting identity rather than reasoning. The members are not trying to kill your project. They are trying to prevent future disasters; they have been woken at 3 AM by the systems that failed the test you are being given. [^3] Treat a hard question as a gift: it is a findability drill on your own blind spots, run while the fix is still free.

## Handling the hard questions without going quiet

Some board members will not ask their concern; they will state a position and wait. Some will interrupt. Some will answer a small point as if it settles the whole design. You keep the session productive by doing one thing consistently: listen for the concern under the words, give it a real answer, and keep the focus on the decision at hand.

When you do not know the answer, say so plainly. "I have not checked that value; I will follow up within two days." That is a working answer, and the board can hold you to it. The alternative, a confident number that is invented, is the one thing that destroys trust faster than any wrong decision, because it tells the board you will lie to them under pressure. In this course the rule is absolute: a fabricated claim fails everywhere, in a lesson and in a review board alike. Say "checking" over "guessing" every time.

When a question is genuinely off-topic for the decision you asked for, name that without dismissing it. "That is a real constraint for the deploy phase; it is not what I am asking you to sign off on today, and I would like to keep the scope to this approval and take that up at the deploy review." You are not dodging. You are protecting the scope of the decision, which is your job as the presenter, and the board's job as the decider.

## Documenting the decision: the board's durable output

The session ends, and this is where most boards silently fail. A design review that produces nothing but a memory is a meeting, not a decision. AWS is explicit about what the board must leave behind: document each ARB action, including approvals, reasons for recycling a design, exceptions required, and follow-ups owed, and add those documented decisions to the project's lifecycle documentation so future inspection of the project or similar solutions has something to read. [^1]

This is the natural continuation of the decision-record discipline from lesson 22. The board's decision documentation is the ADR at institutional scale: it records what was approved, why, with what conditions, and what is owed afterward. A future architect reading the record should be able to answer why this integration exists, why this pattern won, and what tradeoffs were knowingly accepted, without re-arguing any of it from scratch. [^5]

Two details keep the documentation honest.

**Exceptions expire.** Not every design fits every guideline, and the board should have a process for that. But an exception is not a waiver in perpetuity. AWS is emphatic: set expiration dates on exceptions, because they exist to accommodate a temporary nonconformance while the team plans and implements a better long-term solution. [^1] An exception without an expiry becomes a permanent hole in governance that nobody remembers granting.

**Follow-ups have owners.** Every document action needs to point at someone and, ideally, at a date. "Review replay strategy at higher volume" is a sentiment. "Operations owner to validate replay under 5 million events, reviewed at the next board" is an action. The board documents the latter or it has documented nothing. [^1]

## Worked example: taking an integration choice through the board

A team building a self-service portal for field technicians proposes a synchronous integration. When a technician submits an inspection record, the org calls an external scheduling system in real time so the next job is assigned immediately. The design is good on its face: immediate, simple, one callout.

The Salesforce System Architect on the team brings it to the ARB for approval. She presents in order: the problem (technicians need next-job assignment now, not after a nightly batch), two alternatives (the synchronous callout and an event-driven queue), her recommendation (the callout) and her reasoning (simplicity, real-time assignment, low expected volume). Then she names the tradeoff before anyone can: a synchronous callout puts the external system on the critical path, so if scheduling is down, the inspection submission fails.

The board probes. The security architect asks what happens if the callout carries technician PII, and how external auth is handled. The architect answers with the named credential and JWT path from the security lessons in this course, and notes it is a fact she can show, not a judgment. [^6]

The platform architect asks the question that flips the design: "What happens when you add the three follow-on consumers marketing already told you about next quarter?" This is the prepared-mind moment. She knows the coupling cost, because she rehearsed this question. A synchronous callout means each new consumer is new callout code and new failure coupling; an event-driven path means publishing once and letting each consumer subscribe. [^3] She weighs it honestly on the record: the immediate-assignment benefit is real, but the org is about to add three consumers and cannot afford a per-consumer synchronous integration. She revises her recommendation before being forced to.

The board does not reject the feature. It approves an event-driven design: publish an event on submission, the scheduling system subscribes, and a bounded retry and dead-letter path handle the down-scheduler case, the reliability discipline from this series applied at review time. The architect documents the outcome: approval, the two alternatives considered and why the event path won, the requirement that each future consumer subscribe independently, and a follow-up owned by the operations lead to validate replay under projected volume, dated for the next board review. It lands in the lifecycle documentation. [^1]

![The board flips the integration from a synchronous callout to an event-driven queue: the callout puts the external system on the critical path, while publishing once lets each of the three follow-on consumers subscribe independently](</diagrams/architecture-review-board-presenting-defending-tradeoffs-documenting-decisions/3-sync-vs-event-driven.png>)

Six months later, a new architect joins and asks why this portal is asynchronous. The answer is in the record, with context, alternatives, reasoning, and follow-up status. Nobody has to remember, because the board made the decision, and the decision outlived the people who were in the room.

## The pitfall to avoid

**A board that approves everything, or a board whose decisions vanish when the meeting ends.** The two failures are the same disease at different stages. A board that reviews against nothing, or that lets the loudest member decide, produces a platform shaped by whoever was most present. A board that decides but never documents produces the identical outcome, because the decision does not survive to influence anything. The fix is the whole lesson: give the board real checkpoints and real authority, present a decision worth signing, defend it with a prepared mind instead of pride, and write down what was approved and what is owed. Any of these dropped, and the room was a meeting, not a review.

![The two board failures that are one disease: a board that decides nothing, or a board that decides but never documents, both leaving a platform shaped by whoever asked last](</diagrams/architecture-review-board-presenting-defending-tradeoffs-documenting-decisions/4-the-pitfall.png>)

## Try it

1. You are preparing an integration design for your ARB. The security architect is certain to ask about authentication. Which claims in your design are facts you can show (named credential, API contract, license cost) and which are judgment (this pattern fits us)? Write them into two lists before the session, so you know which to defend as data and which to defend as reasoning. [^4]

2. A product owner pushes for a synchronous callout because the dashboard "needs real time," and probing shows they need freshness within minutes, not seconds. Prepare the two-alternative answer: the synchronous callout and the event-driven option, with the tradeoff of each, so you can present both and let the tradeoff carry the reasoning. [^3]

3. The board approves your design with one condition: a two-month exception from the sandbox policy while a certain tool is in pilot. Your follow-up action is to put an expiration date on that exception and an owner on the re-review, because an exception granted indefinitely is a permanent hole in governance, not a temporary accommodation. [^1]

4. A board member asks for a hard number you do not actually know: the exact latency at a volume you have not tested. What do you say, and what does saying it signal to the board? *(Answer: say you have not checked it and will follow up within a set window, then actually follow up. A lying-but-confident number destroys trust faster than any wrong decision, and the honest "I will check" earns credibility under pressure.)*
