# Lesson 74: Monolith vs Microservices vs Platform: When Salesforce Is the Integration Hub

Lesson 73 handed you the shapes: routers, translators, and aggregators, the named building blocks of integration. It treated the system diagram as the given and asked where the shapes sit. This lesson steps back to the question it kept deferring. When you look at a whole enterprise, how many systems should there be, how should they connect, and which one should hold the shared business truth? Three answers dominate the vocabulary: one big monolith, many microservices, or a platform that mediates them all. This lesson defines each, separates two questions that people routinely collapse into one, and gives you the reasoning for when Salesforce should occupy the hub role in some enterprises and not others.

The mistake that derails most of these conversations is treating "monolith versus microservices" as the whole argument. It is not. That axis answers one question: how many deployable units does the code split into, and how independently do they run? There is a second, older, and for this course more important axis: which systems connect directly to which, and does anything sit in the middle of them? That is the hub-and-spoke question, and it is where "platform" enters. You can build a monolith that no other system talks to and be perfectly well integrated. You can also split your code into microservices that still connect point-to-point to a dozen partners, the same wiring a monolith would produce. The two decisions are independent, and a Salesforce architect who does not keep them separate will confuse a code-splitting debate with a topology debate and solve the wrong one.

## Define the terms

A **monolith** is one deployable unit that contains all of an application's logic. Its modules share a process, a codebase, a database, and a single deployment pipeline. The strength is coordination: a call from one module to another is a function call, not a network call, so a transaction can span modules with one source of truth and one rollback boundary. The cost is coupling. Because everything shares one process and one database, a change anywhere risks the whole, a memory leak in one module can take down the feature next to it, and the only scaling lever is to scale everything, the parts that need capacity and the parts that do not. [^1]

A **microservice** is a small, independently deployable unit that owns its own data and exposes a narrow interface. Services communicate over a network, synchronously through APIs or asynchronously through an event bus, and each one can be authored, versioned, and scaled on its own. The strengths are the inverse of the monolith's costs: independent scaling, independent team ownership, and blast-radius isolation, where a failure stays in the service that failed. The costs are the ones every distributed system charges: a function call becomes a network round trip, cross-service transactions become eventual consistency, and you take on orchestration, observability, and retry as first-class design problems rather than afterthoughts. [^1]

A **platform** is the third shape, and it is different in kind from the first two. Where monolith and microservice answer "how many units of code," a platform answers "what mediates." A platform is a managed, shared capability that other systems connect to instead of connecting point-to-point: the platform brokers traffic, owns a piece of the shared truth, and gives every constituent the same interface to reach it. The Salesforce platform is an application platform: multitenant, metadata-driven, and built so that many orgs run on shared infrastructure while each tenant's applications and data stay isolated. [^2]

Hold that distinction firmly. "Monolith versus microservices" is about the shape of one system's code. "Hub versus spoke" is about the shape of a network of systems. You can apply either answer on either axis independently. A Salesforce org can itself be architecturally monolith-like or split into services (often both, as you will see). And a set of systems can be mediated by a hub or left to talk to one another directly, whatever the code shape of each member happens to be. This lesson is mostly about the second axis, and it is the one the System Architect exam actually tests you on.

## Salesforce's own journey settles the muddle

The cleanest way to see the two axes at once is to look at what Salesforce did to its own platform. It started as a monolith: one runtime, one kernel, one database schema shared across every tenant. Over the past several years it evolved toward a structure of independent, metadata-driven services, while deliberately keeping the core runtime as the single system of record for application metadata because a monolithic core turns out to be the best shape for that one job. [^1]

Read that closely, because it is a real-world refusal of the "microservices everywhere" slogan. Salesforce did not throw the monolith out. It kept a monolith at the center, for the part of the system where coordination and one-version-of-truth actually matter (the metadata that describes every app), and it moved the parts that need the ability to scale and change independently out into their own services. It also described the communication between those services as asynchronous and brokered, which decouples them from each other and buffers load spikes between them. [^1]

That is the architect's actual skill, and it is the same in miniature at a customer: decide, capability by capability, whether it belongs in a coherent whole where it can share transactions, or in its own unit where it can scale and fail independently. You do not answer "monolith or microservices" once for the enterprise. You answer it repeatedly, per capability, and you place the boundaries where coordination cost and independence value cross.

## The platform, the hub, and the system of record

Now put the platform on the second axis. When enterprises talk about "the platform" in an integration context, they usually mean the thing in the middle: a system that many others connect to because it owns a shared business truth, or because it brokers the work between them. Salesforce is commonly that thing. Its integration patterns catalog is built around exactly this: remote process invocation, batch data synchronization, remote call-in, and data virtualization all describe Salesforce either reaching out to other systems or being reached into, and the patterns exist precisely so architects pick the right shape for the gap. [^3]

The unifying question is the system of record. Every set of connected systems has some entity that all of them care about: the customer, the order, the product, the account. The system of record is the owner of that entity, the one whose copy is authoritative and from which the others synchronize. Everything else is a spoke. When Salesforce is the integration hub, it is because Salesforce is the system of record for the entity that matters most, the customer and the customer's journey, and the other systems (ERP for orders, WMS for inventory, billing for balances) connect to it rather than to each other.

But notice what follows. If the official customer master lives in the on-prem ERP and Salesforce holds a synced copy for the sales team, then Salesforce is a spoke, not the hub. A proposal to "make Salesforce the integration hub" in that situation is really a proposal to move the system of record, or to add Salesforce as a convergence point in front of a truth that still lives elsewhere. Both are legitimate designs, but they are different designs, and the honest one names where the authoritative copy actually sits. Centralizing at a copy that will drift is how you get four systems that all believe they hold the customer.

## When Salesforce is the right hub

Salesforce is the right integration hub when the business process and the shared truth live naturally in Salesforce, and the integration needs a hub for reasons that match what a platform is good at. The four-clause test from Lesson 8 still holds: reach for a mediating layer when the integration needs complex process flow logic, aggregation of results across multiple systems, transformation of payloads, or transactional integrity across several calls. For a single request-reply call or two systems syncing, no hub earns its keep. [^3]

The platform's native facilities are exactly the ones that make it a defensible hub. Flows give you declarative orchestration, Apex gives you pro-code logic when the declarative layer runs out of room, External Services turn an external operation into an invocable action, Platform Events and Change Data Capture give you the event-driven backbone, and the same platform that carries all of it also ships the audit, security, and sharing model. When the customer record, the automation around it, and the integration that enriches it all live in one governed place, the hub is not an added hop; it is the application. The Well-Architected framework makes the same point structurally: the multitenant platform with its governor limits poses constraints that generic cloud guidance does not address, so the trade-off conversation has to use platform-native concepts rather than borrowed ones. [^6]

The charters that keep it honest are the limits. Because Salesforce is a multitenant platform, no tenant gets unlimited resources, and the platform's governor limits exist to ensure that a runaway process in one org cannot degrade the neighbor orgs sharing the infrastructure. [^4] That means a hub that centralizes all the integration also concentrates all the traffic into a governed, finite budget, and the discipline of being a hub is the discipline of staying inside it. The Apex Developer Guide's tables, for example, cap a transaction at 100 synchronous SOQL queries (200 asynchronous) and 150 DML statements. [^4] You are not told "scale here by buying more"; you are told "fit the shape of the work to the budget." The Well-Architected framework is blunt about this: governor limits cap each tenant's resource consumption so no single tenant can degrade the platform for others, and these are not arbitrary restrictions but architectural boundaries that shape design. [^5]

## The real cost of centralizing: the limits budget

The failure mode that most hub designs die from is not choosing the wrong topology. It is choosing the hub and then ignoring what living inside a governed platform costs. The reliability pillar names the split plainly: Salesforce's SLA covers the platform, but the application-layer reliability, the retries, the circuit breakers, the timeouts, the SLOs, is yours. [^5]

Inside the hub, that translates to a per-transaction resource budget that you must design to, not to the ceiling but to a healthy fraction of it, and to a 24-hour API request budget that is licensed by edition and license count, not a technical ceiling you can push. This is the same argument Lesson 53 made for the aggregation hub and Lesson 70 made for reliability engineering: the answer is batched, cached, asynchronous, and event-driven, moving volume out of the synchronous request path where the tightest limits apply. High-frequency inventory movement does not belong in a synchronous Apex callout from a user click; it belongs on a platform event and a Queueable or Flow that a consumer drives with the right timeout and retry policy.

The reliability pillar gives you the named mechanisms: circuit breakers for all external integrations, retry with exponential backoff for transient failures, and timeouts matched to the operation, roughly five to ten seconds for user-facing calls and thirty to sixty seconds for asynchronous jobs. [^5] Bake those into the hub, and the hub stays a net addition instead of a single point of failure that the business leans on and then discovers is capped.

## Worked example: the manufacturer's order flow

A manufacturer runs Salesforce as its front office. The ERP is the official system of record for orders and pricing. The WMS tracks each warehouse bin at full fidelity. The billing platform holds balances and credit. The ask: when a sales rep closes an order, the order must land in the ERP, the inventory must be reserved in the WMS, the billing platform must open a balance, and the rep must see a confirmation that ties it all together.

The first decision is the system-of-record test. The order's authoritative home is the ERP, so Salesforce does not try to become the owner of the order; it becomes the orchestrator that convenes the participants. That is an honest hub role: it does not claim a truth it does not hold, but it holds the process that moves the work. Salesforce detects the order confirmation, fans out to the ERP to save the order, to the WMS to reserve inventory, and to billing to open the balance, and it coordinates the result that the rep sees. [^3]

![The manufacturer's order flow with Salesforce as orchestrator, not owner: Salesforce detects the order confirmation and fans out to the ERP to save the order, to the WMS to reserve inventory, and to billing to open the balance, where the ERP and billing are write-side operations needing retry and idempotency while the high-frequency WMS reservation moves onto a platform event and an asynchronous consumer](</diagrams/monolith-vs-microservices-vs-platform-salesforce-integration-hub/3-manufacturer-order-flow.png>)

The second decision is where each leg runs. Saving the order and opening the balance are write-side operations where a partial success is not acceptable; they need the retry, idempotency, and compensation behavior from the earlier integration-reliability lesson. The WMS reservation is high-frequency and latency-tolerant, so it moves onto a platform event and an asynchronous consumer rather than holding the rep's synchronous request hostage to a warehouse endpoint that can answer slowly. The confirmation the rep sees returns immediately with a "processing" state, and the event-driven leg updates it when the WMS actually confirms.

The counterfactual is where the design is judged. If the architect had built a bespoke microservices layer just to run these three calls, they would have bought the distributed-systems complexity (network, consistency, observability, deployment) to mediate three endpoints that a governed platform already reaches, with its security, sharing, and audit model intact. If they had left it as point-to-point, each system would grow a connection to every other, and the quadratic wiring from Lesson 8 returns. The hub earns its place because the process and the confirmation are the shared truth that matters, and the platform is the governed place that owns them.

## The pitfalls to avoid

**Treating "microservices" as universally good and splitting a monolith that had no scaling or team problem.** Premature distribution is a cost you pay in network calls and eventual consistency for an independence you did not need. Salesforce's own platform kept a monolithic core for exactly the part where coordination matters, and moved out only what needed to scale and change alone. [^1] Split where independence pays, not where a slogan tells you to.

**Collapsing the two axes into one.** "Should we go microservices?" is not the same question as "should Salesforce be the hub?" A team can microservice its own application and still wire everything point-to-point. An enterprise can keep a coherent monolith and let it mediate a clean hub-and-spoke topology. Answer the code-shape question and the topology question separately, and your diagram will be honest twice.

**Centralizing in Salesforce without budgeting the limits.** A hub concentrates traffic into a governed, financed, capped platform. Route high-volume and high-frequency work onto platform events, Bulk, queues, and caching instead of synchronous Apex, and design to a healthy fraction of the per-transaction budget and the 24-hour API budget, not to the ceiling. [^4] [^5] The reliability discipline belongs to you, not to the platform you are standing on.

**Claiming hub status where the system of record lives elsewhere.** If the authoritative customer master is in the ERP and Salesforce holds a copy, name Salesforce as the spoke it is, or design explicitly to move the system of record. Converging at a drifting copy is how the org ends up with four plausible but contradictory versions of the same customer. [^3]

## Try it

1. Draw the two axes as a four-quadrant grid, with "one unit of code vs many units" on one axis and "direct connections vs a mediating hub" on the other. Place a self-contained CRM monolith, a Salesforce-centered hub, a team's independently deployed services, and a set of microservices wired point-to-point into their correct quadrants.
2. Using the platform-transformation account, name which part of Salesforce stayed monolithic and which became independent services, and explain in one sentence why the core metadata system of record was the part that stayed centralized.
3. A company's official customer master lives in an on-prem ERP, and Salesforce holds a synced copy for sales. A consultant proposes making Salesforce the integration hub. Using the system-of-record test, state what Salesforce is today and what has to be true before "the hub" is an honest label.
4. The hub fans a confirmed order out to the ERP and the billing platform, and the ERP's fulfillment endpoint answers in ten seconds. Using the limits and reliability numbers from this lesson, say whether to keep that call synchronous on the rep's request path, and name the two mechanisms you would use instead.
5. A reviewer asks why the WMS's full-fidelity inventory movement should not live in the hub. Give the two reasons from this lesson that justify leaving it outside.

For scenario 4, the honest answer is not "make the endpoint fast." It is that a ten-second user-facing callout against the synchronous budget is the wrong shape, so you move the fan-out onto a platform event and let a Queueable or Flow drive it with a timeout and retry policy that matches the ERP's behavior, returning the page immediately with a processing state. [^5]
