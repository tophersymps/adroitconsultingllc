# Lesson 48: Data Residency and Compliance: GDPR, Data Classification, Retention, and Audit

Lesson 47 ended with a consolidation that folded two orgs into one, and it closed on a warning: the cleanest migration in the world fails its compliance review if the data ends up stored where the law says it cannot be. This lesson is that warning made concrete. Compliance is not a checkbox you tick after the build. It is a set of constraints you design into the org from the first field, and the architect is the person who turns legal requirements into concrete platform decisions. Where does the record physically live? What may you do with it? How long do you keep it? How do you prove what happened to it? Each question maps to a Salesforce decision, and this lesson walks all four.

![The compliance spine: four questions map to residency, GDPR, retention, and audit decisions, with field-level classification as the layer each rule attaches to](</diagrams/data-residency-compliance-gdpr-classification-retention-audit/1-compliance-spine.png>)

## Define the terms

- **Data residency**: the physical location where a customer's data is stored and processed. Because a record lives in a data center that sits inside a country or region, residency determines which jurisdiction's laws and regulators can reach that data. Residency is about where the data is; sovereignty is about who has legal authority over it.[^1]
- **Data sovereignty**: the principle that data stored in a country is subject to that country's laws, regardless of who owns the system that holds it. A US company storing EU customer data in a German data center is still subject to EU law for that data, because the data sits in the EU.[^1]
- **GDPR**: the General Data Protection Regulation, the EU law that governs how organizations process the personal data of people in the EU. It applies to any organization that handles EU residents' personal data, whether or not the organization is based in the EU.[^2]
- **Personal data**: any information relating to an identified or identifiable individual, called a data subject. The definition is deliberately broad. A name, an email, a phone number, a customer ID that traces back to a person, all of it is personal data.[^2]
- **Data subject**: the identifiable individual whose personal data an organization processes. Under the GDPR a data subject holds rights over that data: access, rectification, erasure, restriction of processing, portability, and objection.[^2]
- **Data controller and data processor**: the controller decides why and how personal data is processed; the processor handles it on the controller's behalf. In a typical Salesforce deployment the customer is the controller and Salesforce is the processor, which is why the customer, not Salesforce, owns the compliance decisions.[^2]
- **Data classification**: the act of labeling data by sensitivity, regulatory requirement, and business importance. In Salesforce this is a field-level metadata practice: you record a data owner, field usage, a data sensitivity level, and a compliance categorization on each field, so the residency and retention rules have something concrete to attach to.[^3]
- **Data retention**: how long a record and its history are kept before deletion or archiving. Retention is a policy decision driven by law, contract, and business need, and it is enforced in Salesforce through field history limits, archiving, and scheduled deletion.[^4]
- **Audit trail**: the record of who did what and when. Salesforce keeps several: the Setup Audit Trail for configuration changes, field history for record-level changes, and Event Monitoring for login, API, and data-access events. Each has a different retention window, and the architect must know which one proves which claim.[^5][^6]

## Residency is a jurisdiction decision, not a storage decision

The first thing to internalize is that data residency is not about picking a nice data center. It is about choosing which country's law governs the data. A record stored in a German data center is data in Germany, and German and EU law can reach it. A record stored in the United States is data in the US, and US law can reach it. The moment you move a record across a border, you change the legal regime that applies to it, even if the content of the record does not change at all.

Salesforce gives you two levers for this. The first is where the org itself runs. Hyperforce is Salesforce's public-cloud infrastructure, built on AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud, and it is available on AWS in a long list of countries including Australia, Brazil, Canada, France, Germany, India, Japan, Singapore, South Korea, Sweden, Switzerland, the UAE, the UK, and the US.[^1] Choosing a Hyperforce region is how you keep the org's primary data inside a jurisdiction. The second lever is Hyperforce Data Residency, a paid offering built on Hyperforce that gives you finer control over what data gets transferred out of the countries and regions where it is supported.[^1]

The architectural point is that residency is a design decision made before you build, not a setting you flip later. If a client has a hard requirement that EU customer data never leaves the EU, you select an EU Hyperforce region and you design the integrations so that no flow, no external service, and no backup job ships that data to a non-EU location. That second half is the part teams forget. The org can be perfectly resident, and a single integration that pushes customer records to a US-hosted analytics tool breaks the promise. Residency is a property of the whole system, not of the org alone.

## GDPR turns data into obligations

The GDPR is the reason residency matters so much in practice. It applies to any organization processing the personal data of people in the EU, and it is built on a broad definition of personal data: any information relating to an identified or identifiable individual.[^2] That means a customer record with a name and an email is personal data, and so is a support case that quotes a customer's complaint, and so is a marketing lead with a phone number.

The GDPR grants data subjects a set of rights over that data, and each right maps to a concrete Salesforce capability you have to design for. The right of access means a customer can ask what personal data you hold on them, and you must be able to assemble it. The right of rectification means they can ask you to correct inaccurate data. The right of erasure, sometimes called the right to be forgotten, means they can ask you to delete their data. The right to data portability means they can ask for their data in a structured, machine-readable format to take to another provider. The right to object and the right to restrict processing let them push back on how you use the data.[^2]

None of these rights is automatic. The GDPR also requires a lawful basis for processing, and consent, when you rely on it, must be freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous.[^2] The architect's job is to make the rights operable. A subject access request needs a way to find every record that touches a person across objects, which is why a single customer identifier and a clean data model are compliance features, not just design preferences. An erasure request needs a defined deletion path that removes the person's data from the records that hold it, including the copies in sandboxes and backups, which is why retention and erasure have to be designed together.

## Data classification gives the rules something to attach to

Residency and GDPR rules are abstract until you can say which field is sensitive and which is not. That is what data classification is for. In Salesforce, data classification is a field-level metadata practice: for any standard or custom object field you record a data owner, the field usage, a data sensitivity level, and a compliance categorization.[^3] The result is a map of the org that says this field holds a national ID, that field holds a marketing preference, and this other field is public.

The value of that map is that every other compliance decision can hang off it. A field classified as high sensitivity is a field you encrypt, restrict, and audit. A field classified as containing EU personal data is a field that must stay in the EU region and must be included in erasure and access responses. A field classified as public is a field you can expose in a community without a second thought. Without the classification, every one of those decisions is made field by field, by memory, and it drifts the moment someone adds a new field. With it, the rules are attached to the metadata, and a new field inherits the classification discipline instead of slipping through unlabeled.

Salesforce's data classification feature records these attributes at the field level, and it is the mechanism that turns a compliance policy into something the platform can enforce and report on.[^3] The architect's move is to make classification a required part of the field design process, so that no field ships without a sensitivity level and a compliance categorization, the same way no field ships without a label and a type.

## Retention is a policy with a platform cost

Retention answers the question of how long a record and its history survive, and it is where the platform's limits bite. The default field history tracking in Salesforce keeps field-level change history for 18 to 24 months.[^4] If your compliance or audit obligations run longer than that, and they usually do, you need Salesforce Shield's Field Audit Trail, which lets you define retention policies that keep archived field history for up to ten years.[^4] That is the difference between being able to answer "what changed on this account in the last year" and being able to answer it for a decade.

The Setup Audit Trail, which records configuration changes such as who changed a profile or deployed a change set, is retained for only six months, and Salesforce's own guidance is to export it every six months because it is not retained in the org after that.[^5] If you do not export it, you lose the record of who changed what in Setup, which is exactly the kind of evidence an auditor asks for. Event Monitoring, which tracks logins, API calls, and data-access events, has its own retention, and you can extend event log file retention to up to a year.[^6]

The architectural lesson is that retention is not one setting. It is a stack of different windows for different kinds of evidence, and each one has a cost and a limit. The architect has to map the audit obligations to the right tool and schedule the exports before the window closes. A compliance review that asks "who accessed this record in March" needs Event Monitoring data that was retained, and a review that asks "what changed on this field two years ago" needs Field Audit Trail data that was configured before the change happened. You cannot retroactively capture history you did not configure to keep.

![Retention is a stack of four audit tools with different windows: field history, Field Audit Trail, Setup Audit Trail, and Event Monitoring](</diagrams/data-residency-compliance-gdpr-classification-retention-audit/2-retention-matrix.png>)

## Audit is the proof, and proof has to be designed in

Audit is the last leg, and it is the one that makes the other three real. Residency, GDPR, classification, and retention are all policies. Audit is the evidence that the policies held. An auditor does not ask whether you have a retention policy; they ask whether you can show that a specific record was deleted when the policy said it would be, and that a specific field change happened when the history says it did.

That is why the audit tools have to be chosen and configured before the events happen. Field history and Field Audit Trail prove record-level changes. The Setup Audit Trail proves configuration changes, but only if you export it before the six-month window closes. Event Monitoring proves who logged in, what API calls ran, and what data was accessed, but only for the events it captured and only within its retention window.[^5][^6] Each tool answers a different question, and the architect's job is to know which question each one answers and to make sure the evidence survives long enough to be useful.

## Worked example: Meridian, a European fintech, builds a compliant org

Meridian is a fintech based in Germany that holds customer accounts, transaction history, and support cases for customers across the EU. It is building a new Salesforce org, and its legal team has three hard requirements: EU customer data must stay in the EU, the org must be able to answer GDPR data subject requests, and it must be able to prove, for seven years, what happened to any customer record.

The architect starts with residency. Meridian selects a Hyperforce region in Germany so the org's primary data physically sits in the EU, and the architect reviews every integration before it is built. A marketing analytics tool hosted in the US is rejected for customer data, and the team routes only anonymized, non-personal aggregates to it. The residency decision is made at design time, and the integration review is part of the build, not a cleanup after the fact.[^1]

Next, classification. The architect enables data classification and walks the data model field by field. Account Name is public. Email and Phone are high sensitivity and flagged as EU personal data. A custom field holding the customer's tax ID is classified as the most sensitive level and marked for encryption and restricted access. Every new field added to the org must carry a sensitivity level and a compliance categorization before it is accepted, so the classification map stays current as the org grows.[^3]

Retention comes next. The legal requirement is seven years of history on customer records. Default field history tracking at 18 to 24 months is nowhere near enough, so the architect enables Salesforce Shield's Field Audit Trail and configures retention policies that keep the required field history for the full period.[^4] The Setup Audit Trail is scheduled for export every six months, because the org will not retain it beyond that window, and Event Monitoring is configured with extended retention so login and data-access events survive long enough to answer an audit question.[^5][^6]

Finally, the GDPR rights are made operable. A subject access request is answered by a report that assembles every record touching the customer across accounts, cases, and transactions, joined on a single customer identifier. An erasure request follows a defined path that deletes the person's data from the records that hold it, including the copies in sandboxes and backups, and the deletion is itself recorded in the audit trail so the org can prove it happened. The org passes its first compliance review because every requirement was designed in from the first field, not bolted on at the end.

## Try it

1. A client requires that EU customer data never leaves the EU. You select a Hyperforce region in Germany for the org. What else must you review before you can claim the requirement is met? *(Every integration, flow, external service, and backup job that could move customer data to a non-EU location, because residency is a property of the whole system, not of the org alone.)*[^1]

2. A customer files a GDPR subject access request asking what personal data you hold on them. What must the org be able to do, and what makes it possible? *(Assemble every record that touches the person across objects, which a single customer identifier and a clean data model make possible, because personal data is spread across accounts, cases, and transactions.)*[^2]

3. Your compliance team needs to prove what changed on a customer field two years ago. Default field history tracking will not do it. What do you configure, and why? *(Salesforce Shield's Field Audit Trail with a retention policy long enough to cover the obligation, because default field history tracking retains only 18 to 24 months while Field Audit Trail can keep history for up to ten years.)*[^4]

4. The Setup Audit Trail records who changed what in Setup, but you discover it is only retained for six months. What must you do to keep the evidence? *(Export the Setup Audit Trail every six months, because Salesforce does not retain it in the org beyond that window, and the export is the only way to preserve the configuration-change evidence.)*[^5]

5. A new field is added to the org that will hold a customer's national ID. Per the lesson, what must happen before the field is accepted? *(It must be classified with a data sensitivity level and a compliance categorization, so the residency, encryption, and audit rules have something to attach to and the field does not slip through unlabeled.)*[^3]

6. An auditor asks whether a specific record was deleted when the retention policy said it would be. What makes that answer possible? *(A defined deletion path that removes the data from the records that hold it, including sandbox and backup copies, with the deletion itself recorded in the audit trail so the org can prove it happened.)*[^2]

## What's next

You have now taken a record from where it lives, to what the law lets you do with it, to how long you keep it, to how you prove what happened to it. Residency, GDPR, classification, retention, and audit are the compliance spine of the org, and they are all decisions you make in the metadata before a single record loads. The next lesson turns from the data to the code that carries it. Lesson 49 is about metadata and packaging: managed versus unmanaged packages, unlocked packages, and release cadence. It follows directly, because the compliance controls you just designed live in metadata, and the way you package and release that metadata determines whether the controls survive the journey from sandbox to production intact.
