# Lesson 66: Development Lifecycle and Deployment Architecture: DevOps at Enterprise Scale

Lesson 19 laid out the environment chain and the release-mechanism fork: change sets versus source-driven development. This lesson takes the next step. It is about the whole lifecycle that wraps around those environments, the discipline that turns a pile of metadata changes into a monitored, released, production system. The Salesforce certification that owns this territory is the Platform Development Lifecycle and Deployment Architect, and its exam guide is a useful map of what an architect is actually accountable for: source control and branching, continuous integration, testing, release management, and the governance that keeps all of it honest. [^1]

The core idea is that a deployment is not the same thing as a release, and neither is the same thing as a working system. A deployment moves metadata into an org. A release makes that metadata visible to users. And a working system is what you only know about after you watch it run. Enterprise DevOps is the machinery that keeps those three things from drifting apart.

## Define the terms first

- **Application lifecycle management (ALM)**: the full set of processes and tooling that govern a piece of software from the first requirement through build, test, release, and ongoing maintenance in production. The development lifecycle is the middle of ALM; ALM also includes the requirements work that comes before it and the governance that runs after it. [^2]
- **Software development lifecycle (SDLC)**: the stages new development travels through from build to release, plus the processes and tooling that let a team move through them. [^2]
- **Source control**: a system that tracks changes to code and configuration over time, so every change is recorded, attributable, and reversible. Git is the standard. [^1] [^2]
- **Branching strategy**: the rules a team uses to create, name, and merge branches in source control, which decides how parallel work is organized and how it converges on the main line. [^1]
- **Continuous integration (CI)**: the practice of merging every developer's changes into a shared main line frequently and running automated validation on each merge, so integration problems surface early instead of at release time. [^1]
- **Continuous delivery (CD)**: the practice of keeping every validated change in a releasable state, so shipping to production is a routine, low-risk act rather than a special event. [^1]
- **Environment promotion**: moving a validated change up the chain of environments, from developer sandbox through integration, staging, and production, with gates at each step. [^1]
- **Observability**: the ability to understand the internal state of a running system from its external outputs, through logs, metrics, and traces, rather than by guessing. [^3]
- **Release**: the act of making a deployed change visible and active for users. A deployment can land in an org without being a release, and a release is what the business actually experiences. [^1]

## The lifecycle as a pipeline, not a phase

The waterfall model treats development as a sequence of phases that complete before the next begins, and it releases the whole project at the end. That is risky for a reason the model hides: a bug introduced in week one can sit undiscovered until the release ten weeks later, and users get no value until the entire project is done. [^2]

Agile and DevOps both reject that shape. They favor small, frequent releases that get value to users incrementally and surface bugs early, when they are cheap to fix. DevOps goes one step further than agile by pulling operations into the lifecycle: monitoring and feedback on how a release behaves in production feed back into the next round of development. [^2]

For Salesforce specifically, there is a quirk that makes this easier than on other platforms. Salesforce teams do not run operations in the traditional sense, with a separate ops team watching live environments. They generally rely on end-user reports and failed tests to flag issues. So Salesforce DevOps is less about integrating a distinct ops team and more about making sure feedback from users and testing flows back into development. [^2]

## Source control and branching strategy

Source control is the foundation everything else sits on, and it is the place where Salesforce teams are most often behind the rest of the software industry. The ecosystem has historically been slow to adopt it, partly because declarative changes feel like they should not need version control. But source control is rapidly becoming the gold standard because it buys four things at once: quality control, transparency, collaboration, and resilience to risk. It keeps a full history of changes so a bad change can be rolled back, and it lets multiple people work without colliding. [^2]

The branching strategy is the set of rules that decides how that parallel work is organized. The Platform Development Lifecycle and Deployment Architect exam guide treats branching, versioning, and merging as a core skill: given a customer scenario, an architect must recommend how branches should be used. [^1] The common patterns are the ones you would expect from any software team. A trunk-based model keeps everyone working on a short-lived branch that merges back to main frequently, which pairs naturally with CI. A release-branch model cuts a branch when a release is being stabilized, so hotfixes can go to production without dragging along unreleased work. The right choice depends on team size, release cadence, and how much parallel work is in flight.

The point an architect should hold onto is that the branching strategy is a governance decision, not a tooling detail. It decides who can merge, how often, and what has to be true before a merge is allowed. Teams that skip this decision end up with merge conflicts at the worst possible moment, which is release time.

## Continuous integration and continuous delivery

CI is the practice of merging changes into the shared main line frequently and running automated validation on each merge. The exam guide lists it as a core capability: an architect must be able to describe source control, branching strategies, and continuous integration in support of application lifecycle management. [^1] The value is that integration stops being a separate, painful phase and becomes a constant, low-cost activity. A change that breaks the build is caught the day it is merged, not the week before release.

The practical first step for a Salesforce team is often a single CI job that automatically moves changes from a stage sandbox to production, or validates a merge before it reaches an integration environment. Even one CI job pays for itself by cutting the time spent manually configuring deployments and by catching errors before human review. [^2] From there the team can add jobs that deploy to testing sandboxes as a release moves toward production, so each environment in the chain is refreshed automatically rather than by hand. [^2]

CD keeps every validated change in a releasable state. The distinction matters: CI proves a change integrates; CD proves it can ship. On Salesforce, the deployment mechanism is the sf CLI and the Metadata API, and the discipline is that a change that passes validation in CI is a change that can be promoted to the next environment without rework. [^1]

## Environment promotion and the sandbox chain

Lesson 19 covered the sandbox types in detail. This lesson is about how they chain together as a promotion path. A change moves from a developer sandbox, where it is built, to an integration environment, where branches merge and integration tests run, to a staging or pre-release environment, where it is rehearsed against realistic data, and finally to production. Each step is a gate: the change has to pass the tests and reviews appropriate to that stage before it moves on. [^1]

The exam guide is explicit that an architect must be able to design and manage the development and test environment strategy, including data and security, for Salesforce releases. [^1] That means the promotion path is not just a sequence of orgs. It is a sequence of increasingly strict checks, and the data in each environment has to be appropriate to the test being run there. A UAT environment needs realistic data; a developer sandbox does not. Getting that wrong produces a release that passes every gate and still breaks in production because the data was never representative.

## Observability: knowing a release works

A deployment that succeeds is not the same as a release that works. The exam guide's operating domain is explicit about this: an architect must understand the implications of changes made directly in production and how to integrate them into the lifecycle. [^1] That is only possible if you can see what production is doing.

Observability is the discipline of understanding a running system from its outputs: logs, metrics, and traces. [^3] For a Salesforce release, the practical version is knowing what to watch after a deployment lands. Which flows are running and failing? Which Apex exceptions are spiking? Is the integration that the release touched still completing on time? The Salesforce platform gives you the raw material, event monitoring, debug logs, and the health of your integrations, but the architect's job is to decide what to watch and what threshold means trouble.

The reason observability belongs in a lesson on deployment architecture is that it closes the loop. DevOps is defined by feedback: monitoring and feedback on a release feed back into the next development cycle. [^2] A team that deploys and walks away is not doing DevOps. It is doing a handoff. The release is only done when the team knows, from evidence, that the change is behaving in production.

## Worked example: a release pipeline for a regulated enterprise

A financial services firm runs on Unlimited Edition with a Full sandbox for staging, a Partial Copy for UAT, and Developer Pro sandboxes for integration. They ship a release every two weeks and are under audit pressure to prove what changed, who approved it, and when it reached production. Here is how they build the lifecycle.

1. **Put source control first.** The team moves all metadata into a Git repository and adopts a release-branch model. Feature work happens on short-lived branches that merge to main through pull requests, so every change is reviewed and attributable. The audit question "what changed and who approved it" now has a real answer in the commit history. [^1] [^2]

2. **Add a CI job that validates every merge.** A CI job runs `sf project deploy validate` against the integration sandbox on every merge to main, catching metadata errors before human review. This is the single CI job that pays for itself immediately. [^2]

3. **Promote through the chain with gates.** A change that passes CI moves to the integration Developer Pro sandbox for automated tests, then to the Partial Copy sandbox for UAT against realistic data, then to the Full sandbox for the staging rehearsal. Each promotion is a deliberate act with a record, not a manual re-deploy from memory. [^1]

4. **Watch production after release.** After the release lands, the team watches the flows and Apex exceptions the release touched, plus the integration completion times. A spike in failures triggers a rollback to the prior commit, which source control makes a routine operation rather than a reconstruction from a spreadsheet. [^1] [^2]

The result is a release where the "what changed" question has a tool-based answer, the "did it work" question has an evidence-based answer, and both answers survive an audit.

## The pitfall to avoid

**Treating deployment as the end of the lifecycle.** The most common failure is a team that invests in CI and environment promotion, gets a release to production cleanly, and then stops. The release is not the finish line. The exam guide's operating domain exists because changes made directly in production, and releases that are not observed, are where the lifecycle breaks. [^1] A deployment that lands and is never watched is a deployment that can fail for a week before anyone notices.

The second pitfall is skipping the branching decision and letting merge conflicts accumulate until release time. Source control without a branching strategy is just a place to store files. The strategy is what turns it into a governance mechanism, and it has to be decided before the team is under release pressure, not during it. [^1] [^2]

## Try it

Your org ships a release every three weeks and has just moved all metadata into Git. Answer before reading on.

1. A developer merges a change that breaks the integration build. Where does the team find out, and why does that matter? *(In CI, on the day of the merge, because CI validates every merge to the shared main line. Catching it there is cheap; catching it at release time is not.)* [^1] [^2]
2. Your audit team asks "what changed between the last two releases and who approved it." What do you point them at? *(The Git history, because source control keeps a full, attributable history of every change, which is exactly what the branching and versioning discipline provides.)* [^1] [^2]
3. A release deploys successfully but a flow the release touched starts failing in production. What is missing from the lifecycle? *(Observability. The team deployed but did not watch the release, so the failure went unnoticed. Monitoring and feedback are what close the DevOps loop.)* [^2] [^3]
4. Your team wants to ship more often but is afraid of breaking production. What is the first, cheapest step? *(A single CI job that validates every merge, which cuts manual deployment work and catches errors before human review.)* [^2]

When you are done, check that your answers treat deployment, release, and working system as three different things. If you find yourself saying a successful deployment means the job is done, you have collapsed the distinction this lesson is built on.
