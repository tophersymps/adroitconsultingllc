# Lesson 61: CTA Overview: What It Takes, the Exam Domains, the Design Scenario

This is the end of the trail. Every credential in this track, from Platform App Builder up through Application Architect and System Architect, is a stepping stone to one destination: the Salesforce Certified Technical Architect, or CTA. It is the pinnacle of the architect certification ladder, and it is not like any exam you have taken before. Most Salesforce exams ask you to pick the right answer from four options. The CTA asks you to design a complete solution for a messy client scenario, then defend it out loud to a panel of judges, and the judges are CTAs themselves. [^1]

That structural difference is the whole point of this lesson. Before you study integration patterns, data models, or identity design, you need to understand what the CTA actually tests, because it changes how you should prepare. The CTA does not test whether you know a pattern. It tests whether, under time pressure and in front of people who have built real systems, you can take an ambiguous business scenario, turn it into a defendable architecture, and justify the tradeoffs you accepted. That skill is what every other lesson in this track is quietly building toward. So this lesson is the map. Let's lay the course out.

## What it takes: the prerequisites and the two-step path

The CTA has two hard prerequisites, and neither can be skipped: you must already hold the **Salesforce Certified Application Architect** and the **Salesforce Certified System Architect** credentials. [^1] Those two were the subject of Lesson 1 of this track. The Application Architect covers what happens inside the org: the data model, the sharing and visibility model, and native features. The System Architect covers what happens between systems: integration, identity and access, and deployment governance. Together they are the entire architectural landscape. The CTA sits above both, and you cannot sit for it until you have proven both halves. [^2]

Once you have both prerequisites, the path to the CTA runs through two gates, and this is where the exam deviates sharply from everything before it. There is no multiple-choice paper. The two gates are:

1. **The Architect Evaluation** (step 1). A one-on-one assessment where you work with a single CTA who reads your architectural knowledge and gives you feedback before the real exam.
2. **The Architect Review Board Exam** (step 2). The live exam you actually pass or fail. You solve a hypothetical scenario and present it to a panel of CTA judges.

[^1]

Salesforce's own wording is worth reading closely: "To earn the Salesforce CTA credential, you will need to successfully pass the **Architect Review Board Evaluation (step 1) and the Architect Review Board Exam (step 2)**." [^1]

Let me be precise about the times and costs, because they are part of understanding what is on the line, and because this credential is the one place where Salesforce charges real money for a real performance.

**The Architect Evaluation** runs online only and gives you one shot to solve a scenario in front of a single CTA evaluator. [^3] The structure: 60 minutes to read, solution, design, and justify a detailed architectural solution, then 30 minutes to present your recommendations, then 30 minutes of Q&A, then 45 minutes of additional feedback and discussion. [^3] The registration fee is $1,500, and a retake is $750. [^3] Note that 45-minute feedback segment. The evaluation is partly an exam and partly a rehearsal. The CTA you work with is pointing at where your thinking is thin before you pay the far larger price for the real board.

**The Architect Review Board Exam** is the live event. [^2] The structure: 180 minutes (3 hours) to read, solution, design, and justify a detailed architectural solution, then 45 minutes to present your recommendations, then 40 minutes of Q&A with the panel. [^2] The registration fee is $4,500, and a retake is $2,250. [^3] Three hours to design, then 85 minutes to present and defend. That is a long, exhausting performance, and it is deliberately so. A board wants to see how you behave when you have spent hours inside a problem and a panel is probing the weakest seam you left exposed.

There are two timing facts every serious candidate should hold onto:

- If you complete the Architect Evaluation successfully, you receive a date for the Review Board Exam 2 to 4 months later. [^2] That gap is your study runway, and it is not incidental. Salesforce is telling you the real exam is a different beast and you need time to retool.
- If you do not pass the Review Board Exam, you can request a re-attempt only after 4 to 6 months from your previous exam date, and you must submit a case showing interest. [^2] No quick retakes here. This is not a knowledge quiz you can burn through a second time next week. The wait is friction on purpose.

So "what it takes" has three layers: the two prerequisites (proven by passing the domain credentials), the evaluation (a paid rehearsal), and the board exam (the real performance, with a months-long gap and a months-long retake window). Most Salesforce exams reward a few weeks of focused study. The CTA rewards years of delivery work, because that is the only way to acquire the judgment the scenario demands.

## What the exam tests: the domains

Every officially published Salesforce exam names its domains. The CTA is unusual in that the "domains" are not a list of discrete topics you can each master and check off. The skills Salesforce lists for the exam are the architectural disciplines themselves: communication, data integration, integration management, business leadership, business solutions, and more. [^4] What that list actually means is that the scenario is not a test of one subject. It is a test of whether you can hold the whole landscape in your head at once and design across every seam in it.

It is more useful to think about the domains as the range of concerns the scenario will throw at you, and that range is exactly what the two prerequisite credentials bound. From the Application Architect side come the data and sharing concerns: the object model, the field strategy, the org-wide defaults and sharing rules, and the native feature choices. From the System Architect side come the integration, identity, and governance concerns: how systems exchange data, how users and systems prove identity, and how change moves safely through the release lifecycle. [^4] A board-grade scenario never asks you to just design a data model, and it never asks you to just pick an integration pattern. It asks you to design a solution where the data model, the integration model, the identity model, and the governance model all have to be consistent with each other under a budget and a schedule.

Hold that thought, because it is the single most important thing to understand about CTA preparation: **the exam is scored on integration of concerns, not on individual expertise.** A candidate who designs a beautiful data model but ignores performance at scale, or a candidate who nails the integration pattern but lets it leak org data through a weak sharing model, will be probed exactly there. The board is not trying to catch a missing topic. It is trying to see whether you can produce a coherent whole, which is what architecture means at the technical architect level.

## The design scenario

The pressure point of the whole exam is the scenario. Salesforce describes it plainly: "The candidate will review and solve a hypothetical scenario and then present it to a panel of CTA judges. The presentation is followed by a question and answer session." [^2] That one sentence hides what is actually hard, so let me unpack it.

A hypothetical scenario is not a multiple-choice stem with one correct answer. It is a rich case: a client business, a set of functional requirements, a set of non-functional requirements, some constraints (budget, timeline, existing systems you do not control), and usually more than one way to satisfy the requirements. Your job, in three hours, is to produce a complete, justified architectural solution and, in the next 85 minutes, to present it and defend decisions you made when the requirements pulled in opposite directions.

Two terms in that description carry the real weight: **justify** and **hypothetical**.

"Justify" is the word that separates the CTA from every earlier credential. You are not marking an answer correct. You are explaining why you chose each component and, just as importantly, why you did not choose the alternatives. The board wants to hear your reasoning, and it wants to hear you name the tradeoffs you accepted. There is no architecture without tradeoffs, and a candidate who presents a solution with no acknowledged costs reads as someone who has never had to make a real call.

"Hypothetical" is the word that keeps the exam honest. Because the scenario is invented, you cannot have encountered it before, which forces you to rely on principles rather than memory. That is why the CTA cannot be passed by memorizing a case study banks. You have to carry the architectural framework in your head, then apply it to a scenario you are seeing for the first time, under a clock, in front of judges.

Your solution has to be **justified** against a landscape you did not choose. The client's existing systems, the ERP the business already runs, the identity provider that is already in place, the budget that does not stretch to the ideal answer. "Within the context of a client's architectural landscape" is not a phrase Salesforce put in for flavor. It is the definition of the job. [^2] The scenario forces you to fit new architecture into a landscape you do not control, which is the defining reality of consulting architecture.

## Worked example: building a board answer

Let me walk through how a board-grade answer actually gets constructed, so the abstract stack above becomes concrete. I will invent a representative scenario in the same shape the real ones take.

> Scenario: Meridian Logistics, a freight forwarder with 1,200 employees, runs Salesforce Sales Cloud today with a messy, undocumented data model, a legacy on-premises ERP for order fulfillment, and passwords. The board wants Salesforce to become the front office for quoting and order management, with a single view of every customer order, single sign-on for employees, and automated order handoff to the ERP. A three-month budget and a hard go-live date are fixed. The existing ERP API is unreliable at high volume.

You have three hours. Here is the split of how a strong candidate actually spends them, and what the board is looking for.

**First, reframe the requirements before touching any diagram.** The correct first move is not to open a whiteboard and draw objects. It is to decide, at the landscape level, which component owns each piece. Is the order of record Salesforce or the ERP? If Salesforce is the front office for quoting but the ERP is the system of record for fulfillment, then the integration has a clear direction and a clear failure semantic. A candidate who keeps the system of record ambiguous has just invited the board's first question.

**Second, design the seams, not the happy path.** The scenario includes an unreliable ERP at high volume. That one line tells you the naive synchronous call on every save will fail, so the design has to include a queue, an out-of-order delivery strategy, idempotent handlers, and a reconciliation path. The board is watching whether you design for the failure case, not just the success case. This is the "won't fail at critical points" idea from the very first lesson of this track, and it is where most incomplete answers fall apart.

**Third, weave identity and governance in as equal citizens.** Employee single sign-on means an identity integration, and the scenario gives you an existing account store to federate with. The data model change touches the sharing model, because a unified customer order view across 1,200 employees means deciding who sees which orders. The three-month fixed timeline means a realistic release plan: what ships in phase one, what is deferred, and how changes are governed. A candidate who treats data, integration, identity, and governance as separate chapters on separate slides is missing the point. The board wants one coherent system.

**Fourth, name the tradeoffs out loud.** The fixed three-month timeline means something has to give. Maybe the real-time order handoff becomes near-real-time with a queue. Maybe the unified view launches without every historical order migrated. A strong candidate states the compromise explicitly, justifies it against the constraint, and flags what it postpones. That sentence, "given the three-month constraint, I would trade X for Y and defer Z," is the single most board-grade move in the entire presentation. It proves you understand that architecture is the discipline of choosing what not to build.

Now notice what this worked example did not emphasize: platform trivia. It did not hinge on remembering a specific governor limit or a specific flow setting. It hinged on judgment about ownership, failure semantics, identity, governance, budget, and tradeoff. That is the exam. Everything else in this track supplies the vocabulary and the patterns; this is how they come together.

## The pitfall to avoid

**Studying the CTA like it is a knowledge exam.** The most common error candidates make is treating the Review Board as a bigger, harder multiple-choice test: accumulate more facts, memorize more patterns, drill more sample scenarios, and assume that passes. That preparation misses what the board actually scores. A candidate can know every integration pattern cold and still fail if they cannot locate the decision about which one to use inside the client's constraints, or if they cannot defend it when a judge pushes on the seam. [^5]

The corollary is just as important. The domain exams that precede the CTA are earned by studying. The CTA is earned by shipping real architectures and living with the consequences, because that is the only place the judgment of "when, not just what" gets built. Salesforce says it plainly: extensive hands-on experience and a comprehensive study plan are recommended for successful completion of the evaluation. [^2] Note the order. Hands-on experience comes first in that sentence, and it comes first in the way the exam works.

There is a second, subtler trap: **over-focusing on your strongest domain.** If you came up through integration, there is a strong pull to spend your three hours building a world-class integration design and hope the data and identity seams go unnoticed. They will not. The board is not scoring your best chapter. It is scoring the coherence of the whole book, and the panel is deliberately staffed with CTAs who are expert across all the seams precisely so a thin area cannot hide. [^4]

A third trap is worth naming because it is the reason so many first attempts go sideways: **presenting a solution instead of a justification.** Boards have posted candidly that candidates can produce detailed designs and still struggle to address the functional requirements of a scenario fully. [^5] The difference between a design and a justification is that the justification ties every component back to a requirement or a constraint the client actually stated. If you cannot say which requirement a component serves, a judge will ask, and "it seemed like the right tool" is not an answer a board accepts.

## Try it

You will not pass the CTA by reading about it, so let the worked example above double as your first practice run. Take the Meridian Logistics scenario and do this exercise in your head or on paper, in a strict three-hour window if you want to feel the pressure:

1. Write the one-sentence answer to "what is the system of record for orders, and what does that imply about the integration direction?"
2. List every seam where the "unreliable at high volume" ERP changes your design, and name the mitigation for each (queue, idempotency, retry, reconciliation).
3. State the sharing decision for the unified customer order view in one sentence, tied to a specific role or team.
4. Write the exact sentence you would say to name the tradeoff the three-month timeline forces, including what you defer and why.

When you have your four answers, check each one against the criterion the board applies: does it connect a requirement or constraint to a concrete architecture component and justify the choice? If any answer is pure platform trivia with no tie back to the scenario, rewrite it. That is the difference between a design and a board-grade justification, and it is the skill this entire track has been building.
