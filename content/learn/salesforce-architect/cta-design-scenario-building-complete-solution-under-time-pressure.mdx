# Lesson 81: CTA Design Scenario: Building the Complete Solution Under Time Pressure

Lesson 61 gave you the map of the CTA. Lesson 69 walked a full scenario end to end with no clock running. This lesson adds the clock, and the clock changes everything.

The Review Board gives you 180 minutes to read and solve a hypothetical client scenario, then 45 minutes to present and 40 minutes for questions. [^1] Three hours sounds generous when you read it in a lesson. It stops feeling generous around minute 95, when you have a system map half drawn, a data model you like, and no NFR table, no deployment plan, and no slide deck. Most failed attempts do not fail because the candidate cannot design a solution. They fail because the candidate designs 60 percent of a solution beautifully and never assembles the other 40 percent.

I want to be blunt about the economics before we start. The Review Board exam costs $4,500 and a retake costs $2,250. The evaluation before it costs $1,500, with a $750 retake. [^2] A failed attempt is not a learning experience you shrug off. It is a four-figure invoice and another multi-month wait. Treat the clock as part of the exam content, because that is what it is.

## Define the terms

- **Complete solution**: an answer that covers every area the exam outline tests (system architecture, security, data, solution architecture, integration, development lifecycle and deployment planning, and communication), and whose parts agree with each other. [^1] Complete does not mean exhaustive. It means nothing important is missing and nothing contradicts.
- **Timebox**: a fixed block of minutes assigned to one activity, with a hard stop when the block ends. The stop is the point. A timebox you extend is a wish.
- **Triage**: deciding what to do first when you cannot do everything. On the board, triage is the act of ordering decisions by irreversibility and blast radius, not by how interesting they are.
- **Artifact set**: the four things the exam lets you produce: the scenario Google Doc, a Lucidchart diagram, a Google Sheet, and a Google Slide deck. [^1] Each artifact has a job. Using two artifacts for the same job costs you minutes you do not have.
- **Decision density**: the number of consequential, defensible choices per unit of time. A board-grade hour is dense with decisions. An hour spent redrawing a box is not.
- **Consistency pass**: a deliberate read-through at the end where you check that the system of record in the document matches the one in the diagram, that the identity model covers every user class you named, and that the NFR numbers on a slide match the NFR table in the sheet.
- **Completeness test**: for each exam area, you can point to a decision, the artifact that carries it, and the tradeoff you accepted. If any of the three is missing, that area is not complete.
- **Clock budget**: the allocation of your 180 minutes across reading, deciding, drawing, tabulating, writing and rehearsing. You set it in the first five minutes and defend it for the rest of the exam.

## What complete actually means on the board

The exam outline is the specification for completeness. It lists seven areas: System Architecture (the mix of on-platform and off-platform components, reporting and analytics, single or multi-org, license mix, document management), Security (platform mechanisms, portal architecture for internal and external users, declarative and programmatic record security, object and field permissions, end-to-end identity management), Data (large data volumes, data modeling and database design implications, migration strategy and tools), Solution Architecture (declarative versus programmatic mix, external applications), Integration (enterprise integration strategy, technology choices, patterns, platform-specific integration technology and its limits), Development Lifecycle and Deployment Planning (project risks and mitigations, methodology impact, test strategy, governance, environment management, source control and CI), and Communication (articulating choices, handling objections, using visualization and documentation tools, handling unexpected roadblocks). [^1]

That list is not a menu. It is a coverage checklist, and the judges will notice a missing row faster than they will notice an imperfect one. A complete answer touches all seven. An incomplete answer touches four of them in great depth.

Here is the distinction that matters in practice. Complete is a coverage and coherence property. Finished is a polish property. You want complete and slightly rough, not polished and hollow. A hand-drawn system map that shows the right boundaries and the right data flows beats a beautiful diagram of the wrong architecture every time. Judges score the thinking they can hear, not the line weight of your arrows.

The completeness test I use, area by area:

1. **Decision.** What did you choose? "Salesforce owns customer and case data; the ERP stays the system of record for financials." That is a decision. "We will integrate with the ERP" is not.
2. **Carrier.** Where does a judge find it? A decision in your head is not a decision. It belongs in the scenario doc as a numbered decision, on the diagram as a labeled boundary, or in the sheet as a row.
3. **Tradeoff.** What did the choice cost? Every real decision costs something. If your decision has no cost, you have not finished thinking about it. The cost can be latency, money, migration effort, operational burden, or a capability you gave up.

If all three exist for an area, mark it complete and move on. Do not gold-plate it. The clock rewards breadth of coverage with adequate depth over depth in two areas and nothing elsewhere.

## The four tools and the one job each owns

The exam gives you a specific kit, and the kit is a constraint you should design around: a scenario in a Google Doc, a Google Slide deck, a Google Sheet, a blank Lucidchart with access to the Salesforce Shape Library, and an onscreen timer. No other materials are allowed, and Lucidchart templates are explicitly prohibited. [^1]

Assign each tool a job and refuse to let jobs drift.

![Four tools and the single job each owns: the doc is the record, Lucidchart is the spatial argument, the sheet makes numbers defensible, and the deck is the argument you deliver](</diagrams/cta-design-scenario-building-complete-solution-under-time-pressure/2-four-tools.png>)

**The Google Doc is the record.** Read the scenario here, and annotate it. Extract requirements into a numbered register as you read. Note the business why for each cluster of requirements. List your assumptions and constraints. Keep a running decision log with the option chosen, the alternative rejected, and the reason. The doc is where a judge goes to check whether you actually read the case. It is also where you write the narrative you will speak from. Treat it as the source of truth: if a number or a decision changes, it changes here first, then propagates to the other three artifacts.

**Lucidchart is the spatial argument.** One system map that shows the boundaries between Salesforce, the ERP, the external systems, and the users. One data model that shows the core objects and the relationships that matter. One or two integration flows that show the failure path, not only the happy path. A judge should be able to read the diagram without your narration and understand the shape of the solution. Skip the exhaustive field list. A board diagram is a decision diagram, not a schema dump. Since templates are banned, build the legend and the notation yourself in the first ten minutes so every later box takes seconds, not minutes. [^1]

**The Google Sheet is where numbers become defensible.** Put the requirement traceability matrix here: requirement, decision, component, NFR. Put the NFR table here with measurable targets. Put the risk register here with likelihood, impact, mitigation. Put the license and volume arithmetic here if the scenario gives you user counts or record volumes. Sheets are for quantities. A sheet that repeats prose is wasted time.

**The slide deck is the argument you will actually deliver.** It is not a tour of the diagram. It is a sequence: the business problem and your recommendation, the system map, the data model, the security and identity model, the integration design, the reliability and NFR story, the delivery plan, the risks, and the close. Build it last, but build it deliberately. The judges only see what the deck and your voice put in front of them.

The failure pattern to avoid: drawing the diagram first and then discovering at minute 130 that you have not written a single decision down. The document leads. The diagram illustrates. The sheet quantifies. The deck persuades. Four jobs, four tools, no overlap.

## A default clock plan for 180 minutes

This is the allocation I would walk in with. It is not sacred. The shape matters more than the exact minutes: read first, decide the irreversible things early, draw and quantify in the middle, and protect the final half hour for the deck and the consistency pass.

![The 180-minute design clock split into eight timeboxes, from reading and triage through the system map, data and security, NFRs, the deck, the consistency pass, and the opening rehearsal](</diagrams/cta-design-scenario-building-complete-solution-under-time-pressure/3-clock-plan.png>)

- **Minutes 0 to 15: read and annotate.** Read the entire scenario before solving anything. Number every requirement. Mark the constraints that cannot move (compliance, existing systems, budget, timeline). Write the business why in one sentence. Do not open Lucidchart yet. Reading is the cheapest 15 minutes in the exam and the most commonly rushed.
- **Minutes 15 to 30: triage the irreversible decisions.** Identify the system of record for each major data domain. Choose the org topology (single org, multi-org, or a hub with satellites). Decide the identity model's spine (which identity provider, which user classes). These choices constrain everything after them. Make them now, write them down, and stop revisiting them unless a hard requirement forces it.
- **Minutes 30 to 70: system map and integration.** Draw the system map. Label every boundary and every integration with its pattern and direction. Decide synchronous versus asynchronous per flow. Mark the failure path on the flows that can fail. This is where most of the architectural risk lives, so give it the biggest single block.
- **Minutes 70 to 105: data and security.** Build the core data model. Address large data volumes and the migration approach. Then layer the security model: external user access, record-level mechanisms, field-level permissions, and the identity flows for each user class. Security is a scored area and it is easy to leave implicit, so put it on the clock on purpose.
- **Minutes 105 to 135: NFRs, lifecycle, risks, traceability.** Write measurable NFRs. Decide the environment and release strategy. Fill the risk register. Complete the traceability matrix so every requirement has a home. If the scenario names a timeline and a team, sketch the delivery phases.
- **Minutes 135 to 160: build the slide deck.** Convert the decisions into a narrative. One idea per slide. Put the recommendation on slide one. Leave the detail in the doc and the sheet, and put only what you will say on the slides.
- **Minutes 160 to 172: consistency pass.** Run the five cross-checks below. Fix contradictions. Do not add new design.
- **Minutes 172 to 180: rehearse the opening.** Say your first three minutes out loud. You will present for 45 minutes and the opening sets the frame. [^1]

Two rules make this plan survive contact with reality. First, front-load irreversible choices. A system-of-record decision made at minute 120 invalidates an hour of drawing. Second, write as you decide. A decision that lives only in your head is not yet an artifact, and the consistency pass cannot check it.

## Worked example: Kestrel Logistics on the clock

Here is a scenario I wrote to exercise the clock. Read it once, then watch the minutes.

> Kestrel Logistics is a freight forwarder with 2,800 employees across 14 countries. It grew by acquisition and now runs four Salesforce orgs: a Sales Cloud org for the freight sales team, a Service Cloud org for customer service, a legacy org used by customs brokerage, and a small org from a 2024 acquisition in Singapore. SAP ECC is the system of record for financials and invoicing. A third-party transportation management system (TMS) owns shipment milestones and GPS events. Customers want a self-service portal to book shipments and track them in real time. Compliance requires seven-year retention of customs documentation with audit trails. The CIO wants a single customer view and fewer orgs. There is no budget for a full ERP replacement. The programme window is nine months. Peak volume is 40,000 shipment events per hour during holiday season.

At minute zero I read it twice. First pass for the story: acquisitions created four orgs, customers cannot self-serve, compliance is strict. Second pass with a pen: I number 14 requirements and mark four constraints that cannot move (SAP stays, TMS stays, seven-year retention, nine months).

By minute 12 I have the why in one sentence: Kestrel needs one trustworthy customer and shipment view across channels, without replacing the systems that already own financial and operational truth. That sentence goes at the top of the doc.

Minutes 15 to 28 are the irreversible calls. System of record: SAP keeps financials and invoicing; the TMS keeps shipment milestones and GPS; Salesforce owns customer, contact, case, and the commercial shipment record. Org topology: consolidate the four orgs into one Service Cloud and Sales Cloud org, with the customs brokerage capability rebuilt on the consolidated org rather than kept as a separate system. Identity: the corporate identity provider handles workforce single sign-on; external customers get Experience Cloud identities federated to the same provider where possible. I write these as decisions 1, 2 and 3 with the alternative I rejected and why. Decision 2's cost is a migration and a sharing-model reconciliation, which I note as a risk rather than hiding.

Minutes 30 to 68: system map and integration. The map shows SAP, the TMS, the identity provider, the Experience Cloud portal, and the consolidated Salesforce org. The integration choices: order and invoice data from SAP arrive by batch and near-real-time API for status changes; shipment milestones arrive from the TMS as platform events, with a fallback polling job if the event stream lags; portal requests flow into Salesforce as cases. I mark the failure path on the TMS event flow: if events stop, the polling job takes over and raises an alert. That single labeled failure path is worth more to the judges than three more happy-path arrows.

![Kestrel Logistics target architecture: SAP ECC and the transportation management system stay systems of record, one consolidated Salesforce org owns the commercial record, and the identity provider and customer portal connect through labeled integration patterns with one marked failure path](</diagrams/cta-design-scenario-building-complete-solution-under-time-pressure/4-kestrel-system-map.png>)

Minutes 68 to 100: data and security. Core objects: Account, Contact, Case, Shipment, Document. The shipment record in Salesforce is a read model of TMS events plus the commercial fields Salesforce owns. That resolves the plural-ownership trap early. Large data volume: 40,000 events per hour at peak means I cannot store every GPS ping as a Salesforce record, so GPS history stays in the TMS and Salesforce stores milestone events only. Retention: customs documents live in a document management layer with an audit trail, not as attachments on the case. Security: internal users get role hierarchy and sharing rules; portal users get an external identity, a customer community licence with a sharing set scoped to their account; field-level security hides margin fields from portal users. Identity flows for both user classes go on one slide in the doc.

Minutes 100 to 132: NFRs, lifecycle, risks. NFRs get numbers: portal page load under two seconds at 2,000 concurrent users; milestone event propagation within five minutes of TMS receipt for 95 percent of events; 99.9 percent portal availability during the holiday peak; seven-year document retention with immutable audit records. Lifecycle: a single release pipeline with source-driven development, three sandbox tiers, and a change freeze during the holiday peak. Risks: the four-org consolidation is the schedule risk; TMS event volume is the reliability risk; customs data retention is the compliance risk. Each gets a mitigation and an owner role.

Minute 135: the deck. I already have the decisions; building the deck is assembly, not thinking. Slide one states the recommendation: one consolidated Salesforce org, SAP and TMS remain systems of record, portal on Experience Cloud, event-driven milestones. Slides two through eight follow the seven exam areas in the order I will speak them. The traceability matrix stays in the sheet; the deck references it.

Minute 162: the consistency pass catches a real contradiction. My data model drew Shipment as a custom object Salesforce owns, but my integration notes said the TMS owns milestones. I resolve it explicitly: Shipment is a Salesforce object whose milestone fields are populated from TMS events and never edited by users. The doc, the diagram and the sheet all get the same sentence. Without the pass, a judge would have found that in Q&A and I would have spent the answer repairing my own artifacts.

That is the shape of a complete run. Note what I never did: I did not draw every field, I did not model every object in the legacy orgs, and I did not build a cost model for licence types beyond the headline split. Those are cuttable. The system of record, the identity model, the NFR numbers, and the integration failure path are not.

## The failure modes that eat the clock

Time pressure does specific things to an architect's judgment. Knowing the failure modes in advance is most of the defence.

**Diagram perfectionism.** Redrawing boxes for visual balance feels like work and produces no decisions. Counter: set a hard stop on the diagram block. If a box is not carrying a decision, it does not need to be pretty.

**Solving the interesting domain twice.** You will find one area you enjoy, usually integration or data, and spend three times its budget there. Counter: the clock plan assigns a block per area. When the block ends, you stop even if you are not satisfied. An 80 percent integration design plus a 60 percent security design scores better than a 100 percent integration design plus nothing.

**Anchoring on the first requirement.** The first requirement in the doc is rarely the most important one. Counter: read the whole scenario before deciding anything. The 15-minute read exists to prevent this.

**Re-reading instead of deciding.** Anxiety drives candidates back to the scenario text. Counter: annotate on the first read and work from your annotations. Re-reading feels safe and burns minutes.

**Writing prose instead of decisions.** Long narrative paragraphs in the doc feel productive. Counter: write decisions as numbered lines with the alternative and the cost. Save the prose for the presentation script.

**Leaving the deck to the end.** The deck is the only artifact the judges see during your presentation. Counter: protect minutes 135 to 160. If you must steal time, steal it from polish elsewhere.

**Presenting options instead of a recommendation.** Salesforce's own guidance for stakeholder conversations is blunt: providing options without recommendations is sloppy advisory. [^3] Counter: decide, then present the rejected alternatives as rejected. A board wants your judgment, not a menu.

## Triage when you are behind

You will fall behind. Everyone does. What separates a passing run from a failing one is the order in which you cut.

Cut first: exhaustive field lists, secondary requirements that do not change the architecture, visual polish on the diagram, per-object security matrices when one row covers the pattern, detailed cost tables beyond the headline licence split, and slide animations. None of these carry a scored decision.

Cut last, and only under protest: the system of record for each domain, the identity model for every user class, measurable NFR targets, the integration failure path, and the top three risks with mitigations. These are the areas where an omission is visible to a judge and expensive to defend in Q&A.

![Triage order: exhaustive field lists, visual polish, and secondary requirements cut first, while the system of record, the identity model, measurable NFR targets, the integration failure path, and the top risks are cut last](</diagrams/cta-design-scenario-building-complete-solution-under-time-pressure/5-triage-ladder.png>)

One more triage rule. When two requirements conflict and you cannot resolve both, decide in favour of the requirement with the harder constraint (compliance, existing system, contractual obligation) and record the other as an accepted gap with a mitigation. Judges respect a named gap far more than an unmentioned one.

## The 45 minutes after the 180

The presentation is 45 minutes and the questions are 40. [^1] Most candidates under-prepare the presentation because they spent everything on the design. That is a mistake. The design only counts if you can defend it out loud.

A structure that works: three minutes on the business problem and your recommendation, five on the system map, six on data, six on security and identity, six on integration, five on reliability and NFRs, four on the delivery plan, four on risks, three to close. That is 42 minutes, leaving three as buffer. Put the recommendation first. Judges who hear the recommendation early spend the rest of the presentation checking whether your details support it, which is exactly the conversation you want.

Rehearse the five questions you fear most. For each, answer in the same three-part shape: the decision, the reason tied to a requirement, and the tradeoff you accepted. "We chose platform events for milestone propagation because the business needs near-real-time tracking and the volume exceeds what polling can sustain; the cost is operational complexity and a replay requirement, which the risk register covers." That answer takes 20 seconds and demonstrates judgment. An answer that restates the diagram takes two minutes and demonstrates nothing.

## Try it

Set a timer for 180 minutes. Use only a doc, a diagram tool, a spreadsheet and a slide deck. No notes.

> **Scenario.** Halcyon University runs Salesforce for recruitment and student services across three campuses. Its student information system (SIS) owns enrolment and grades. A legacy portal handles tuition payments. International students need visa document uploads with strict retention rules. Faculty want an advising case queue. The CIO wants a single student view, the registrar wants enrolment data to be authoritative in the SIS, and the CFO wants the payment portal retired within 12 months. Peak load is registration week: 9,000 concurrent users and 60,000 form submissions in three days.

Work the clock:

1. **Minutes 0 to 15.** Number the requirements. Write the why in one sentence. Name the constraints that cannot move.
2. **Minutes 15 to 30.** Decide the system of record for student, enrolment, payment and document data. Decide the org topology and the identity spine. Write three numbered decisions with the rejected alternative and the cost.
3. **Minutes 30 to 70.** Draw the system map. Label each integration with pattern and direction. Mark one failure path.
4. **Minutes 70 to 105.** Build the core data model. Address large data volume for registration week. Write the security model for students, faculty and staff.
5. **Minutes 105 to 135.** Write four measurable NFRs. Decide the release and environment strategy. List three risks with mitigations. Complete the traceability matrix.
6. **Minutes 135 to 160.** Build eight slides. Slide one is your recommendation.
7. **Minutes 160 to 172.** Run the consistency pass: system of record matches across doc, diagram and sheet; identity covers every user class; NFR numbers match; every requirement has a home; integration flows match the diagram.
8. **Minutes 172 to 180.** Say your opening out loud.

Then score yourself against the completeness test, not against how good the diagram looks. For each of the seven exam areas, can you point to a decision, its carrier, and its tradeoff? Any area with a missing leg is where you would have lost points. Run it again next week with a different scenario and the same clock. The second run is where the time discipline becomes reflexive.
