# Lesson 83: Advising the Business: Translating Architecture into ROI and a Roadmap

Lesson 82 ended with a design you can defend. That is not the same thing as a design someone will pay for.

Watch what happens after a good architecture review. The architect closes the deck on the diagram, the non-functional requirements table, and the sequencing, and the room nods along. Then the sponsor asks a question the document does not answer. Sometimes it is "what does this cost us over five years." Sometimes it is "what do we get back." Usually it is the third one, asked slightly differently: "when does the money start coming back, and what am I exposed to until then."

Every architect has watched a technically sound design stall at that moment. Not because the design was wrong, but because nobody translated it. The design said flows, objects, and integration patterns. The sponsor was listening for a number, a date, and a risk.

That translation is the last section of the CTA outline, the one labeled Communication, and it is also the part of the job that decides whether your architecture ever gets built. This lesson covers the vocabulary, the arithmetic, and the roadmap artifact that make the sale.

## Four terms, defined before anyone argues about them

Sponsors argue about numbers when the definitions are fuzzy. Settle the vocabulary first, in plain sentences.

**Total cost of ownership (TCO)** is every cost the solution creates over its life, direct and indirect, one-time and recurring. It is not the licence price, and the difference between the two is usually the largest number in the conversation.

**Return on investment (ROI)** is the value returned minus the cost incurred, divided by the cost incurred, over a stated period. The statement of period is not optional. "ROI of 340 percent" means nothing without the word "over five years" attached.

**Payback period** is the point at which cumulative benefit covers cumulative cost. It is the simplest of the standard measures, sitting alongside net present value, internal rate of return, and break-even, which is why it is the one a sponsor reaches for first.[^6] It also ignores the time value of money, so it flatters long programs unless you show the ramp behind it.

**Cost of delay** is the value forgone per unit of time the capability is not live. It is the number that turns a sequencing debate into arithmetic, because it prices the difference between a wave shipping in Q2 and the same wave shipping in Q4.

Those four terms carry the whole conversation. The CTA data and design rounds test whether you can architect; the communication round tests whether you can make a sponsor believe the architecture is worth the cheque.

## TCO: six direct lines and four invisible ones

Start with cost, because cost is the half of the case you control most directly. The Salesforce Well-Architected pillar on resource and cost optimization is explicit about the trap here: looking at a single component such as licence cost produces an incorrect understanding of what a solution actually costs, because a modest licence fee can hide implementation, operational, integration, and change costs that dwarf it.[^1]

The pillar separates the lines into direct costs, which are clearly associated with the solution, and indirect costs, which are real but easy to overlook. Here is the full set, with the Salesforce-specific content each line carries.

Direct costs:

| Line | What it covers | The Salesforce detail people miss |
|---|---|---|
| Licence and consumption | Subscription fees by edition, user type, and feature set, plus usage-based credits | Edition choice is foundational because per-user differences are large; consumption is hard to model early and has to be revisited as design decisions land |
| Implementation | Design, development, testing, data migration, training | Predominantly one-time, but it creates a maintenance obligation proportional to complexity; effort is systematically underestimated because teams focus on development and undervalue testing and training |
| Operational | Administration, user support, monitoring, incident response, tooling | Grows with complexity, and stays invisible in planning because it shows up as internal effort rather than an invoice |
| Maintenance | Enhancement, technical debt remediation, release adaptation, configuration change | Typically consumes 60 to 80 percent of development capacity on mature solutions, which makes it the largest ongoing category in the model |
| Integration | Integration platform licences, API consumption, synchronization development and upkeep | Grows with ecosystem complexity, because point-to-point maintenance compounds as the system count rises |
| Change | Business process redesign, change management, adoption, stakeholder coordination | Increases with reach across business units and regions, and is frequently omitted because it is business team effort rather than a line on a supplier invoice |

Indirect costs, and this is the part that decides whether your model is honest:

- **Organizational costs.** Internal administrator, developer, and architect salaries; version control and CI/CD tooling; training and certification upkeep; and the opportunity cost of development capacity allocated to maintenance instead of innovation. That last item is the hardest to see, because it never appears as spend. It appears as the innovation that did not ship.
- **Technical debt interest.** The compounding cost of architectural shortcuts. A shortcut taken to hit a launch date creates a maintenance burden that can require several times the original effort to resolve, and every sprint spent remediating debt is a sprint not delivering new capability.
- **Governance overhead.** Approvals, coordination meetings, and manual reviews. Governance buys real risk reduction, but excessive governance costs you in delayed decisions and duplicated effort, which is why the design goal is safe autonomy rather than a central approval bottleneck on every change.
- **Unused functionality.** Capabilities that were implemented and never adopted. A partially deployed solution consumes maintenance without returning proportional value, and licence-utilization and feature-adoption monitoring is what surfaces it.

For mature implementations, indirect costs often exceed direct costs. A model that includes only the six direct lines is not conservative, it is incomplete, and the gap is exactly where a cost challenge will land a year later.

### The model, not the number

Two mechanical rules make a TCO model usable rather than decorative. Build it for the baseline **and** for the optimized architectural alternatives before committing to an approach, and project 3 to 5 years with the assumptions written down. Then run sensitivity analysis on the assumptions that matter most, so you know the boundaries of your own estimate. The pillar makes the point that writing the assumptions down is itself part of the value, because it turns a later reassessment into an evidence-based comparison instead of a fresh argument.[^1]

If you want to see why that matters, watch a build-versus-buy debate run on initial cost. Ready-made solutions carry compounding annual subscription fees but arrive with vendor-provided maintenance and release compatibility. Custom development carries a one-time build but hands you full maintenance responsibility and full responsibility for staying compatible with every Salesforce release. Project both over 3 to 5 years and the ranking usually changes, and it changes in favor of whichever option looked more expensive on day one. Four factors decide the rest: strategic differentiation (is this capability a competitive advantage or a commodity), time to value (how fast do you need it), organizational capability (does a team exist to maintain it), and exit cost (how locked in does the choice leave you).[^1]

## Value lines a sponsor will accept

Cost lines are arithmetic. Value lines are where architects lose credibility, because the temptation is to count anything that sounds good. A value line earns its place with four attributes: a unit, a rate, a source, and an owner.

- **Unit.** "Order desk hours saved," "invoices corrected," "licences retired."
- **Rate.** A loaded hourly cost, a cost per rework, an annual contract value. Ideally from the client's own finance data.
- **Source.** The system or team that produced the baseline count. If the source is a vendor's ROI calculator, the line does not go in the model.
- **Owner.** The named person who will confirm the number is real when someone challenges it in month nine.

Five categories survive that test:

1. **Labour returned by removing manual work.** Staff count, hours per week, loaded hourly cost, working weeks per year. This is the most defensible line in most cases because the client already knows all four inputs.
2. **Error and rework elimination.** Error volume, cost per error, and the failure rate the current process produces. Get the error count from the system of record, not from a workshop estimate.
3. **Retired systems and licences.** The line architects forget to claim. Decommissioning a legacy order system, a pricing tool, and a duplicate support platform is a real annual saving with a contract date attached, and it is verifiable in a way that productivity claims are not.
4. **Revenue you can attribute defensibly.** Faster quoting that wins orders the slower process lost. Keep the rate conservative and label it as the softest line in the model. A sponsor who trusts three hard lines will accept one soft one if you say so out loud.
5. **Risk and compliance avoidance.** Leave it out of the ROI arithmetic unless the client's own risk register already carries a monetary figure for the exposure. Inventing a breach cost to pad a case is the fastest way to lose the room.

Now the worked example. It is illustrative, not a benchmark, and the arithmetic is there so you can audit it.

## Worked example: Calder Ridge Distribution

Calder Ridge is a wholesale distributor with 320 Salesforce users. Its order management system is out of support, quoting runs on a homegrown tool, and service tickets live in a separate platform. The ERP stays as the system of record for inventory and finance. The program is to move order capture, quoting, and service onto Salesforce, integrated to the ERP.

**Program cost, five-year view**

| Cost line | Year 0 (one-time) | Annual (run rate) |
|---|---|---|
| Implementation (design, build, migration, test, training) | 520,000 | |
| Licence and consumption | | 190,000 |
| Operational (two administrators plus support) | | 145,000 |
| Maintenance and enhancement | | 120,000 |
| Integration platform and API consumption | | 45,000 |
| Change and training refresh | | 20,000 |
| **Total** | **520,000** | **520,000** |

**Value at run rate**

| Value line | Unit and rate | Annual |
|---|---|---|
| Order desk labour | 45 staff x 5 hours per week x 40 per hour x 46 weeks | 414,000 |
| Order error rework | 1,200 errors per year x 55 per error | 66,000 |
| Legacy order and pricing platforms decommissioned | Contract and hosting, confirmed by the CFO | 165,000 |
| Duplicate support platform retired | Contract, confirmed by the CFO | 45,000 |
| Incremental orders from same-day quoting | 583 additional orders x 120 contribution margin (softest line) | 70,000 |
| **Total** | | **760,000** |

Steady-state arithmetic is the easy part. Net annual value is 760,000 minus 520,000, so 240,000. Payback on the 520,000 one-time investment is 2.2 years. Five-year net is 320,000 on 2,890,000 of total cost, so roughly 11 percent ROI, and the sensitivity range runs from about 4 percent if the margin line does not materialize to about 19 percent if the labour saving lands at 7 hours per week instead of 5.

Now the part that decides whether the sponsor signs. Steady state does not arrive on day one.

**Ramp and cumulative position**

| Year | Value realized | Cost | Net | Cumulative |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | 520,000 (one-time) | -520,000 | -520,000 |
| 1 | 275,000 (Phase 1 live at month 6, half-year of order and quoting benefit) | 340,000 | -65,000 | -585,000 |
| 2 | 655,000 (Phase 2 service migration live mid-year) | 470,000 | +185,000 | -400,000 |
| 3 | 760,000 | 520,000 | +240,000 | -160,000 |
| 4 | 760,000 | 520,000 | +240,000 | +80,000 |
| 5 | 760,000 | 520,000 | +240,000 | +320,000 |

Discounted at a 10 percent hurdle rate, the five-year net present value is approximately 67,000. Compare that to the 320,000 undiscounted figure and the reason becomes obvious: most of the value lands in years 4 and 5, and discounting punishes exactly that shape.

Three sentences come out of this table, and they are the whole business case. The program returns 240,000 a year once it is running. Cumulative cash turns positive early in year 4. The single most valuable thing the architecture can do is move value out of year 2 and into year 1.

That last sentence is not a finance observation. It is an architecture instruction. It says sequence the capability that carries the labour and error value first, deliver the legacy decommission as soon as the service migration allows it, and treat every month of slip in Phase 1 as a cost with a number attached. That number is the cost of delay, and for this program it is roughly 46,000 of net value per month during the ramp.

## The roadmap is the business case

Most roadmaps are written after the business case, as a Gantt chart of promises. That is backwards. In a phased program the roadmap **is** the financial model, because sequencing decides the shape of the cash curve, and the cash curve decides whether the investment gets approved and kept funded.

Salesforce publishes six reference roadmap diagrams for exactly this purpose, and the difference between them is the level you are planning at. A capability roadmap groups capabilities and identifies implementation order, dependencies, and value. A component roadmap adds system functionality, relevant features, timeframes, and stakeholder dependencies. A feature roadmap works at feature sequence for collaboration between business and technical teams. A system roadmap covers system implementation order, dependencies, and feature priorities, and there are Agentforce release plan and roadmap variants for AI rollouts. All of them share one purpose: they show implementation order, dependencies, and priorities so stakeholders can see the phased approach rather than a lump of scope.[^3]

Four sequencing rules do the actual work:

1. **Dependency before convenience.** Identity, integration fabric, and the data lifecycle are foundations. A capability built on a foundation that does not exist yet is a rewrite waiting to happen. Sequence foundations early even when they carry little visible value, and say so in the roadmap rather than hiding it.
2. **Value per unit of exposure, not value alone.** The first wave should be the highest-value capability that needs the least new infrastructure. That is how you buy the second wave with the first wave's results.
3. **Align to the funding and release calendar.** A wave that needs a funded quarter and a Salesforce release window cannot start when the design is ready. Lesson 78 covered the release train; the roadmap has to land on it rather than beside it.
4. **Every wave needs an exit criterion and a gate.** A wave ends with a stated capability live, a value measure shown, and a written decision to continue, re-sequence, or stop. Without gates, a roadmap is a schedule, and schedules do not survive contact with a business.

For the phasing pattern itself, the Salesforce Architects guide on the IT architecture of the agentic enterprise is worth reading as a template, even if your program has nothing to do with AI agents. It describes a phased approach where each stage names a business objective and the value it produces, then names the specific layer investments that stage requires. Level 1 is information retrieval, valued as human productivity rather than headcount replacement. Level 2 is single-domain orchestration, valued as routine task automation. Level 3 is multi-domain orchestration across functions such as quote-to-cash, valued as process cycle time and silo removal. Level 4 is multi-agent operations redesign, valued as step changes in productivity. Targeted investment moves with the stage instead of arriving in one lump.[^2]

That is the structure to copy: value statement, capability, layer investment, gate. It makes each wave independently fundable, which matters when a programme outlives the sponsor who approved it.

### Three roadmap anti-patterns

**The feature list wearing a timeline.** Dates against a backlog is not a roadmap. If a row cannot state the capability and the business value it produces, it does not belong on the artifact you show a sponsor.

**The roadmap with no gates.** If the answer to "what happens if wave 1 underdelivers" is "we keep going," you have removed the sponsor's ability to manage risk, and they will manage it by cutting the whole program instead.

**The roadmap that only shows delivery.** Include the decommissioning and the licence retirements as dated milestones. They are the most verifiable value in the model, and they belong on the same page as the build.

## Saying it out loud: the sponsor conversation

You now have the model. The delivery of it follows rules that Salesforce's own executive communication training spells out, and they are worth following literally.[^4]

**Lead with outcomes.** Get to the point of the conversation first and provide only the necessary context. Start with the facts, without adjectives. Then sum up, describe the impact, and state your recommendation or ask. Not the other way round.

**Have an agenda.** Objectives and expected outcomes, shared in advance, with the sponsor invited to add topics. It signals that the meeting has a business purpose.

**Be knowledgeable.** Know your numbers inside out. A single "I would have to check that" on a value line costs more credibility than the line is worth, so bring the source and the owner's name.

**Be concise, and speak with conviction.** Make the point and move on. Simple questions do not always have simple answers, so train yourself to answer the short version and let them pull the detail. Do not soften a recommendation into a menu.

**Present rather than converse, and you lose the room.** Determine your purpose, provoke, and pause for questions. A slide deck read aloud is not a conversation, and the sponsor's real concern rarely appears on the slide.

The one-page shape that works: lifecycle cost, net annual value at run rate, the quarter cumulative cash turns positive, what you are asking for, and the decision you need by when. Three numbers, one ask, one date. Everything else is appendix.

Two challenges arrive in every one of these meetings, and both have predictable answers.

"Can we do it cheaper?" The answer is scope, not price. Show the specific cut, the value lines it moves, and the new payback. A sponsor who hears "yes, we can shave 15 percent by dropping the decommission" is making an informed trade. A sponsor who hears "we will find savings" is being managed, and they know it.

"Can we do it faster?" That is a cost-of-delay question, so answer it with the per-month figure you already calculated. If the value is 46,000 net per month during the ramp and acceleration costs 180,000, the arithmetic is on the table and the sponsor can decide. Bringing the number makes it a decision rather than a demand.

Before you commit to any of these numbers, three design questions determine whether you can defend them. What is the AS IS process, actually, rather than the process as documented in the policy manual. Which parts of the target process should adopt standard platform features rather than custom build, because standard features lower implementation and maintenance cost and raise return on investment. And what is the client willing to change about how they work, because a design that only automates the current process inherits every inefficiency in it.[^5]

### Keep the case true after go-live

The business case does not end at signature. Two mechanisms keep it honest, both straight out of the Well-Architected pillar.

Put cost impact in the architecture decision records that document major design choices, and require a TCO projection for anything above a defined investment threshold. Then give the architecture review board the cost perspective alongside the functional and non-functional requirements, so investment implications are visible during review instead of discovered after deployment.[^1] A cost challenge in month nine is far easier to answer if the decision record already carried the number.

Adopt a cadence: a monthly anomaly review that catches spending spikes, a quarterly utilization audit that validates licence assignments and capacity usage, and an annual comprehensive TCO assessment that realigns spending with strategic priorities. Chargeback or showback across business units is what turns the model into accountability rather than a document.[^1]

That cadence is also your defence. The value lines you sold in year one need to be re-measured in year two, and the architect who shows up with the original assumptions and the current actuals is the one who gets funded for the next wave.

## Try it

Take the Calder Ridge example and work it. A spreadsheet and 60 minutes.

1. **Build the value lines yourself.** Pick one line at a time and write the unit, the rate, the source, and the owner's name. Then compute it. If you cannot name a source, replace the line or delete it.
2. **Add one indirect cost line and price it.** Use technical debt interest or the opportunity cost of maintenance capacity. State the assumption and the sensitivity range.
3. **Cut scope to improve payback.** Remove one Phase 1 capability that carries about 120,000 of annual value and 90,000 of build cost. Recompute net annual value, simple payback, and the year in which cumulative cash turns positive. Write the one-sentence trade you would offer the sponsor.
4. **Rebuild the ramp so value arrives earlier.** Move the legacy decommission from Phase 2 to Phase 1 and add 60,000 to Phase 1 cost to make it possible. Recompute. Decide whether the earlier cash position earns the extra spend, and say why in one sentence.
5. **Write the roadmap rows.** Five rows maximum: capability, the value it produces, its dependency, its dated milestone, and its gate decision. Include the decommission milestones.
6. **Write the one page.** Lifecycle cost, net annual value at run rate, the quarter cumulative cash turns positive, the ask, the decision date. Then write the two challenges you expect and your answer to each, in the three-part shape: the number, the reason, the trade.

Score yourself on one test. If a sponsor asked "why this price and why this order," could you answer both from the page in front of them without reaching for the appendix? If the answer is yes, the CTA communication round stops being a gamble on your delivery style and becomes a reading of a model you already built.

## What's next

You can now take a defensible design and turn it into a funded, sequenced program: cost modelled across direct and indirect lines, value lines that survive a challenge, a ramp that shows when the cash comes back, and a roadmap where each wave carries its own value statement and gate. What none of it covers yet is how the pieces relate to each other once you lay them side by side. Five sections of this series have each owned one dimension of the picture, and each has been written as if it stood alone. Lesson 84 closes that gap. It is called **The complete architecture picture: stitching integration, security, data, and lifecycle**, and it assembles the whole thing: how the integration fabric, the security model, the data lifecycle, and the deployment path constrain each other in a single design, and what changes when you have to defend them together rather than one at a time.
