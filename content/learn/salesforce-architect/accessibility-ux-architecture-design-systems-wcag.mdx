## Lesson 57: Accessibility and UX Architecture: Design Systems, WCAG, and Inclusive Patterns

Lesson 56 kept one org correct for many languages, regions, and currencies. This lesson is about the harder question underneath all of that work: whether the system is actually usable by every person who depends on it. A screen that formats a date perfectly in German and prices a deal in the right currency still fails the moment a user cannot see it, cannot reach it with a keyboard, or cannot understand what it is asking. Accessibility is not a courtesy layer you bolt on at the end. It is a set of architecture decisions about how the interface is built, and those decisions are made long before any testing happens.

The architect is the right person to own this. Accessibility cuts across data model, component choice, styling, navigation, and third-party integration. No single developer controls all of it. The person who can see the whole build and set the contract is the architect. This lesson gives you the frame: what WCAG actually is, how its principles organize the work, how a design system like SLDS and Lightning base components carry most of the weight for you, and how to think about inclusive patterns instead of a patch list.

## Accessibility is an architecture concern, not a testing box to check

Here is the common failure. A team builds an interface, then runs an automated accessibility scanner at the end, fixes the red flags it finds, and calls the work done. That sequence treats accessibility as a defect list: find a contrast failure, bump the color, move on. It misses the problems that cannot be detected by scanning at all. Keyboard trapping that only appears when a modal opens, a screen reader that reads a dashboard in an order that makes no sense, a focus that disappears when a SPA rerenders. Those are structural problems baked into how the page is composed, and no scanner catches them.

That is why WCAG is organized around principles and not symptoms. The guidelines rest on four things any user must be able to do with your content: perceive it, operate it, understand it, and rely on it as technology changes. [^1] Those four words, perceivable, operable, understandable, robust, are the load-bearing structure of the whole standard. Every specific success criterion is an expression of one of them. When the architect holds the four principles in mind during design, accessibility stops being a checklist and becomes a way of reasoning about every feature that gets built.

**Perceivable** means information must be presentable in ways users can perceive: text alternatives for images, captions for audio, content that works when color alone is not the only signal. **Operable** means the interface can actually be used: keyboard access to everything a mouse can reach, enough time to read and respond, no traps that swallow focus. **Understandable** means the information and the operation make sense: predictable layout, readable text, input help when the platform expects a specific format. **Robust** means the content holds up as assistive technology evolves: valid markup, proper landmarks, semantics that a screen reader can depend on. [^1]

This ordering matters for the architect. If you prioritize a robust, semantic build, the perceivable and operable criteria become far easier to meet. A screen is much more likely to be perceivable when it is built from real form labels and real headings instead of styled divs and aria-hidden hacks everywhere. The principles give you a design filter, not a remediation list.

## WCAG and the conformance levels the architect keeps in mind

Web Content Accessibility Guidelines is the shared international standard for web accessibility, maintained by the World Wide Web Consortium through an open process. [^2] Salesforce itself states that it strives for conformance with WCAG 2.2 Level AA, and publishes Accessibility Conformance Reports (ACRs) for its products. [^3] That is worth holding onto for a concrete reason: when you build on Salesforce's accessible components and follow its design system, you inherit a large portion of the conformance work and do not have to recreate it from first principles.

WCAG organizes its success criteria into three conformance levels. **Level A** is the minimum: the platform must not lock a user out of core functionality. A missing text alternative on a meaningful image, or an action reachable only by mouse, is a Level A failure because it blocks access entirely. **Level AA** is the level most regulations and enterprise requirements target, and the level Salesforce names in its conformance statement. It adds things like adequate contrast, captions, and consistent navigation. **Level AAA** is the top tier, and it is typically not a practical target for an entire application because some AAA criteria, stripping out every word that could confuse, are at odds with normal content. The working bar, almost everywhere, is Level AA.

Here is the practical table an architect carries. Contrast: normal text needs at least a 4.5:1 contrast ratio against its background, and large text needs at least 3:1. [^4] "Large" is defined as at least 18 point (24 CSS pixels) regular weight, or 14 point (18.66 CSS pixels) bold. [^4] Every interactive element must be usable from the keyboard alone. Focus must be visible. Meaning cannot be conveyed by color alone. Form controls need labels. These map to the principles, and they map to components you choose rather than styles you hand-write.

## What a design system does for accessibility

Here is where the architecture work gets concrete. A design system is a shared language for a product: the design tokens, components, patterns, and guidance that let a team build consistent interfaces without rediscovering every decision each time. For the architect, a design system is the single highest-leverage accessibility decision in a build, because accessibility is most reliable when it is inherited from a maintained, tested component library rather than reconstructed in every screen.

Salesforce's design system is the Salesforce Lightning Design System, SLDS. It ships component markup, styling, and guidance that follow the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines and the W3C ARIA Authoring Practices for interactive components. [^5] The word blueprint comes up a lot in SLDS: each component is defined by accessible markup, a pattern for its behavior, and the styling to make it look right. When you build from an SLDS blueprint, you start from markup where the roles, states, and keyboard behavior have already been reasoned through.

The practical route for most builds is the Lightning base components, the ready-made set that ships on the platform: lightning-input, lightning-button, lightning-datatable, lightning-modal, and the rest. These components contain accessible HTML markup from SLDS. [^5] They follow the W3C ARIA Authoring Practices Guide for interactive components, including keyboard behavior and the management of ARIA roles and properties. [^5] That is a strong default. Choose a base component, and the platform maintains the accessible behavior underneath you, including updating ARIA state as users interact, for example an accordion toggling aria-hidden as sections open and close. [^5]

Base components also conform to SLDS color guidelines, which meet or exceed WCAG 2.1 AA standards for contrast, so choosing a base component removes most of the contrast guesswork. [^5] They follow WCAG patterns for predictable input and input assistance on forms. [^6] For an architect this is the whole story in miniature: the highest-quality accessibility decision available in a Salesforce build is usually "use the base component," because the hard, subtle work of accessible behavior is already designed, tested, and maintained there.

## When you must build custom: the gaps you are responsible for

Base components cover a lot, but not everything. Custom Lightning Web Components are inevitable in a real build, and the moment you write one you take responsibility for its accessibility. The Lightning Web Components developer guidance is direct about this: develop components so all users can perceive, understand, navigate, and interact with them, and follow the WCAG guidelines. [^7] The recommendation is to prefer the accessible path: use a base component, or build from an SLDS blueprint when no base component fits. [^7]

If you do write custom markup, three habits carry most of the weight. First, use real semantic elements: a button element for a button, a heading for a heading, a form control with a real label. Screen readers depend on semantics, and fake interactive elements built out of divs with click handlers leak accessibility no matter how much ARIA you add. Second, manage focus deliberately. When a modal opens, focus moves into it. When it closes, focus returns to the element that opened it. This is exactly the kind of behavior base components handle for you and custom components make you own. Third, never convey meaning through color or shape alone: pair every visual signal with text or an icon that has a text alternative. [^4]

These habits echo the four principles. Semantic markup is robustness. Real labels and text alternatives are perceivability. Focus management is operability. Predictable behavior is understandability. When custom work is forced, the principles tell you what to protect.

## Worked example: an accessibility pass on a service console

Put the frame on a concrete build. Suppose you are architecting a new service console for a field-service team: a Lightning app with a case list, a detail pane, lookups to accounts and assets, and inline edit on case status. You want to know what accessibility decisions the design actually forces.

Start with the component layer. The case list is lightning-datatable, the status change is lightning-combobox or lightning-input with a proper label, the account lookup is lightning-input-field bound to a real field, and the save action is lightning-button. Every one of these is a base component, so keyboard behavior, ARIA state, and contrast inherit from the platform. [^5] That single choice resolves most of the perceivable and operable work before a single custom style is written.

Then think about the screen reader flow. The console has many regions: the list, the pane, the utility bar. For robustness, the page should use real landmarks. Lightning handles the app-level shell, but the details that matter, the pane title that describes what the case record is, come from real headings, so a screen reader user can jump between cases instead of tabbing through everything. [^1]

Now the operability question that scanners miss. When a user tab-navigates from the case list into the detail pane and opens an inline edit, focus must land on the edit control and, on close, return to the trigger. If the team implemented that with custom JavaScript and got the focus return wrong, a keyboard user would be stranded mid-page. Deferring to how the platform moves focus, and testing it with the keyboard, is the architect's call to make explicit in the design. [^1]

Finally, contrast and reliance. The team wants a custom status badge that shows "Overdue" in red. Color alone fails both perceivability and the color-not-sole-means rule. The fix that keeps the design and satisfies the standard: text that says the word "Overdue" plus an icon with a text alternative, with any red checked against the 4.5:1 ratio for the small text next to it. [^4] No custom work needed to be avoided here; the design just had to stop relying on color as the only channel.

![A worked accessibility pass on a service console, from the base component choices through screen reader flow, focus management, and the Overdue badge contrast fix](</diagrams/accessibility-ux-architecture-design-systems-wcag/3-service-console-pass.png>)

That is the whole pattern the lesson is teaching. Choose accessible components, protect semantics and focus, and never let a single channel carry meaning alone. The principles make each of those calls obvious.

## Inclusive patterns: not just compliance, but who gets excluded

Conformance is the floor. The architect who stops at "meets AA" has built a compliant system that may still exclude people in predictable ways. Inclusive design is the next step up: deliberately considering the range of human ability and making the design work for more of it, rather than treating minimums as the design target.

The Salesforce accessibility program is explicit that the goal is wider inclusion, not conformance theater. Salesforce reports that around 1.3 billion people worldwide, roughly 15 percent of the global population, live with some form of disability. [^3] That is not a niche. Gather the org, its contact center reps, its field technicians, its customers; a meaningful fraction of every one of those groups works or thinks differently from the default assumption a developer tends to picture.

Inclusive patterns that an architect can design in from the start: give users enough time and don't expire critical actions silently, so an "operable" interface is also usable by someone who needs longer to read. Offer text alternatives and captions so the perceivable works for varied senses. Keep layouts predictable so the understandable holds for someone who gets disoriented by sudden change. And when in doubt, test with people who actually experience exclusion, not just with an automated report. [^8] The Salesforce design guidance makes this concrete: understand needs before building, choose accessible colors, prioritize keyboard access from the start, and co-design with users who experience exclusion. [^8]

The architect-oriented version of all of this is one sentence. Build reliability into the structure so inclusion does not have to be patched back in later.

## Pitfalls to design around

A few failure modes show up in nearly every build, and naming them saves a lot of rework. The first is treating an automated scanner as a verdict. Scanners catch contrast and missing alt text; they do not catch a modal that traps keyboard focus or a screen reader order that makes no sense. [^8] Plan manual keyboard and screen reader passes alongside the automation.

The second is over-ARIA-ing custom markup. When a team builds a clickable div and then layers on roles and aria-labels to try to make it announce like a button, it is working against the browser. A real button element gets the keyboard and announcement behavior for free. [^5] Semantics before ARIA, always.

The third is padding the requirement. Targeting Level AAA everywhere makes teams give up on accessibility entirely rather than shipping AA and being honest about the rest. Anchor on AA, document any gaps, and let the four principles drive what you protect.

The fourth is forgetting that the platform carries the conformance but not the whole responsibility. Lightning base components give you an accessible foundation; they do not make a misconceived page accessible. The architect still decides what the page is made of and how it flows.

## Try it

Take an application you are working on, real or imagined, and run it through the WCAG lens as an architect rather than a tester.

Pick one screen, for example a record page or a flow screen. Answer three questions on paper. First, perceivable: does every meaningful image have a text alternative, and is any meaning conveyed by color alone? Second, operable: can a user reach every action with the keyboard alone, and does focus move visibly and predictably, landing in a modal when it opens and returning when it closes? Third, robust: is the page built from real headings, labels, and form controls that a screen reader can rely on, rather than styled divs? [^1][^4][^5]

Now make the answer better the way the lesson suggests: for any custom control, name the Lightning base component that replaces it, and for any color-only signal, describe the text or icon you would add. You have turned a compliance review into an architecture decision.
