# Lesson 87: Exam prep: Application and Platform Architect domains review

Lesson 86 reviewed the three domains the Review Board scores hardest: integration, security and data. This lesson goes one floor down, to the exams that decide whether you get to sit in that room at all.

The Architect credential path is not a single exam. It is a stack of exams, and the two credentials that gate the Review Board are Application Architect and System Architect. Each one is granted when a defined set of prerequisite exams has been passed, not when a portfolio has been assembled or a manager has signed off. Salesforce publishes which exams those are, and it publishes every exam guide with the domain weights attached.[^2]

That structure decides how you prepare. There is no narrative to deliver and nobody to convince on the way up. There are 60 multiple-choice items, a stated passing score, and a published percentage sitting over each section of the outline. A candidate who reads the outline as a budget for study hours will outscore a candidate who reads it as a table of contents. This lesson does that reading for the six exams behind the two credentials, and names the traps that cost points in each.

## The credential stack, defined

**Application Architect.** Granted once all four prerequisites are completed: Platform App Builder, Platform Data Architect, Platform Developer, and Platform Sharing and Visibility Architect. Salesforce describes the holder as having a deep understanding of native platform features and functionality, and expertise at modeling a role hierarchy, data and appropriate sharing mechanisms.[^1][^2]

**System Architect.** Granted once Platform Developer, Platform Development Lifecycle and Deployment Architect, Platform Identity and Access Management Architect and Platform Integration Architect are completed.[^2] The Application Architect stack is about the solution inside one org. This one leans toward the systems around it and the life the solution has after go-live.

**Technical Architect.** The Review Board credential, which requires the Architect Review Board Evaluation alongside both the Application Architect and System Architect certifications.[^2] Lesson 86 covered its format and its seven outline areas.

Platform Developer appears in both prerequisite lists. If both credentials are on your plan, that is the exam to sequence first, because it is the only one that pays for itself twice.[^2]

**Domain weighting.** The percentage each section of an exam outline carries. It is the closest thing to a study plan the vendor publishes, and it is the number most candidates skim past on the way to the objectives.

**Scenario stem.** The phrasing that opens most objectives: given a scenario, given a set of conditions, given a requirement. A stem is a signal about the item. It will not ask what a feature is called. It will ask what to do with that feature, and under which constraints.

**Unscored items.** Each of these exams includes up to five questions that carry no score, mixed in at random to gather performance data on future items. They cannot help or hurt your result, which is worth remembering when one item feels unlike anything in the guide.[^3]

Four of those exams are the ones a reader is most likely to be sitting. Here is what the published guides say about each of them.

| Exam | Items and time | Passing score | Largest section |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Platform Sharing and Visibility Architect | 60 multiple-choice questions plus up to five unscored, 120 minutes | 58 percent | Access to Records at 39 percent[^3] |
| Platform Identity and Access Management Architect | 60 multiple-choice questions plus up to five unscored, 120 minutes | 65 percent | Accepting Third-Party Identity at 21 percent[^4] |
| Platform Development Lifecycle and Deployment Architect | 60 multiple-choice questions plus up to five unscored, 105 minutes | 65 percent | System Design at 15 percent[^5] |
| Platform Data Architect | 60 multiple-choice questions plus up to five unscored, 105 minutes | 58 percent | Data Modeling and Database Design at 25 percent, tied with Salesforce Data Management[^6] |

Read the version line in a guide before trusting the rest of it. The Sharing and Visibility guide says its items align to the Winter '23 release, the Identity guide to Summer '23, and the lifecycle and data guides to Spring '23. A guide pinned to a release a few cycles back will not name the newest platform capability, and the outline is not rewritten every release. Use the guide for the objective wording and the weights, then read release notes for what has landed since.

## Reading an exam guide as a scope document

Two lists inside those guides are worth more than the outline, and most candidates skip both of them.

The first is headed something like "a candidate for this exam will likely need assistance with". It names the work the exam expects you to find hard. On the Sharing and Visibility guide that list includes being able to implement programmatic sharing, determining performance issues and scalability of large data sets with sharing, and determining which types of sharing are impacted in an LDV scenario.[^3] Read it as a map of where the hard items live.

The second is the "not expected to know" list, which bounds scope from the other direction. The same guide excludes complex role hierarchy design, measuring or predicting LDV impacts on security design, documenting or gathering legal requirements around sensitive data, and org merge security design.[^3] Those exclusions tell you where a working model is enough, and they hand back hours you can spend on the section carrying 39 percent of the marks.

Then count the stems. In the Sharing and Visibility outline, 18 of the published objectives open with a given-a-scenario style stem.[^3] The weights tell you where items cluster. The stems tell you what shape they take. Both together are the study plan, and they are more useful than any third-party question bank you have to pay for.

## The Application Architect stack

### App Builder and Developer: where the declarative boundary gets tested

App Builder owns the declarative half of this stack. Platform Developer owns the programmatic half: Apex classes and triggers, the order in which things run during a save, governor limits, and the tests that let a deployment move at all. Both sit in the Application Architect prerequisite list, and Platform Developer also sits in the System Architect list.[^2]

Items at this level rarely ask for a feature. They ask for a choice, and the sharing guide says so when it asks candidates to describe best practices and justification for when to use standard Salesforce functionality when solutioning for complex security requirements and when customizations are a better fit.[^3]

Worked example. A services firm wants an intake process with a two-step approval, a running total of approved requests shown on the parent account, and a nightly reclassification of requests older than 90 days.

The weak answer builds an Apex trigger on the request object that starts the approval, recalculates the parent total and reclassifies on update. It would work. It also names no reason for the choice, and it puts three unrelated jobs in one transaction.

The scoring answer splits the three jobs by mechanism. The approval runs as a Flow with a declarative approval process, so the business can change the steps without a deployment. The parent total becomes a roll-up summary field, which stays accurate without code and without a scheduled job. The reclassification runs as scheduled work outside a user's transaction, because it touches records nobody is editing and it should never slow down an intake submission. Apex earns its place only if the reclassification has to call something external or enforce a rule the declarative layer cannot express.

Then name the trade-off, because the trade-off is the scored part. Roll-up summary fields require a master-detail relationship, and master-detail brings its own sharing behavior with it. If intake requests need their own owner, their own queues or their own sharing rules, that is a legitimate reason to accept a scheduled recalculation instead of the roll-up. Saying which option you gave up, and why, is what separates two answers that both happen to work.

### Platform Sharing and Visibility Architect: 39 percent sits in one section

This is the exam that most often stands between a strong administrator and the Application Architect credential, and its weights explain why. Permissions to standard objects, custom objects and fields carries 27 percent, Access to Records carries 39 percent, Access to Other Data covers files, reports, dashboards and big objects at 16 percent, and Implications of Security Model Choice carries the remaining 18 percent.[^3]

The record access objectives read as a ladder, and the order of the rungs is the answer to most items:

1. Set organization-wide defaults first, tightest first. The section opens by asking for the appropriate organization-wide defaults to restrict access to records, because every other mechanism on this page is an exception to that default.[^3]
2. Choose the relationship deliberately. Master-detail and lookup produce different record access behavior on the child, and the correct object relationships get their own objective under record access.[^3]
3. Use the role hierarchy for access that follows the management line, and only that.
4. Use sharing rules, both criteria-based and ownership-based, and the groups they point at, for access that crosses the hierarchy.
5. Use teams where the business already thinks in teams: account teams, sales teams, case teams.
6. Reach for sharing sets and sharing groups when the audience is external, because a partner or customer population rarely maps onto an internal role hierarchy.
7. Reach for Apex managed sharing only when the declarative layer cannot express the rule, and then say what the declarative layer could not express.
8. Use record access overrides where the platform's standard answer is wrong for the scenario, such as a child record that should not follow its parent.

A scenario to practice on. Meridian Field Services has 1,200 internal users across three regions, 300 partner dealers who log in to track their own jobs, and regional managers who must see every opportunity in their region and nothing outside it.

The answer that scores states the organization-wide default and its reason, then walks the ladder. Opportunities private, because regional isolation is the starting point. Role hierarchy opened so each regional manager sees the region below them. Criteria-based sharing rules keyed on the region field for the cross-hierarchy exceptions. Dealer visibility delivered through a sharing set scoped to the dealer's account rather than by widening the object. Partner records filtered by license type, since the guide asks for the license limitations that will impact the intended sharing solution.

Then the 18 percent section that candidates forget: state the scalability implications of the sharing solution, the license limitations that will impact the intended sharing solution, and how to test the sharing model.[^3] For Meridian that means naming the record count the regional rules will recalculate over, saying how a bulk data load will be handled so the recalculation does not stall it, and describing the test accounts and test users that prove a dealer cannot see a neighboring dealer's jobs before the model goes live.

### Platform Data Architect: the model has to obey the sharing model

The data architect credential is the fourth prerequisite in the Application Architect stack.[^2] Two sections carry 25 percent each in its outline: Data Modeling and Database Design, and Salesforce Data Management. Large Data Volume Considerations carries 20 percent, Data Migration 15 percent, Data Governance 10 percent, and Master Data Management 5 percent.[^6]

Two objectives in that outline matter most for an architect who also owns security. The guide asks candidates to recommend approaches and techniques to design a scalable data model that obeys the current security and sharing model, and to recommend approaches and techniques to avoid data skew, naming record locking, sharing calculation issues and excessive child to parent relationships as the things to avoid.[^6] That objective is where the data exam and the sharing exam meet. The relationship you choose for a child object decides who locks it, who inherits access to it, and how much work the sharing engine does on every save.

The expected skills list is a fair inventory of the rest: custom fields, master-detail and lookup relationships, standard object structure, making the best use of standard and big objects, the association between standard objects and Salesforce license types, indexing, LDV migrations, and data stewardship.[^6]

Worked example. A 60 million row interaction history feeds both a rep-facing list and an executive trend dashboard, and the business wants fourteen months of it available inside the product.

The weak answer keeps everything on one custom object and adds filters to the list view. The scoring answer separates the requirement by operation. The rep-facing list reads a rolling window on the main object behind an indexed filter. The dashboard reads a summarized layer refreshed on a schedule. The older history moves to a big object that is written and read in bulk rather than one record at a time. Then the answer states what the design refuses to promise, which is list-view speed across the full history.

## The System Architect stack

### Development Lifecycle and Deployment Architect

Weights first: Application Lifecycle Management 8 percent, Planning 13 percent, System Design 15 percent, Building 14 percent, Deploying 14 percent, Testing 13 percent, Releasing 13 percent, Operating 10 percent.[^5] Nothing dominates, which is the point of the exam. It punishes a candidate who is strong in one phase and vague in the rest.

The objectives read like a delivery lead's job description. Design an environment strategy that names the sandbox types it needs, including multiple project streams, training requirements, staging, production and hotfixes. Choose between an org-based and a package-based development model, and between scratch orgs and sandboxes. Recommend a branching, versioning and merging strategy. Describe the capabilities and constraints of the Metadata and Tooling APIs, and handle the pre and post deployment steps those APIs do not cover. Manage technical reference data, which is configuration stored as object data. Recommend a testing methodology and a test data strategy that uses representative data securely. Decide between managed, unmanaged and unlocked packages. Map a sandbox strategy to a release plan. Handle the multi-org case, including how common release artifacts are managed across orgs, and describe what happens when someone makes a change directly in production.[^5]

Worked example. A manufacturer runs two project streams against one production org, releases quarterly to match a Salesforce window, and needs a one-day hotfix path for pricing rules.

A scoring answer names the topology before it names a tool. A full sandbox refreshed after each release for performance and data volume testing. A partial-data sandbox for integration work. Developer sandboxes per developer, or scratch orgs if the team is source-driven. A staging environment that mirrors the release plan. Branching that gives each stream a long-lived branch with short-lived feature branches merged to a release branch before the window. A hotfix branch with a defined path to production and a merge back into both streams, because a hotfix that lands only in production becomes a defect that the next release reintroduces. Then the governance sentence the exam wants: who approves a production change, how it is recorded, and how it is reconciled into the next release plan.[^5]

### Platform Identity and Access Management Architect

Weights: Identity Management Concepts 17 percent, Accepting Third-Party Identity in Salesforce 21 percent, Salesforce as an Identity Provider 17 percent, Access Management Best Practices 15 percent, Salesforce Identity 12 percent, and Community, meaning Experience Cloud partner and customer access, 18 percent.[^4]

The concepts the guide expects a candidate to hold without assistance are specific enough to rehearse as a list. The difference between federated and delegated single sign-on. SAML configuration, and the difference between identity provider initiated and service provider initiated SAML with the case for each. How trust is established between an identity provider and a service provider. The high level flows of OAuth, SAML and OpenID Connect. Social sign-on in the Salesforce context. Authentication mechanisms for communities. The common failure conditions in SSO and how to resolve them. Why a solid SSO strategy matters for enterprise security. Two-factor authentication and the strategies for implementing it. Login flows. The use cases for Identity Connect. And the user lifecycle management techniques of automated user provisioning, just-in-time provisioning and manual account creation.[^4]

Two items on that list catch people. The first is provisioning, because every scenario in this exam eventually asks how a user comes to exist. A complete answer covers creation, the assignment of roles, profiles and permission sets during the SSO process, and how those assignments stay current after a person changes job.[^4] The second is the session. Access Management Best Practices asks for the right multi-factor authentication method and the right type of session it should yield, which means the answer has to say how long a session lives and what re-authentication the user faces, not simply that MFA is enabled.[^4]

Worked example. A distributor has 900 employees authenticating through a corporate identity provider, and 3,000 dealer users whose accounts live in a partner's own directory with constant turnover.

The weak answer runs both populations through one connected app and one SAML configuration. The scoring answer separates them by authentication path, because they have different identity stores, different session needs and different license types. Employees federate to the corporate provider with SAML, with provisioning driven from the directory so a leaver loses access the same day. Dealers authenticate against their own provider through a separate configuration with just-in-time provisioning, because manual account creation does not survive a population that churns. Both paths land in the same role, profile and permission set structure, so the audit of who can see what runs in one place. Then the trade-off. Just-in-time provisioning simplifies onboarding and leaves terminated dealer users in place until the partner deactivates them, so the design adds a periodic reconciliation of active dealer users against the partner's active contact list.

## Exam traps

**Learning the weights and skipping the stems.** A candidate who knows that Access to Records carries 39 percent but has never answered a given-a-set-of-conditions item is still guessing.

**Naming a mechanism without a reason.** "Use a sharing rule" is a symptom of not having chosen a model. The scoring version names the requirement the rule satisfies and the requirement it deliberately does not.

**Leaving the declarative boundary unresolved.** Every one of these guides asks for justification when custom code is chosen over standard functionality.[^3] State the declarative mechanism first, then name what it cannot express before reaching for Apex.

**Skipping the implications section.** On the Sharing and Visibility exam it is 18 percent and it asks for scalability, license impact and how the model will be tested, which are exactly the three questions a real design review asks and exactly the three most candidates leave out.[^3]

**Treating licensing as procurement.** License type appears in the sharing guide's implications section, in the data guide's expected skills and in the identity guide's license selection objective, because licenses change what the platform will let a user do.[^3][^4][^6]

**Reading a guide as if it were current.** Each guide is aligned to a specific release, and the outline wording can lag the platform. Check the version line, then check release notes for anything the guide predates.[^3][^4][^5][^6]

**Studying the "not expected to know" list.** It is scope control, not a syllabus. The sharing guide excludes complex role hierarchy design and org merge security design, and neither exclusion earns you points.[^3]

## Try it

Pick the credential you are closest to, then spend 60 minutes on this.

1. **Pull the exam guide for each prerequisite exam** in your credential and copy the outline sections with their percentages into one sheet.[^2]
2. **Count the stems.** For each section, count how many objectives open with given a scenario or given a set of conditions, and write the count beside the weight.
3. **Rank your hours.** Sort the sections by weight times your own weakness on that section. The top three sections are your study plan for the next two weeks.
4. **Answer three stems out loud** in the decision, reason, trade-off shape that lesson 86 described. Record them. If any answer runs over 90 seconds, that section is not finished studying.
5. **Write the exclusion list.** Copy the guide's not-expected items into the same sheet and keep them visible, so you stop losing hours to material the exam will not score.[^3]
6. **Do one timed run.** 60 questions in the published time limit for your exam, using the weights to decide what to skip when the clock runs low.

Score yourself on one question. Could you explain, without notes, why a section carrying 25 percent of the marks has a lower priority this week than a section carrying 15? If the answer is your own weakness and not the weight, you are studying a person's gaps rather than a table of contents, and that is the right way round.

## What's next

You now have the item-level view of both credentials: the prerequisite stacks, the domain weights, the stems, the answer shape and the traps that cost points on the way to the Review Board. What none of it has covered yet is the room itself on the day. Lesson 88 is called **Exam prep: CTA review and scenario practice**, and it converts this preparation into rehearsal: reading a full scenario under the clock, producing the solution, design and justification artifacts the Review Board expects, presenting the recommendations in 45 minutes, and surviving 40 minutes of questions about the decisions you made. It is the last review pass in the series before the scenario itself, and it assumes you already hold the domain knowledge that lessons 86 and 87 have been testing.
