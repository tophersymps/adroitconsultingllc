# Lesson 1: What a Salesforce System Architect Actually Does

"Architect" is the most overloaded title in the Salesforce ecosystem. Companies hand it to whoever draws the org chart, and consulting firms put it on any senior consultant. But Salesforce itself defines the role precisely, and there is a certification ladder that tells you what each tier of architect is responsible for. This lesson is the map for the whole track: what the roles are, how the credentials stack, and where it all ends (spoiler: CTA).

## What "architect" means on Salesforce

Salesforce's own definition is the one to internalize:

> Salesforce Architects are big-picture thinkers and in-depth problem solvers. They design systems for the long-term that stand up to high volumes and won't fail at critical points.
> [^1]

Two phrases there do the heavy lifting. **"For the long-term"**: an architect is optimizing for the org's state five years out, not the demo next week. **"Won't fail at critical points"**: high volumes, security boundaries, integration failures, release windows. The architect is the person who answers "what happens when this scales / when this breaks / when this gets hacked?" before anyone ships.

Both phrases describe a job that is mostly judgment: judgment about what to build, what to refuse to build, and where to spend a limited budget of complexity. The phrase "big-picture thinker" is doing more work than it looks. It does not mean the architect is the person with the cleanest slide deck. It means the architect's unit of analysis is the system around the org, not the org itself. When the business frames a problem as "Salesforce needs to do X," the architect's first move is to reframe it as "the system needs to do X, and here is which component should own that," because quite often the answer is that the ERP, the data warehouse, or a middleware layer should own it and Salesforce should consume it.

"Won't fail at critical points" is where most of the architect's attention actually goes, and it explains why the job is distinct from a senior developer or a team lead. A developer owns a component. An architect owns the answer to "what does this system do when it is under stress," and that question gets asked at the boundaries: at high volumes, at security boundaries, at integration failures, and at release windows. A design can be elegant and still fail at any one of those four seams. Architects get brought into programs late precisely because nobody asked those questions early, and the cost of fixing them late is structural.

The practical consequence is a working definition worth writing down, because the entire track hangs off it. **An architect translates business requirements into a platform design that is secure, scalable, maintainable, and deliverable, then defends that design through delivery.** Four adjectives, and the last two are the ones people forget. Maintainable means a team that is not you can run it next year. Deliverable means you can actually build it with the budget and the calendar you have. A design that is secure and scalable but cannot be delivered is not architecture. It is a wish.

## The three-tier certification arc

Salesforce organizes architect credentials into two "domain" architect certifications, and one pinnacle credential above them both. [^1]

### 1. Application Architect: the on-platform owner

> Certified Application Architects have a deep understanding of native Salesforce features and functionality. They're also experts at modeling a role hierarchy, data, and appropriate sharing mechanisms.
> [^2]

The Application Architect owns what happens **inside** Salesforce: the data model, the sharing and visibility model, and native features. The credential is granted automatically after four prerequisites:

- Salesforce Certified Platform App Builder
- Salesforce Certified Platform Data Architect
- Salesforce Certified Platform Developer I
- Salesforce Certified Platform Sharing and Visibility Architect

[^2]

Look at what those four exams cover, because each one maps to a piece of the job. Platform App Builder is the declarative half of building on the platform: objects, page layouts, flows, and the judgment about which requests should be solved with clicks and which need code. Platform Data Architect is data modeling: which objects exist, how they relate, and where duplication is worth the cost. Platform Developer I is Apex, triggers, and the order of execution, which matters because an on-platform design only holds up if the code that writes to the data respects the platform's rules. Platform Sharing and Visibility Architect is the security model: org-wide defaults, role hierarchy, sharing rules, and manual sharing. Put together, the Application Architect is a data modeler, a declarative designer, and a sharing architect. The exam list is a specification for the job.

### 2. System Architect: the between-systems owner

> Certified System Architects focus on off-platform systems, integration, and securing access between systems. They're also skilled at managing governance and testing capabilities for deployment and ongoing Salesforce modification requirements.
> [^3]

The System Architect owns what happens **between** systems: integration architecture, identity and access between systems, and the release/governance machinery that keeps changes safe. The credential is granted automatically after four prerequisites:

- Salesforce Certified Platform Developer I
- Salesforce Certified Platform Development Lifecycle and Deployment Architect
- Salesforce Certified Platform Identity and Access Management Architect
- Salesforce Certified Platform Integration Architect

[^4]

Developer I appears on both lists, which is worth pausing on. It is the shared foundation, and its presence on the System Architect side is the tell that integration work is still platform work: you cannot design between systems without knowing how the platform executes code, because every integration lands inside an org that has triggers and validation rules waiting for it. The other three prerequisites map to the three concerns in the definition. Deployment lifecycle and governance is the release machinery. Identity and access is how users and other systems prove who they are. Integration architecture is how data moves. Same pattern as before: the credential's prerequisite list is the job description, written as exams.

Note the split: it's the mental model that will organize this entire track:

| Concern | Owner |
|---|---|
| Data model, sharing, native features | **Application Architect** |
| Integration, identity, deployment, governance | **System Architect** |

Salesforce classifies both credentials as **Advanced** level, Architect role. [^4]

It is worth understanding why the split is drawn there rather than somewhere else, because it is a real taxonomy of enterprise architecture work and not an arbitrary division of a syllabus. Everything on the application side happens inside a single trust boundary: the org itself. Everything on the system side crosses a trust boundary: between the org and the ERP, between the org and the identity provider, between a sandbox and production. Cross-boundary work is where contracts, credentials, retries, and failure semantics live, and it is where an outage in a system nobody in the Salesforce team owns can take down a Salesforce process. That is the seam the System Architect owns, and it is why the two credentials exist rather than one.

![Where the split is drawn: the Application Architect owns on-platform concerns inside a single trust boundary, while the System Architect owns off-platform concerns that cross it](</diagrams/what-salesforce-system-architect-does/2-on-off-platform-split.png>)

### 3. Technical Architect (CTA): the endgame

> Salesforce Certified Technical Architects have experience in designing and implementing secure, high-performance, integrated technical solutions using the Lightning platform within the context of a client's architectural landscape.
> [^5]

The CTA is the pinnacle credential. It requires **both** the Application Architect and System Architect credentials as prerequisites [^5], and then a two-step evaluation: an Architect Evaluation, then the Architect Review Board Exam. [^6]

Look at how the two-step evaluation mirrors the definition. The Architect Evaluation is a review of work you have actually done, which is why there is no version of the CTA you can pass by memorizing a study guide. The Review Board is a live scenario defense, which tests exactly the thing the definition names: designing a solution "within the context of a client's architectural landscape." The landscape is the operative phrase. The board is not testing whether you can design an elegant Salesforce solution in isolation. It is testing whether you can design one that fits a landscape you do not fully control, and then defend the tradeoffs you accepted.

Salesforce's own blog calls CTAs "the pinnacle credential holder" and "quite rare gems." [^7] The history explains why the bar is high:

- The CTA credential launched in **2011**; the first 10 CTAs were certified in September of that year.
- The **Architect Academy** launched in February 2016, the first official CTA preparation resources.
- At **Dreamforce '17**, Salesforce introduced the "Architect Journey": domain certifications as stepping stones to the CTA pinnacle.

[^7]

So the arc is: **eight domain prerequisite exams → Application Architect + System Architect → CTA**. That's the mountain. This track is the trail map.

![The certification arc: eight domain prerequisite exams feed the Application Architect and System Architect credentials, which together unlock the Technical Architect pinnacle with its two-step evaluation](</diagrams/what-salesforce-system-architect-does/1-certification-arc.png>)

If you count the exams on both prerequisite lists you will notice a couple of names that appear once each, and the arithmetic works out to eight distinct domain exams feeding the two domain credentials. That is a multi-year path for most people, and it is meant to be. The credential ladder is not a marketing funnel. It is a sequence that forces you to spend real time inside each concern before you are asked to judge across all of them.

## Worked example: who owns what

A client wants Salesforce integrated with their legacy ERP, with single sign-on for all employees, and a governed release process so the integration team can deploy weekly without breaking the org. Here's how the tiers divide the work:

- **Application Architect:** designs the Salesforce data model (e.g., how the ERP's orders map to custom objects and fields), the role hierarchy, and the sharing rules so each department sees only its records. Owns "inside the org."
- **System Architect:** designs the integration: how Salesforce and the ERP exchange data (API choice, sync vs async, error handling), how SSO federates identity into Salesforce, and the sandbox → production deployment pipeline with change governance. Owns "between systems."
- **CTA:** sits above both. The CTA weighs the tradeoffs end to end: is a custom integration better than a managed package? Is SSO via an existing IdP enough, or does the identity model need rework? When requirements conflict, the CTA makes the call and defends it. The Review Board exam is precisely this: a rigorous, scenario-based defense of a complete architecture. [^7]

![Who owns what in the ERP integration: the Application Architect owns the data model and sharing inside the org, the System Architect owns integration, SSO, and the release pipeline between systems, and the CTA weighs tradeoffs above both](</diagrams/what-salesforce-system-architect-does/3-who-owns-what.png>)

Now push on the seams, because that is where the split gets interesting. Take the ERP order mapping. The Application Architect decides that an external ERP order becomes a custom object with a lookup to Account and a unique external ID field. That is an on-platform call and it is theirs. But whether that record arrives via a nightly Bulk API job or a real-time platform event is a System Architect call, and the answer changes what the Application Architect must build: a nightly load means the org needs a way to reconcile deletions, while a real-time event means the org needs to handle duplicate or out-of-order deliveries. Neither architect can finish their design without the other's decision.

Take SSO. The System Architect owns the federation setup, the identity provider configuration, and the assertion handling. The Application Architect owns what happens once the user is inside: the role hierarchy, the permission set assignments, and the sharing model that the federated identity lands in. Identity is a system concern. Authorization is an application concern. Programs that let one team own both end up with either a sharing model that assumes identities nobody provisions, or an identity setup that grants access the sharing model was built to prevent.

Where the CTA earns the credential is the conflict case. Suppose the client insists on real-time order sync to the ERP, the ERP team says its API cannot sustain the volume, and the business says a nightly batch is unacceptable. There is no single-domain answer. The CTA has to weigh renegotiating the volume commitment, adding a queue and retry layer on the Salesforce side, or reshaping the process so that only priority orders need to be real-time. Whichever call gets made, the CTA owns the reasoning and has to be able to defend it to a board, which is exactly what the Review Board Exam simulates.

## The pitfall to avoid

**Treating the certificate as the job.** The System Architect certification is a *gate*: it proves you passed the four prerequisite exams, but the role is judgment: choosing between a synchronous REST call and a queued batch when the ERP is down, deciding where identity lives, designing a governance model that developers will actually follow. The exams test that you know the platform's patterns; the career is about knowing *when* to use them. Trailhead's career path says it best: "Good architecture is about balancing what works today with what will scale tomorrow." [^8]

A second, subtler trap: mixing up the two domain architects. "System Architect" is not "the architect who knows everything about systems." It is specifically the **off-platform** concern (integration, identity, governance), while the **Application Architect** owns the **on-platform** concern (data, sharing, native features). Getting that split wrong on an exam question is the fastest way to lose points. Get it wrong in a real org and you'll have two teams designing past each other.

There is a third trap worth naming, and it is the one that bites people who have already passed an exam. **Assuming the credentials are the destination rather than the entry ticket.** Passing four prerequisite exams proves you can recognize the platform's patterns under exam conditions. It does not prove you can sit in a room where the business wants one thing, the security team wants another, and the budget covers neither, and then produce a design those three parties can live with. That skill is built by shipping designs and living with the consequences, and it is why the CTA path requires documented real work before the board will even see you. If you are building a study plan from this lesson, build the credentials in parallel with real delivery work rather than before it.

The fourth trap is quieter and more common in consulting. **Optimizing for the demo instead of the steady state.** A design that works beautifully on clean data, at low volume, with one integration, is not a design. It is a demonstration. The architect's recurring question is what the same solution looks like in year three, after the integration count has tripled, the data has grown an order of magnitude, and the person who built it has left. Every one of the phrases in Salesforce's definition is pointed at that question.

## Try it

For each of these five requirements, decide: does it belong to the Application Architect's domain (on-platform) or the System Architect's domain (off-platform)?

1. "Sales reps need partner accounts to see only their own pipeline."
2. "Orders must flow from Salesforce to the ERP every 15 minutes."
3. "Employees log in with their corporate Okta credentials."
4. "The integration team needs a sandbox that mirrors production data."
5. "Opportunity stages must be standardized across all business units."

Then write one sentence for each naming the specific prerequisite credential that maps to it (e.g., #2 → Platform Integration Architect). When you're done, check the answers against the prerequisite lists linked in the Sources below.

Work the reasoning out loud rather than jumping to the label, because the label is easy once the reasoning is right. For #1, the question is where the visibility decision gets enforced: it is a sharing question inside the org, so the owner is the Application Architect and the mapping credential is Platform Sharing and Visibility Architect. For #2, the question is how data crosses a boundary and on what schedule, which is the integration concern and maps to Platform Integration Architect. For #3, the question is who vouches for a user's identity, which is an identity and access concern, and the credential is Platform Identity and Access Management Architect. For #4, the question is how change is controlled across environments, which is lifecycle and deployment, and the credential is Platform Development Lifecycle and Deployment Architect. For #5, standardizing a picklist across business units is a data model decision, so it belongs to the Application Architect and maps to Platform Data Architect. Notice that three of the five are System Architect concerns, which is roughly the ratio you will see in an enterprise program and the reason this track spends most of its time on the system side.
