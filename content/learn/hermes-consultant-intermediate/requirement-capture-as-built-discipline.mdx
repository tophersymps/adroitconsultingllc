# Lesson 2: Requirement Capture as an As-Built Discipline

Lesson 1 gave you the calendar: five phases mapped onto real weeks, with gates and buffers and a decision rule for slippage. But a plan holds only as well as the discovery underneath it, and discovery pays off only if you capture what you found. This lesson is the deep dive on that. It teaches you requirement capture as an as-built discipline: how to record what the client actually does and needs so that nothing important depends on your memory, and how to write requirements that survive the handover to the team that maintains the system after you leave.

Level 1 taught you to listen, write a problem statement the client agrees with, and scope the work. That gets you to the start line. The gap between Level 1 and here is the gap between agreeing on a problem and pinning it down so precisely that the build and the handover both survive contact with the client's real world. Most engagements do not die from bad problems. They die from requirements that were never precise enough to be true for more than a week, or that lived only in the head of the consultant who wrote them.

## The as-built discipline, defined

Here is the core distinction that makes this lesson work. There are two versions of every system: the one people describe and the one that actually runs. Consultants call the first the aspirational view. It is the process as documented, the rule as remembered, the happy path as imagined. The second is the as-built view. It is the process as performed, the rule as enforced, the exceptions as they actually occur.

An engagement survives on the as-built view. A system you build to the aspirational view works in the demo and breaks in week one, because the real process diverged from the described one and nobody captured the divergence. Handover fails for the same reason. You hand the client a runbook that matches the process on paper, and their own team runs a different process, so the runbook reads like fiction.

The as-built discipline is the habit of writing down what the system actually does and needs, in enough detail that the reality is preserved even after you are gone. It is a discipline, not a technique, because it is a set of habits you apply continuously rather than a step you take once. You capture the enacted rule, not the remembered one. You write down the exception the moment you see it. You test the requirement before you declare it captured. And you treat your own memory as the last place a fact is allowed to live.

## Why memory is the enemy

The person who builds a system carries it in their head: which rule governs this case, why the agent refuses that input, where the data lives, which edge case was the one that ate two days. All of it is true and all of it is useless, because the team that maintains the system after you leave was never in the room. The value of what you know is zero until it is written down in a form someone else can act on.

Measure every requirement against that test. A requirement is captured, not when you understand it, but when the next person can run the system and change it from what you wrote, without you. If a new owner has to call you to answer "why does the agent refuse that input," the requirement was never captured. It was held. And held knowledge evaporates the day you are not available.

This is why the as-built discipline is the thing that makes handover real. Handover is not a transfer of files. It is a transfer of the ability to change the system. That ability exists only if the captured requirements are specific enough, and truthful enough, for the new owner to trust them as the ground truth of how the system works.

## The four parts of a requirement that survives

A requirement is not a sentence like "the agent drafts the report." That is a wish, not a requirement. A requirement that survives handover has four parts, and if any part is missing you have not finished capturing it.

**The behavior.** What the system does. The agent reads the three source systems, applies the reconciliation rules, and drafts the Monday report.

**The rule.** The governing logic stated precisely enough to be encoded and tested. This is the reconciliation rule itself: when the warehouse total and the sales total disagree, use the warehouse figure, and only when the difference is under a defined tolerance. A rule is not a rule until it has its exact condition and its exact threshold.

**The exception.** What the system refuses or flags instead of guessing. When a number does not reconcile, the agent does not invent an explanation. It posts the mismatch to the channel and leaves the case for a human. The exception is where an agent earns or loses its safety, and it is the part consultants most often skip because it only shows up when you watch the real process.

**The boundary.** What the system is explicitly not doing, and who is allowed to change that. The agent never sends the report to the director without a human approval. It never writes to the source systems. The boundary is the guardrail, and it must be written down as firmly as the capability, because it is what protects the client from the worst version of the tool.

Every requirement you capture should have all four. Behavior without a rule is a slogan. Rule without an exception is an assumption that never breaks until it does. Boundary without an owner is a hope. Write all four, for every requirement, and you have a requirement that can be built, tested, and handed over.

## Capture the enacted, not the stated

The single biggest source of divergence between the aspirational and the as-built is that people describe the process they intend, not the process they perform. The documentation says the team reconciles three systems. Watch them and you find they reconcile two and hand-check the third, because the third system's export is unreliable. The stated rule is one thing. The enacted rule, the one your agent has to match, is the other.

So the discipline has a field component, and it is the same discipline discovery taught you. Watch the work happen, do not just ask about it. Interview the operators, not only the sponsor. Ask "what happens today when this goes wrong," and write down the answer, because that answer is your exception list. The divergence between the documented process and the performed one is not a bug in your discovery. It is the raw material. Every divergence you catch is a requirement you would otherwise have gotten wrong, and every one you miss becomes a failure the agent ships with.

Capture both versions and label them. Keep a column for the stated rule and a column for the enacted rule, and make the build match the enacted one. When you present the requirements to the client, show them the divergence you found. It is the most convincing thing you can do, because it proves you actually watched the work, and it forces the client to confront the gap between what they think they do and what they do. That conversation, uncomfortable as it is, is where the requirements stop being guesses.

## Where the requirements live in the system

For an agent delivery, the captured requirements do not stay on a document. They get embodied in the running system, and the as-built discipline requires you to keep the two in sync. In Hermes Agent, the concrete working example here, the requirements land in four places, and each one maps to a part of the four-part requirement. [^1]

The behavior lives in the profile: the agent definition that names what the agent is for and which connectors it uses to reach the source systems. [^2] The rule lives in the skills: the written procedures that encode the reconciliation logic and the exact conditions under which each action is valid. [^3] The cadence lives in the schedule that runs the agent on the right rhythm, the Monday-morning trigger that fires the report build. [^4] And the boundary lives in the guardrails and the approval flow: the configuration that says the agent never sends anything without a human, and refuses to guess on a mismatch. [^5]

The point is that in an agent delivery, the requirements and the implementation are the same artifact viewed two ways. The document is the human-readable twin of what the profile and skills encode. That makes the as-built discipline both easier and harder. Easier because the captured requirement is directly testable: you run the agent and check whether it behaves the way the requirement says. Harder because the document and the system can drift apart. The document gets edited for the client, the skills get edited for the machine, and if nobody reconciles them, the runbook stops matching the running system. The discipline is to treat the document and the system as two views of the same requirement, and to update both together or neither.

## Worked example: the Monday report, captured as-built

Take the six-week logistics engagement from Lesson 1 and run it through the as-built discipline. Discovery produced a problem statement: assembling the Monday report eats four people for a morning and produces inconsistent numbers. Now capture the requirements underneath it, as-built, in the four-part shape.

Start with the behavior. The agent reads the three source systems, applies the reconciliation rules the team confirmed, drafts the Monday report, and posts a draft into the team channel for a human to approve before it goes to the operations director.

Then the rule, and this is where the field component matters. The documented process says the team reconciles all three systems equally. Watch them and the enacted rule is different. Two systems reconcile cleanly against each other. The third, the warehouse system, exports figures that are frequently stale by a day. The team does not reconcile the third system; they treat its numbers as fixed and hand-check only the outliers. So the enacted rule is not "reconcile all three." It is "reconcile the two clean sources, treat the warehouse system as authoritative, and flag any warehouse figure that moved more than the defined tolerance from the previous week." That is the rule the agent gets encoded, and it is only findable by watching the work.

Then the exception. The rule produces a hard case: a warehouse total that jumps by far more than the tolerance, which usually means a loading dock error, a data-entry slip, or a genuinely new shipment. The team cannot tell which from the number alone. The agent cannot tell either. The captured exception is: when the warehouse figure exceeds the tolerance, the agent does not reconcile it away. It posts the mismatch, names it as unresolved, and leaves the case for a human before the report is finalized. That exception is the requirement that keeps the agent honest, and it came straight from the "what happens when this goes wrong" conversation.

Then the boundary. The agent never sends the report to the director without a human approval on the draft. It never writes to any source system. It never edits the approved numbers after signoff. The boundary has an owner: the operations director owns the approval step, and the team lead owns the mismatch decisions. Write the owner down, because a boundary without an owner is a hope, and hopes do not survive handover.

Now write those four requirements into a register, one row per requirement, with the stated rule, the enacted rule, the exception, and the boundary. That register is the deliverable of capture. It is precise enough to build to, it is honest enough to test against, and it is complete enough to hand over. The new owner who inherits the system reads the register and knows exactly what the agent does, why it refuses certain inputs, and who is allowed to change the boundaries. They can change a rule without calling you, because the rule is written down, traced to where it lives in the system, and testable.

## The verification loop

A requirement is not captured until it is demonstrated. Writing it down is necessary, but it is not enough, because writing down the wrong rule is worse than writing nothing. The as-built discipline closes with a verification loop: test each captured requirement against real data, with the owner watching, and treat the test as the proof that the captured requirement matches the enacted reality.

In the logistics example, this is the parallel run from Lesson 1, where the agent drafts the Monday report against two live weeks alongside the human team. Every difference between the agent's draft and the team's is a curriculum. Each one is either a rule the consultant captured wrong, a case the agent should handle, or a case it should refuse. You update the requirement, re-test, and only declare it captured when the agent's behavior matches what the requirement says, on real data, repeatedly.

This is what turns the register from a document into a guarantee. The client signs off on the register, the agent is validated against it, and the register becomes the acceptance contract for the build. It is also what makes handover clean. You do not hand the client a promise that the system does what the requirements say. You hand them the validation record that proves it, on their own data. The new owner does not have to trust you. They have to trust the record, and the record is evidence, not memory.

## The pitfalls that break capture

**Capturing the stated process and stopping.** The biggest error. You interview the sponsor, write down the documented process, and never watch the work. Your requirements match the fiction and the build fails in week one. The discipline has a field component. If you have not watched the process run, you have not captured it.

**Writing behavior without the rule.** "The agent reconciles the systems" is not a requirement. It becomes one only when you write the exact condition and threshold. Behavior without a rule cannot be encoded and cannot be tested, which means it cannot survive handover.

**Skipping the exception.** The happy path is easy. The exception, the case where the agent refuses instead of guessing, is where the safety lives, and it only appears when you ask what happens when things go wrong. Consultants who skip it ship agents that fabricate explanations on mismatched data. That is the failure the client remembers.

**Letting the document and the system drift.** You edit the runbook for the client and the skills for the machine, and they stop matching. The new owner reads a runbook that does not match the running system and learns not to trust either. Update both together or neither.

**Treating memory as storage.** The facts live in your head. The engagement closes, you leave, and the knowledge leaves with you. The measure of a requirement is not whether you understand it. It is whether the next person can run and change the system from what you wrote. If they cannot, you did not capture it, you held it.

## Try it

Take the discovery work you did in Level 1, or the engagement you planned in Lesson 1. Turn it into an as-built capture.

1. For the core task, write the behavior in one sentence: what the system does.
2. Write the enacted rule with its exact condition and threshold. If you can, watch the work or interview the operator to find the divergence between the stated process and the performed one, and write both.
3. Write the exception: the case the system refuses or flags instead of guessing. Ask "what happens when this goes wrong" and capture the answer.
4. Write the boundary: what the system explicitly does not do, and who owns the right to change that.
5. Assemble a register, one row per requirement, with all four parts, plus the stated rule and the enacted rule labeled separately.
6. Write the verification plan: how you will prove each requirement on real data with the owner watching, and what counts as the case where you re-test and update the requirement.

If you can defend all six for a real task, you are not just scoping an engagement. You are building the artifact that survives it.
