# Lesson 22: Building Repeatable Processes: Checklists, Templates, and a Delivery Framework v1

Through twenty-one lessons you have learned to capture requirements, estimate, price, split work across a team, run incidents, review security, handle difficult clients, and hand a system over so the client can run it. Each of those lessons taught a technique. What none of them taught you is how a practice stops depending on one person remembering all of it. The consultant who runs every engagement as a fresh improvisation is not compounding. They are repeating the same learning curve at a new client, paying again for lessons they already paid for, and the price of that repeat shows up in the estimate, the schedule, and the quality.

This lesson is where you stop doing that. You build a delivery framework, version 1, out of three assets: checklists that catch the failures you have actually hit, templates that hold the reasoning behind past decisions, and a sequence of phases with gates that turns delivery into a repeatable process. The v1 in the title is deliberate. You are not building the perfect system on your first pass. You are building the honest first cut, with the expectation that every engagement after it improves it. Hermes Agent is the concrete vehicle for the worked examples, but the skill you are learning, how to design, extract, and version a delivery process, transfers to any tool and any engagement.

## The problem a framework solves

Before the framework, your delivery knowledge lives in one place: your head, and the heads of the people who worked with you. That is fine for the first few engagements. A solo consultant can hold the whole shape of a small engagement in mind, run it, and adjust on the fly. The break happens the moment you grow past that. When a second consultant delivers a similar engagement, or a client hands you work while you are mid-delivery on another, the knowledge has to move from the heads it lives in to a form the next person can pick up cold. That move does not happen by itself.

The failure mode this lesson guards against is not incompetence. It is the silent loss of the lessons each engagement taught. A checklist item you dropped, a scope boundary you forgot to restate, a step in the handover you skipped because it was the tenth time you had done it, these are not mistakes of skill. They are mistakes of memory, and memory is the worst delivery system a practice can run on because it looks reliable until it is not. The incidents you studied in Lesson 11 were almost always a memory failure somewhere in the chain: a guardrail documented and then not enforced, an eval gate scheduled and then skipped, a model swap logged and then forgotten. A framework exists to move those lessons out of memory and into a place that loads them on every engagement, whether or not the person running it remembers they exist.

## The three assets, and what each one actually does

The phrase "repeatable process" bundles three different things, and they fail in different ways if you treat them as one. A checklist, a template, and a framework are not synonyms for "being organized." They each do a specific job, and you will design each one against a different failure.

A checklist is a short list of the high-consequence steps that get dropped under pressure. Its job is to catch the failure, not to teach the process. A pilot does not consult a checklist to learn how to fly; they use it to make sure the one step that kills people when it is forgotten actually happened. A delivery checklist works the same way. It is not a restatement of your plan. It is a pre-mortem device: a list of the steps where failure is silent, expensive, and historically real, so that running through it before you move on proves those steps are done.

A template is a captured decision history. The difference between a blank form and a useful template is that the useful template carries the reasoning. Every field in a good template exists because a past engagement learned that the answer to that question matters. The template is not a blank page you fill in; it is a record of the questions that have already cost you something when you did not ask them. That is why a template is versioned. A template you never revise is a template that has stopped teaching, because the engagement that just taught you something new is not allowed to improve it.

A delivery framework is the sequence of phases with explicit gates that turns delivery into a process. It is the skeleton the whole engagement runs on: what work happens in what order, what output has to exist before you move to the next phase, and who signs off at each gate. The framework is what makes estimation, pricing, staffing, and quality predictable, because you are estimating and pricing a known process rather than guessing at an unknown one. It is the connective tissue that the checklists and templates hang on.

## Designing the checklist against your real failures

The checklist is where most consultants go wrong first, because they design it as a to-do list. A to-do list restates the work you already planned. It adds length, not safety. A checklist protects you only when it names the step that, when dropped, causes damage that is silent at the moment of dropping. That means you cannot invent a good checklist from a textbook. You have to extract it from the failures you have already lived through.

The design rule is short and specific. A checklist item is a single step that is either done or not done, written so that anyone can verify it without context. "Confirm the client's data source is reachable and the schema matches the as-built register before the first import" is a checklist item. "Handle data carefully" is not. The list is short because a long list stops being consulted under pressure, which is exactly when it is needed. A pre-delivery checklist of six or eight items, each one a step that has actually failed for you or your team before, is worth more than a thirty-item list that nobody reads.

The raw material for those items is your incident history. Lesson 11 taught you to treat every incident as a source of process improvement. This lesson closes that loop. Every incident you resolved is a candidate checklist item, because an incident is by definition a failure that slipped past the process you had. Naming it as a checklist item does not prevent all future versions of it, but it removes the silent part: the next time the situation arises, the item is there to be checked, and the consultant has to consciously decide to skip it.

## The template as a decision history, not a blank form

The template is the asset that does the most to compound your work, and it is the one that most often gets built wrong. A consultant who creates a blank project template with empty sections for scope, schedule, and budget has built a form, not a template. The empty sections do not tell the next person what to think about. They just give them boxes to fill, which is exactly as much guidance as no template at all.

A template earns its name when the field itself encodes the question that a past engagement learned to ask. Consider a kickoff meeting template. The blank version has an agenda: introductions, objectives, roles, timeline. The decision-history version has fields that carry the reasoning. It asks for the stated process versus the enacted process, because Lesson 2 taught you that the client's description of their workflow and the way it actually runs are rarely the same thing. It asks who the real decision maker is versus who sits in the room, because a past engagement learned that the gatekeeper is not the buyer. It asks what the client considers done, because acceptance was a fight in an earlier engagement when the two sides held different definitions.

Every one of those fields exists because a real engagement learned that the answer matters. That is what makes the template transferable. A consultant who picks up your template is not filling in blanks. They are answering the questions you have already paid to discover are important. The as-built register from Lesson 2 is the natural raw material for building these fields, because it is exactly the record of what past engagements enacted and what they learned.

## The framework v1: sequence and gates

The framework is the asset that makes the other two hang together, and it is the one you version most explicitly. The v1 in this lesson's title is a commitment: you are building the first version of a framework you expect to revise, not the final version of a method you will defend forever. A framework that cannot change is a cage, and a consultant who treats their own process as fixed has stopped learning from the engagements they run.

The core of a delivery framework is the phase sequence with gates. You already know the phases from Level 1: discover, design, implement, handover, support. What the intermediate practitioner adds is the gate discipline: each phase produces a defined output, and work does not move into the next phase until that output exists and is agreed. The discovery phase produces the as-built register and the requirements set. The design phase produces the architecture and the scope boundary. The implementation phase produces the working system and the eval results against the golden set. The handover produces the operating docs and the trained operator. The support phase produces the run record and the review that feeds the next engagement. Each gate is the answer to one question: is the output of this phase good enough to build the next phase on, and who has to say so.

The gate is what makes delivery repeatable, because it is the point where variation gets caught early. A discovery phase that rushes out a requirements set with no sign-off produces a design phase that builds the wrong thing. The gate does not prevent all of that, but it makes the failure cheap and visible instead of expensive and late. It is also the point where the framework connects to your estimation and pricing from Lessons 6 and 18. You can price a framework you know, because the phases and their outputs are a known quantity with a known failure profile. You cannot price an improvisation.

## Build it by extraction, not invention

The single most important design rule in this lesson is that you do not build the framework from a textbook, a competitor's process, or a generic template you found online. You extract it from the engagements you have actually run. The distinction is the whole difference between a process that protects you and a process that impresses you. A textbook framework describes how delivery should go in the abstract. It has no memory of the specific failure that your second client hit when the export changed schema on a Tuesday, or the gatekeeper who went silent while holding the security approval, or the catalog defect that turned out to be the client's to own. Those specifics are exactly the lessons a framework exists to carry, and only your own engagement history contains them.

The extraction loop is simple and it is the mechanism your framework runs on. After every engagement, run a review that asks three questions. What worked and should be repeated exactly as it was? What failed or nearly failed, and what step, if added, would have caught it earlier? What decision did we make that the next person will need to make again, and what context will they need to make it well? The first answer becomes a step to protect. The second becomes a checklist item or a gate. The third becomes a field in a template. Then you bump the version.

That loop is why the framework is v1 rather than the final method. Version 1 is what you can build honestly after the engagements you have already run. Every engagement after it feeds the loop and produces a v2, a v3, and so on. A framework that never increments is a sign that you are not actually running the review, or that the review findings are not being allowed to change the process. Both are failures of the framework's core purpose.

## Hermes as the mechanism for running the framework

The framework is the transferable asset, but it has to be operated somewhere, and for you that somewhere is Hermes Agent. Hermes gives the three assets a concrete home, and it is worth being precise about which capability carries which asset, because conflating them is a common mistake. [^1]

A Hermes skill is the natural home for a checklist and its accompanying procedure. Skills are built for progressive disclosure: the agent loads the working procedure and knowledge for a task only when that task is relevant, which is exactly how a checklist should behave, present and available when you start the work, not cluttering every other engagement. [^1] Your pre-delivery checklist, your handover checklist, your scope-boundary checklist, each becomes a skill that loads when the engagement reaches the phase it protects.

A scheduled job is the home for the steps that should run on their own. Hermes cron can run a script on a schedule with no agent involved, and it produces a record of what each run did, which is the mechanism your recurring process steps and your quality gates can run on without waiting for someone to remember them. [^2] The eval gate you built in Lesson 9, the runbook check that should fire after every deployment, the weekly review that feeds the extraction loop, these are cron jobs. Automating a step you are going to do anyway is not laziness. It is the framework enforcing itself.

Templates live wherever your team can reach them, and the discipline from Lesson 4, where you packaged your delivery toolkit as reusable skills, is what turns a pile of documents into a working template library. The point to hold on to is that the mechanism matters less than the design. You could run this framework with a shared folder, a paper checklist, and a calendar reminder. Hermes makes it cheaper to run, but the asset that compounds is the framework you extracted, not the tool you extracted it into.

## Worked example: building v1 after three engagements

Put the pieces together with a concrete practitioner. You have delivered three small agent engagements and you are about to take a fourth, a slightly larger one, on your own. Before you scope it, you build your framework v1.

The extraction loop gives you the material. From the first engagement you remember that you skipped a proper discovery sign-off and the design drifted for two weeks. From the second, a data source changed its schema without notice and the first import failed. From the third, the handover lacked a clear owner for the runbook, and support calls bounced until you named one. Three lessons, three assets.

Your checklist gets three items that map to those failures: confirm the data source is reachable and the schema matches the as-built register before the first import; hold a discovery review and get the client's written sign-off on the requirements before design starts; name a single support owner for the runbook before handover. You add three more items from your team's shared incidents, and you stop there. Eight items, each one a step that has genuinely failed, each one verifiable.

Your kickoff template gets fields that encode the same history: the stated process versus the enacted process, the decision maker versus the people in the room, the client's definition of done, and the data-source facts you need before week one. Every field has a reason behind it, and a new consultant can see the reason in the field's label rather than having to rediscover it.

Your framework v1 is the five phases with the gate discipline: discovery ends when the as-built register and the requirements set are signed off; design ends when the scope boundary and the architecture are agreed; implementation ends when the system passes the golden set on the eval gate; handover ends when the runbook has an owner and the operator is trained; support runs on a schedule and feeds the next review. It is not elaborate. It is honest, it carries the three failures you have actually paid for, and it is versioned so the fourth engagement can improve it.

## The try-it: run your own extraction loop

Before the next engagement, run the loop on your own history. Pick the most recent engagement you delivered or helped deliver. Answer the three questions for it: what worked and should be repeated, what failed and should be caught earlier, and what decision the next person will have to make again. Turn the failure into one checklist item written as a verifiable step. Turn the decision into one template field. Write the two together as a single artifact, call it your framework v1, and put it where your next engagement will load it.

The point is not the artifact. The point is that you have started the loop, because the loop, run after every engagement, is what turns a consultant into a practice.

## The pitfalls that turn a framework into busywork

**Designing v1 from a textbook.** A generic framework carries no memory of your failures, so it protects you from nothing specific. Extract the process from the engagements you have run, and let v1 be the honest first cut.

**Building a to-do list and calling it a checklist.** A checklist names the silent, expensive, historically real step and is short enough to be consulted under pressure. If you could not point to the failure it prevents, it does not belong on it.

**Templates as blank forms.** An empty section tells the next person nothing. A field earns its place when a past engagement learned the answer matters, and the field's label should carry that reasoning.

**A framework without gates.** Phases with no defined outputs and no sign-off points are a timeline, not a framework. The gate is where a failure becomes cheap and visible, and it is what makes the process estimable and priceable.

**Never versioning.** A template or framework that does not increment after a review is a sign the review findings are not allowed to change the process, which is the framework failing at its only job.

**Automating before the process is stable.** Cron is powerful, but a scheduled job running a step that is not yet defined well enough to run on its own just automates the confusion. Get the process right first, then automate it. [^2]

## What's next

You have built the machinery that makes delivery repeatable: checklists that catch your real failures, templates that carry your decision history, and a framework v1 with gates that make the process known and estimable. But a repeatable process is only worth something if the buyer can see it worked. The next lesson, measuring outcomes and reporting value to the buyer, is where the framework proves itself. It teaches you to define the metrics that show the delivered agent actually produced the result the buyer signed for, to tie those metrics back to the business case and the acceptance criteria in your SOW, and to report that value in a form the buyer can act on. Where this lesson made delivery predictable, the next one makes it demonstrable.
