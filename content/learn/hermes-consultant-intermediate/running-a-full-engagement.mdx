# Lesson 1: Running a Full Engagement

Level 1 gave you the container: five phases, discover, design, implement, handover, support, each with a product and an exit test. That lifecycle is true but abstract. It tells you what has to happen, not when, not how long each part gets, and not how to keep a multi-week engagement alive while it runs. This lesson does the next thing. It turns the lifecycle into a calendar. You will learn how to map the five phases onto a real week-by-week plan, how to set gates that hold, how much buffer to carry, and where the schedule quietly goes wrong on real engagements. The working example is a six-week Hermes Agent delivery, and the skill transfers to any tool you ever run.

The jump from Level 1 to here is the jump from knowing the phases to running the engagement. Both require the same five phases. The difference is that a running engagement has a deadline, a client calendar, a budget that depletes every Friday, and a reviewer whose availability you do not control. A plan that ignores those is a diagram, not a plan.

## What a week-by-week plan actually is

A phase is a guarantee about sequencing. A plan is a guarantee about time. When you write a week-by-week plan you are committing, out loud, to three things at once: what each week produces, what has to be true before the week ends, and what you do when a week slips. Most consultants write the first one and skip the other two. That is why most plans look good on Monday and are fiction by Wednesday.

Here is the core idea that makes a plan hold together. A phase is not a task you spend weeks on. It is a decision you have to reach, and the decision has a natural moment. Discovery does not take three weeks because the work is huge. It takes as long as it takes to make the client agree on the problem and sign the scope. Design takes as long as it takes to get signoff on what will be built. Implementation takes as long as it takes to build to that signoff and prove it. Each phase ends at a gate, not at a time.

So the discipline of turning phases into weeks is this: you decide the gate for each phase first, then you size how long it will realistically take to reach it, then you lay those blocks on the calendar in order. You do not decide how many weeks the project has and then cram the phases into it. The first method gives you a plan you can defend. The second gives you a plan you will apologize for.

There is one more distinction worth naming before the example. A week-block is not a week of working time. A discovery block of "week 1" does not mean you have forty hours of discovery work. It means discovery is the priority for that week, and you finish it by the end of the week, usually with less than the full week of actual work. The slack inside the block is your first buffer. Most people plan back to back, task to task, with zero slack, and then wonder why the first slip cascades through the whole calendar.

## The scheduling realities that shape every plan

Before you lay out a single week, get honest about the four forces that will fight your plan. Every engagement hits them. The ones that go wrong are the ones that did not plan for them.

**Client availability is the binding constraint, not your effort.** You can work on your own calendar. The client's stakeholders, the ones who have to answer questions, approve the scope, review the build, and sign off, have their own jobs. Their availability decides how fast discovery and design can actually go. If your plan assumes the operations lead answers within a day, and they answer within a week, your plan is wrong before it starts. The move is to find out who must approve what, and how fast they respond, in the first conversation, and to schedule review gates around their calendar, not yours.

**Review is a bottleneck, and it is almost never where you think.** The expensive part of a build is not writing it. It is the loop of show the client, get feedback, change it, show them again. Each loop eats real time, and the number of loops is something you do not fully control. Plan for a minimum of two review cycles on the design and the validation, and treat a third as normal. If you size the plan assuming one review and the client asks for three, the plan dies of review, not of bad code.

**Slip compounds through dependencies.** The phases are sequential for a reason. If discovery runs a week late, design starts a week late, and so does implementation, and so does handover. A one-week slip anywhere is a one-week slip at the end unless you rebuild the plan, not just push every date. The discipline is to notice the slip at the gate, not at the end. That is why each gate has a pass or fail and a decision. When a gate slips, you make a call immediately: absorb the slip with buffer, cut scope, or tell the client the date moves. You never let a slip ride silently.

**Handover needs runway, not a day.** The single most common way a plan fails is that implementation eats the schedule and handover gets a weekend. A system nobody can run is a failed delivery by the only definition that matters. Handover is documentation, training, and transfer of ownership, and it takes real, scheduled time. Protect it in the plan the way you protect the build. If you have to steal time from somewhere to save the date, handover is the last place you steal it from.

## The six-week example

Put it together. A mid-size logistics company wants to automate a weekly reporting task. Every Monday a team of four spends the morning pulling data from three systems, reconciling it, and building a status report that goes to the operations director. The job is tedious, error-prone, and everyone hates it. The company has no dedicated IT team, which means the consultant is also, in practice, the system administrator. The engagement is fixed-price, six weeks, and it has to include handover, because there is nobody else who will run the system.

Here is the week-by-week plan.

**Week 1: Discovery.** The consultant interviews the four report-builders, the operations director, and the person who owns the source systems. They watch a Monday-morning report build happen, not to be polite but because the process on paper and the process in practice diverge, and the divergence is where the requirements hide. They map the data sources, the reconciliation rules, and who approves the final numbers. They land the problem statement, one sentence the client nods at: assembling the Monday report eats four people for a morning and produces inconsistent numbers. They make the honest no-go or go call. It is a go: high volume, low judgment, rules that can be encoded, and a human signoff step satisfies the risk. The gate: the client has agreed to the problem statement and the consultant has a written scope that both sides sign by the end of the week.

**Week 2: Design.** The consultant writes the shape of the solution. A Hermes Agent profile reads the three source systems through their connectors, applies the reconciliation rules the team confirmed, drafts the report, flags any number that does not reconcile instead of guessing, and posts the draft into the team's channel for a human to approve before it goes to the director. [^1] The guardrails are explicit: the agent never sends anything without approval, never overwrites a source system, and refuses to guess on a mismatch. The design goes to the operations director for review. The gate: written signoff on the design, including the boundaries, before any code exists. The consultant books the design review for midweek, not Friday, so a requested change can be folded in and resubmitted inside the week.

**Week 3: Implement, first pass.** The consultant stands up the profile in the company's environment, connects the three data sources, writes the reconciliation skill from the confirmed rules, and builds the approval flow into the team's channel. This week is about getting the pipeline real and the edge cases visible. The gate at the end of the week is internal: the agent produces a correct report on the last week's real, anonymized data, and the cases it cannot reconcile are flagged, not fabricated.

**Week 4: Implement, validate with the client.** The consultant runs the agent against two live weeks in parallel with the human team. The team compares the agent's draft to their own, and the differences are the curriculum: each difference is either a rule the consultant missed, a case the agent should handle, or a case it should refuse. The validation record grows: cases handled, cases refused, failures caught and safe. The gate at the end of the week is the client agreeing that the agent does what the scope promised, demonstrated on their real data, not on the consultant's demo.

**Week 5: Handover.** This week is not spare time. The consultant writes the runbook: how the profile is configured, how to add a data source, how to change a reconciliation rule, how to recover from a failed run, how to update the skill. They train the four report-builders and the one person who will own the system going forward. They transfer the keys: the owner can change the system without the consultant in the room. The gate is demonstrated, not claimed. The consultant watches the new owner make a change end to end before the week is over. [^2]

**Week 6: Support and close.** The agent runs in production for the first full Monday under supervision. The consultant watches real volume, fixes the edge cases that only appear when the system runs for everyone, and confirms the Monday report went out with the human approval step intact. The support window closes with a record of what changed during the week and a clear written decision about whether the company wants an ongoing retainer. The gate: a clean close, with the client able to run the system and a documented handoff.

Now step back and look at the shape of it. Discovery and design together are two of six weeks, implementation is two, and handover and support are two. That is not an accident. It is the honest distribution for a fixed-price delivery that has to leave the client self-sufficient. A consultant who spends four of six weeks building and two weeks handover is planning a different engagement, one where they stay involved. If the goal is to make yourself unnecessary, handover gets its share of the calendar, because that is the phase that makes you unnecessary.

## What the example is not telling you

The plan above reads clean. The real engagement will not. Three things will happen that no plan removes, and the difference between a consultant who survives them and one who does not is how they built the plan to absorb them.

The first is that the gate will slip somewhere. Real stakeholders miss meetings, real data is messier than the demo, real approval takes longer than the email promised. The plan has to have slack built in, and it has to have a decision rule for when a gate slips. The rule that works is simple: when a gate slips, do not push every later date by reflex. Decide what gives. Cut the optional nice-to-have from the build, or tell the client the date moves, or absorb it from the slack you deliberately carried. The wrong move, every time, is to pretend the gate did not slip and let the calendar quietly compress.

The second is scope drift dressed as iteration. The client will ask for a good thing in week 4: can the report also include the warehouse metric? It is a reasonable request. It is also new scope, and if you fold it in without a conversation about time and price, you have just signed a free upgrade. The discipline is to treat every request past the signed scope as a change request, to say yes to the good ones, and to name the trade in the same sentence: that adds two days, here is what I will drop to hold the date, or here is what it costs. Saying yes without naming the trade is how fixed-price engagements quietly become fixed-price, plus a lot of free work.

The third is that handover will resist being scheduled, and the client will help you skip it. Everyone is busy, the report works, do we really need the training? Yes. A system that works only while you are in the room is not a delivery. Schedule handover as firmly as the build, demonstrate the owner's independence, and refuse to let the last week collapse into more implementation. The client who is too busy for handover today is the client who calls you, angry, six weeks after you leave.

## Try it

Take the engagement you planned in Level 1, or any real one you can imagine. Turn it into a six-week week-by-week plan on paper.

1. Write the gate for each of the five phases in one line each. Discovery ends when the client signs the scope. Design ends when they sign the build. Implement ends when they agree it works on real data. Handover ends when the owner runs it without you. Support ends when the window closes with a record.
2. Lay the phases on six weeks in order. Decide the distribution, and be honest about why you gave handover the weeks you did.
3. Write two buffers: one named block of slack somewhere in the plan, and one decision rule for what you do when a gate slips.
4. Write the three things you will refuse to let creep: the request you will treat as a change, the review loop you will cap, and the handover day you will not sacrifice.

If you can defend all four without reaching for the tool, you are planning an engagement, not just building into one.

## What's next

You have a calendar: five phases mapped onto six weeks, with gates and slack and a decision rule for slippage. But a plan is only as good as the discovery underneath it, and discovery lives or dies on one discipline: capturing requirements precisely enough that the handover and the build survive contact with the client's real world. The next lesson goes deep on that. It teaches you requirement capture as an as-built discipline, how to record what the client actually does and needs so that nothing important depends on memory, and how to write requirements that survive a handover to the team that has to maintain the system after you leave. That is where a running engagement turns into a repeatable one.
