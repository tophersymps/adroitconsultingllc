# Lesson 14: Managing a Small Portfolio of Clients: Scope, Expectations, and Time

Lesson 13 made you optional in each delivery, so you can finally take on a second client without leaving the first one stranded. That is the moment the course changes character. Every lesson so far treated the engagement as the unit: one client, one scope, one delivery, your full attention. This lesson takes the view one level up. The unit is now the portfolio, the set of clients you hold at once, and the skill is keeping several engagements alive and honest at the same time. It is a different craft, and it fails in ways single-client delivery never exposes.

The hard truth this lesson rests on is that a portfolio of clients is not three of the same job running in parallel. It is three commitments sharing one brain, one calendar, and one reputation. When a solo consultant takes a second client, they do not gain a customer. They gain a competition for their own attention, and that competition is the whole subject here. The three enemies are scope bleeding across engagements, expectations that outrun your actual availability, and time that you budget as if context switching were free. None of them is a client problem. All three are yours, and this lesson teaches you to name, measure, and control each one.

## What changes when there is more than one client

Single-client delivery has a forgiving shape. Your scope is the client's scope. Your expectation is the client's expectation. Your time is entirely the client's time. Everything points one direction, and when something slips, there is one place it can fall. The moment you add a second client, that shape breaks, and the break is structural, not a matter of working harder.

The first structural change is that scope now has a boundary you have to defend. In a single engagement, scope creep is a conversation about whether an extra deliverable fits. In a portfolio, scope creep in one engagement is a theft from another, because the hours a stray request eats come out of a budget you already promised to someone else. The cost of the creep is not just extra work. It is a missed commitment to a different client, and that client notices even if the first one is thrilled.

The second change is that your attention is now a scarce, divided resource, and every client operates with the assumption that they have it. Each client builds a mental model of how fast you respond, how present you are, how available you are in a crisis. Those models form from your behavior, and they persist. If you respond to client A in an hour and to client B in three days, both clients learn something, and neither of them learns what you intended to teach.

The third change is that time stops being a single budget and becomes a portfolio allocation. When you had one client, you could spend freely up to the deadline. With several, you are deciding, every week, how much of your finite capacity each engagement gets, and every allocation is a decision to not spend it somewhere else. The discipline is treating that allocation as an explicit, reviewed decision rather than whatever the loudest client happened to extract that week.

## Scope: the boundary you have to defend

Scope in a portfolio is not about saying no to work. It is about knowing which client the work belongs to, and that turns out to be harder than it sounds, because client work does not arrive neatly labeled. A question about client A's reconciliation rules arrives while you are deep in client B's dashboard. A feature request that "reminds you of" something you built for client C lands in your inbox. The danger is that the work migrates across boundaries without anyone deciding it should, and it lands wherever your attention happens to be at the time.

The fix is to make the boundary a deliberate artifact instead of a habit. You define, for each engagement, the exact list of deliverables you are responsible for, the definition of done for each, and the explicit list of things that are out of scope and belong to a future phase or a separate arrangement. You write this down, in the client's terms, and you keep it where you can see it while you work. The written scope is the thing you consult when an ambiguous request arrives, and it lets you answer "which client does this belong to" before you start spending effort, not after.

When an out-of-scope request arrives, the intermediate move is not a blunt refusal and not silent absorption. It is a re-scope conversation, offered in the client's terms: here is what this would take, here is what it would push out of this phase, here is the choice you have. You present the trade as the client's decision, with real costs attached, and you let them choose. What you never do is quietly absorb the request and let it eat another client's hours, because that is the silent theft that turns a healthy portfolio into a slow emergency.

![A flow showing that an out-of-scope request leads to a re-scope conversation in the client's terms, never a blunt refusal or silent absorption](</diagrams/managing-a-small-portfolio-of-clients/2-rescope-conversation.png>)

Hermes gives you a concrete way to hold these boundaries, and it is the same isolation lesson you have used all course: one client per profile. A profile is its own Hermes home directory, holding its own config, memory, sessions, skills, and scheduled jobs, separate from every other agent on the machine. [^1] When you run one client per profile, the scope boundary stops being a mental reminder and becomes an architectural fact. Client A's skills are in client A's profile, client A's scheduled jobs live there, client A's memory accumulates there, and client B's delivery literally cannot load them by accident. The platform enforces the separation you would otherwise have to maintain by willpower, and that frees your discipline for the requests that cross boundaries on purpose.

## Expectations: hold what you actually are

A portfolio lives or dies on expectations, because expectations are the contract you are actually judged against, and they are set by your behavior long before any written agreement. The mistake is to assume each client still has your full attention and to let them believe it. The honest intermediate move is to set the expectation deliberately, in writing, up front, so no client discovers your divided attention as a surprise.

Name the expectations you actually control. Response time: what is a realistic window for a normal question, and what counts as urgent. Delivery cadence: when each client can expect to hear from you with real progress, not just a status ping. Availability in a crisis: when you are truly reachable and when you are not. Escalation: what a client does when they cannot reach you and the thing is on fire. Each of these is a number or a rule you can state plainly and then hold.

The discipline is that you set these expectations at the level you can actually sustain, not the level you wish you could. It is tempting to promise an hour response time to keep a client happy. The portfolio truth is that with several clients you cannot hold a universal hour, and the client who discovers the exception is the one who feels betrayed. Set the honest number, then beat it. An expectation set low and exceeded builds trust. An expectation set high and missed burns it, and the burn is worse than the modest promise would have been.

Hermes turns expectations into configuration rather than memory. The gateway is a single background process that connects to your messaging platforms and handles sessions, and you can give each client's profile its own bot and its own delivery targets. [^2] Client A's updates land in client A's channel, client B's in client B's, and neither sees the other's traffic. The scheduled jobs that run each client's delivery resolve their own model, provider, and delivery target at fire time from their own pin. [^3] So the expectation "client A gets their report every Monday at 7am" is not a promise you remember to keep. It is a configured job in client A's profile that fires whether or not you are thinking about client A that morning. You set the expectation once, the platform holds it, and you spend your attention on the parts that genuinely need a human.

## Time: allocation, not just hours

Single-client time is a countdown. Portfolio time is an allocation problem, and it fails when you treat it like a countdown, spending freely until some invisible budget runs out and then discovering you have overdrawn three clients at once. The intermediate frame is to treat your capacity as a finite asset and to decide, deliberately and repeatedly, how it is divided.

Start by measuring the real cost of switching, because it is the number that changes everything. Every time you move from client A's work to client B's work, you pay a re-entry cost: you lose the working state you had in A, you reload B's context, and you reorient. With several engagements, that cost is not a rounding error. It is a regular tax on every transition, and it means a week with many small switches delivers less than a week with a few large blocks even when the total hours are identical. The intermediate response is to consolidate: cluster each client's work into contiguous blocks rather than scattering it across the week, so you pay the switching cost a handful of times instead of a dozen.

![Two week layouts comparing scattered work with many context switches against consolidated contiguous blocks that pay the switching cost only a handful of times](</diagrams/managing-a-small-portfolio-of-clients/3-scattered-vs-consolidated.png>)

Then budget for slack, because a portfolio without slack is a portfolio that collapses on the first surprise, and surprises are guaranteed. If every client's committed hours sum to one hundred percent of your capacity, you have zero room for the thing that always happens, an urgent issue in one engagement, a request that was really a fire, a delivery that took longer than its estimate. The intermediate rule is to hold committed work at a utilization you can absorb against, so that when a client needs you suddenly you can move without abandoning someone else. The exact number depends on you, but the principle is fixed: reserve capacity you are willing to spend on the unplanned, and treat it as a cost of holding the portfolio, not as idle time to be sold.

Hermes turns some of this time into autonomous delivery so it does not consume your attention at all. Scheduled jobs run in fresh agent sessions, do the work they are configured to do, and deliver to their targets without you supervising the routine. [^3] The routine reconciliation, the weekly report, the monitoring that would otherwise eat your focus, can run itself in each client's profile, and you review the output instead of producing it. [^1] That is the portfolio superpower: the work that does not need a human runs unattended, and your finite attention goes to the work that does. The consultant who delivers the routine by hand can hold one client. The consultant who delegates the routine to configured automation holds several, because the routine stopped consuming their hours.

## The weekly portfolio review

All three enemies, scope, expectations, and time, need a cadence where you confront them, and the intermediate tool is the weekly portfolio review, a standing thirty minutes where you look at the whole set of engagements rather than the one in front of you. You ask the same questions every week, and you write the answers down.

On scope: for each client, did any request cross a boundary this week, and did I route it deliberately or absorb it? On expectations: for each client, did I meet every response time, delivery cadence, and crisis window I promised, and if not, why? On time: for each client, did I spend the allocation I budgeted, did context switching eat more than it should, and is there slack left for next week's surprises?

The value is not in the answers. It is in forcing the portfolio into view at a fixed interval, because it is the engagement in front of you that always demands attention, and it is the one you are not looking at that quietly starves. The review is how you catch a starving client before they notice they are starving and go find another consultant.

## Worked example: the reconciliation consultant holds two clients

Put the whole discipline on the course's running example. You have been delivering the weekly reconciliation agent, and since lesson 13 you have handed it over so completely that the client runs it themselves. You have capacity, and a second client, a distributor, signs on for a similar engagement. Suddenly you are a portfolio, and everything this lesson teaches is about surviving the transition.

First, scope. You write the distributor's scope explicitly, the deliverables and the definition of done and the explicit out-of-scope list. You create a dedicated profile for the distributor, its own Hermes home with its own memory, skills, and scheduled jobs, separate from the first client's profile. [^1] The two engagements are now architecturally isolated. When a request arrives, you know which client it belongs to before you start, and when something is out of scope for the distributor, you run the re-scope conversation in their terms instead of silently absorbing it and eating the first client's hours.

Second, expectations. Before you promise anything, you set the numbers you can actually hold: a response window for normal questions, a delivery cadence, a crisis escalation path. You configure the distributor's profile with its own delivery target so their updates land in their channel and nowhere else. [^2] You pin the distributor's weekly report to its own model, provider, and delivery target so it fires on schedule regardless of where your attention is that morning. [^3] The expectation is written, configured, and real, and you have not promised the distributor an hour response time you cannot deliver to two clients at once.

Third, time. You measure the switching cost and you consolidate, clustering each client's work into contiguous blocks instead of scattering it. You hold committed utilization below one hundred percent, reserving slack for the surprise that is coming. You let the routine reconciliation run itself in each profile and spend your attention on the exceptions, the incident, the change, the judgment call. [^3] The first client's system runs without you because lesson 13 made it self-sufficient. The second client's system runs without you because it is configured to. Your attention, finite and precious, goes where it matters, and you hold two clients without quietly sacrificing either one.

## The transferable skill: run the portfolio, not the engagements

Pull the whole discipline together and it reduces to one posture, the same whether your clients are logistics firms, banks, or internal teams. It has four steps, each a deliberate act repeated on a cadence.

**Name the boundaries.** Write each engagement's scope, definition of done, and explicit out-of-scope list, and defend the boundary when work tries to cross it without a decision.

**Set honest expectations.** State your response times, cadence, and crisis availability in writing, at the level you can actually hold, and let the written promise do the promising.

**Allocate time deliberately.** Measure the switching cost, consolidate each client's work into blocks, hold committed utilization below your ceiling, and reserve slack for the unplanned.

**Review the whole every week.** Force the portfolio into view on a fixed cadence and confront scope, expectations, and time for every client, especially the quiet one.

![A four-step cycle for running a portfolio: name the boundaries, set honest expectations, allocate time deliberately, and review the whole every week](</diagrams/managing-a-small-portfolio-of-clients/4-four-step-discipline.png>)

The consultant who holds these four steps is running a portfolio. The consultant who skips them is running a series of individual engagements and hoping they fit together, which they will not. And the reason this matters, the reason the course spent a whole lesson on it, is that a small portfolio well-run is the foundation of a practice. It is the proof that your delivery does not depend on your total attention, and it is the base you build the next stage of the craft on, translating everything you have learned so it does not depend on one tool.

## The pitfalls that sink a small portfolio

**Scope bleed.** Work crosses from one engagement to another without a decision, and the hours a stray request eats come out of a budget you promised elsewhere. Defend the boundary and run a re-scope conversation instead of absorbing silently.

**Promising attention you do not have.** You tell every client you are fully available, and then the divided reality surfaces as a broken promise. Set expectations at the level you can actually hold, in writing, and beat them.

**Scattered time.** You spread every client's work thinly across the week and pay the switching cost a dozen times. Consolidate into contiguous blocks so the tax is paid a handful of times instead.

**One hundred percent utilization.** Every committed hour sums to your full capacity, leaving zero slack for the guaranteed surprise, and the first incident collapses the portfolio. Reserve capacity you are willing to spend on the unplanned.

**Delivering routine by hand.** The weekly report and the reconciliation eat your attention when they could run configured and unattended. Let each client's scheduled work run itself and spend your attention on the exceptions.

**Never looking at the whole.** The engagement in front of you always wins, and the one you are not looking at quietly starves. Run the weekly portfolio review, every week, on purpose.

## Try it

Take the two engagements you have been carrying through the course, the reconciled first client and the distributor you just signed, and run them as a portfolio on paper.

1. Write the scope for the new engagement: the deliverables, the definition of done for each, and the explicit out-of-scope list. State the boundary in the client's terms.
2. For one ambiguous request that could plausibly belong to either client, write the re-scope conversation: what it would take, what it would push out of this phase, and the choice you would offer.
3. Write your honest expectations for both clients, response time, delivery cadence, crisis availability, and escalation, at a level you could actually hold with both of them at once.
4. Sketch a week: cluster each client's work into contiguous blocks, count the context switches you pay, and show where you have left slack for a surprise.
5. Name what each client's profile would run automatically, and what would still need your attention, in the Hermes isolation model. [^1]
6. Run a simulated weekly portfolio review: for each client, did scope cross a boundary, did every expectation hold, and was time spent where you budgeted it?

If you can produce all six, you are not running two engagements. You are running a small portfolio, and that portfolio is the base for the course's next and final stage.
