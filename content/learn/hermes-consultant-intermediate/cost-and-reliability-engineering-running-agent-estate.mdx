# Lesson 10: Cost and Reliability Engineering for a Running Agent Estate

Lesson 9 kept a running delivery correct at volume. This lesson asks what it costs to keep that delivery alive, and whether it stays alive. The two are the same question looked at from opposite ends. A system you cannot afford to run gets turned off. A system you can afford but cannot depend on gets turned off too, usually by an incident. The practitioner who operates a real engagement for a client manages both, because the levers are shared and the trade-offs are one ledger.

Here is the shift this lesson asks you to make. When you delivered the system, you were building. Now you are operating, and operating has different economics. A build is a one-time cost you estimate once. An estate is a recurring cost you model per run and per week, forever, plus a reliability obligation that has no end date. The client is not paying you to have built an agent. They are paying to run a small, always-on system that keeps working next month and next quarter at a price they can predict. That is the deliverable now, and it is why the whole framing changes from "does it work" to "what does it keep costing and what does it take to keep it standing."

## An estate is a fleet of runs, and its economics are recurring

The first principle is to stop thinking of the delivered agent as one thing and start thinking of it as a fleet. A working agent delivery is not a single program. It is a collection of scheduled jobs that fire on their own rhythms, the plumbing that delivers their output, the models and providers underneath them, and the state they read and write. Call that whole thing the estate, because that is what it behaves like: many independent moving parts, each with its own failure modes and its own per-run cost, sharing a small number of things that, if they break, break everything.

Once you think in runs, the cost model writes itself. Every recurring job has a unit cost per run, which is mostly the inference it spends, and a frequency, which is how often that unit cost repeats. Multiply them and you get the weekly burn of that job. Sum the jobs and you get the weekly burn of the estate. That number is the one you model before you promise the client anything, because it is the number that will come back to you when the invoice arrives or when the client asks why the system they approved is costing three times what the build did.

The failure to do this arithmetic is the most common cost surprise in delivered agent systems. Consultants estimate the build and underweight the run. A job that fires hourly spends its unit cost 168 times a week, and a fleet of ten such jobs is a real line item, not a rounding error. The discipline is to compute the weekly burn during the engagement, before handover, and to put it in front of the client as a number they can see and sanity-check. If the number is higher than the client's appetite, you find out during the project, not after it is running. That single sentence saves more engagements than almost any technical fix.

## Route the work a script can do off the model entirely

The cheapest reliable run is the one that never calls a model. This is the first and largest cost lever, and it is the one consultants reach for last because it is invisible from inside an agent workflow. A large share of what a fleet does is not judgment. It is mechanical: pull data, check a condition, transform a file, run a test, compare two values, report a status. Every one of those tasks, run through an agent, spends tokens on reasoning that adds nothing, because there is nothing to reason about.

Hermes names this distinction directly. A scheduled job can run in no-agent mode: a script on a schedule, its output delivered verbatim, with zero LLM involvement, no tokens, no model, no provider fallback needed. [^1] The script is deterministic, so it cannot drift in its own judgment, and it costs nothing per run. It is the right tool for the mechanical share of the estate, and moving that share off the model is the cheapest reliability improvement available, because a deterministic check is the one component that cannot quietly change its own mind.

The rule of thumb is the same one Lesson 9 gave you for scoring golden-set cases, because it is the same rule. The more mechanically checkable a job's work is, the more it should be a script. Watchdog jobs that confirm the estate is alive, monitoring jobs that pull a number and compare it to a threshold, delivery jobs that format and ship a report, these are scripts. Keep the model for the part that genuinely needs judgment, the part where output quality depends on reasoning over ambiguous material, and route everything else around it. In a well-shaped estate the majority of scheduled fires should never touch the inference layer. The fleet still does its job, it just stops paying per call for the calls that were never doing anything.

## Tier the model work, and cap the run before it blows up

What is left after the scripts are the jobs that genuinely need judgment, and they are not all the same difficulty. A recurring summary that consolidates known routine material is not as hard as a document that must reason about a new and ambiguous source. Running every judgment job on the most capable model at full reasoning effort is like using a senior consultant to file. The cost lever here is tiering: match the model and the reasoning budget to the difficulty of the job.

Hermes supports this at the level of the fleet. You can set a cron-wide default model that every unpinned job runs on, independent of your interactive model, so switching the model you chat with never silently moves the fleet. [^2] Then individual jobs can pin their own model and their own reasoning effort, so the heavy weekly analysis runs at high effort while the cheap recurring jobs run at minimal. [^3] The effect is that each run spends in proportion to the judgment it actually needs, and the estate's weekly burn settles into a shape you can defend to the client instead of a flat wall of premium tokens.

The companion discipline is capping the blowup, because the cost surprise is not the normal run. It is the run that goes sideways. A job that hits an ambiguous input and reasons its way into a long spiral spends many times its normal unit cost in a single fire, and if it happens every time the input is messy, it is a standing leak. Pinning a reasoning level is partly about that: it puts an upper bound on how far one run can wander. A fleet is only affordable if no single bad run can blow the week's budget, and the way to guarantee that is to make the normal and worst-case spend of each job a bounded, known number rather than an open-ended one.

## Fails closed, so a broken estate does not spend and does not lie

Now the two halves meet. The reliability posture that protects you from runaway spend is the same posture that keeps an unattended fleet honest, and it comes down to one principle: when something is wrong, an unattended job should skip and alert, never guess and spend. This is the difference between fails closed and fails open, and it is the correct default for anything that runs with nobody watching.

Consider the quiet failure that costs the most money. An estate's jobs are unpinned, following a global default. Someone, months in, switches the global model to a more capable and more expensive one, for their interactive work, not thinking about the fleet. The next morning every unpinned job fires on the paid model, and the fleet quietly spends many times its normal burn, because the change was never aimed at the estate and nobody was watching when it took effect. Hermes guards this exact case. When an unpinned job's global default changes, the job fails closed: it skips the run, makes no inference call, and alerts once, staying silent on later ticks until someone acts. [^2] The deliberate way to route cron spend is to pin the model and provider yourself, and the guard exists precisely so that an unattended job does not silently inherit a switch that was never meant for it. That is cost control and reliability control in the same mechanism.

The same principle governs a misconfigured job. Before a scheduled run, Hermes validates that the job can actually succeed: that the provider key resolves, that attached skills are ready, that delivery targets exist. [^4] If validation fails, the job reports a blocked configuration, alerts once, and makes no LLM call. A broken job never spends tokens on a run that was doomed anyway, and it does not retry itself into a bill. The estate's failures surface as a single clear signal instead of a quiet, expensive, repeating failure.

## Engineer so no single part can take the estate down

Reliability engineering for a fleet is mostly single-point-of-failure analysis. Walk the estate and ask what one thing, if it broke, stops everything. For an agent system the answer is almost always a provider or a model, because the fleet routes its judgment through a small number of inference paths. When that provider has an outage or rate-limits the key, every judgment job in the estate fails at once, and the whole delivery looks dead even though nothing you wrote is wrong.

The fix is redundancy at the layer where the single point actually lives, which is the inference path, and it is cheap compared to the failure. Hermes cron jobs inherit your configured fallback providers and credential rotation. If the primary provider is rate-limited or errors, the job falls back to an alternate provider, and it can rotate to the next credential in the pool for the same provider. [^5] The practical shape is a fallback chain: the same model reachable through a second, independent pipe, so a provider outage degrades to a reroute instead of a dead run, and below that a different model so a model-level failure has somewhere to go too. You do not need to run two of everything. You need the one thing that is truly shared, the inference path, to not be a single point.

The discipline is deciding, per job, how much redundancy the job earns. This is where cost and reliability are literally the same decision. A high-value, low-frequency job, the weekly report the client's operations team depends on, earns a full fallback chain, because the cost of a missed run is high and the redundancy is paid rarely. A cheap, high-frequency routine job, the one that fires hourly and costs almost nothing, may not earn a second provider, because the cost of an occasional missed fire is low and paying for a redundant path 168 times a week is not worth protecting a trivial run. The rule is to spend redundancy only where failure is expensive, and to let the cheap, frequent jobs fail into an alert rather than into a second always-hot pipe. Redundancy is insurance, and insurance is priced against the cost of the loss, not applied uniformly.

## A running estate needs a health signal the client can read

The last reliability deliverable is observability, and it is more than a dashboard of logs. A client does not want to read logs. They want to know, without paging through anything, whether the estate is working, and they want to be told the moment it is not, not discover it later. So the estate needs a health contract with three parts: what tells you it is working, what tells you it broke, and who gets told how.

The working signal is often a script. A watchdog job that confirms the estate's other jobs fired and delivered on schedule is mechanical, so it should be a no-agent job, and it is exactly the kind of thing that should run free and deterministic. [^1] The broke signal is the estate's own failure reporting: a job that errors alerts, a delivery that cannot be confirmed is flagged rather than logged as delivered, a blocked configuration surfaces once instead of vanishing. [^4] The discipline is that a broken job must not fail silently, because a fleet that dies quietly is a fleet the client discovers is down by noticing the output stopped. That is the failure mode that destroys trust, and it is entirely preventable.

The third part, who gets told, is the part consultants undersell. Define the alert path during the engagement and test it before handover. The client's operations person should get the delivery and the failure notices, and you should know, for the first weeks at least, that you will also be told when something breaks, so the client is never the one who tells you the estate stopped. A reliable estate is one where the operator hears about a problem from the system before the client hears about it from their own customers.

## Worked example: a year of operations on a fixed budget

Bring it back to the release-notes and runbook engine you handed over in Lesson 9, because that is where cost and reliability bite. The system drafts documents for the client's product suite every week, and the client is happy with quality. Now they want it to run for a year on an operations budget they can predict, and they need it to not go down, because those documents reach their customers and their own operations team. The build is done. The estate is the job.

Start with the cost model, because it sets everything. You open the fleet and list what actually fires. There is the weekly document run, which needs judgment and rightly runs a capable model at high reasoning effort. There are also the things that grew around it: a daily status check that pulls a build number and compares it to a threshold, a formatting pass, a delivery job that ships the finished documents. Left alone, all of them would run as agent jobs, paying inference on work that is mechanical. You route the mechanical ones to scripts first, and the weekly burn drops immediately, because the share of the fleet that needed judgment was a fraction of what was spending. [^1]

Then you tier the judgment. The weekly document run keeps the capable model and the high reasoning effort, because a softer release note is the thing that reaches a customer and does damage. A weekly consolidation that summarizes routine changes, lower-stakes, runs cheaper at minimal effort. [^3] Each run now spends in proportion to what it protects, and the estate's cost settles into a shape you can put on one line for the client: this much for the customer-facing judgment, this much for the routine consolidation, nothing for the mechanics, all of it predictable.

Now reliability, and the near-miss that proves the design. Three months in, the primary provider has an outage during a document run. The engine's judgment jobs are pinned to a fallback chain, the same model reachable through a second provider and a second credential, so the run that would have missed simply reroutes and delivers on time. [^5] The client never notices, which is the entire point of the redundancy: they did not pay for it on every run, only for the runs that needed to be bulletproof, and this was one. You walk the client through what happened in the weekly call, and it is the moment they stop thinking of the system as fragile. A failure happened and nothing failed, because you spent the reliability budget where the failure was expensive.

The watchdogs run alongside. A no-agent job confirms the fleet fired and delivered each week, free and deterministic, and the day the document run misses, the watchdog and the estate's own error alert both fire. [^1] You hear about it from the system within the hour, fix the cause, and by the time the client checks, the problem is already diagnosed and the rerun is queued. The client does not discover the estate was down. They learn it was down after it was already back up, which is the only way a client should ever find out.

Over the year, two things hold. The weekly burn stays inside the number you put on the line, because you capped the blowups and routed the mechanics off the model. And the estate never has a single point of failure where it matters, because you engineered the inference path to survive a provider dying. Cost contained, reliability held, and both from the same set of decisions. That is the deliverable.

## The pitfalls that sink a running estate

**Pricing the build and ignoring the run.** The weekly burn of a fleet is the number that comes back to bite. Model it during the engagement and show it to the client before handover, not after the first invoice.

**Running judgment work as scripts, or mechanics as agents.** Both directions are wrong. A job that needs reasoning but got a script produces bad output cheaply. A mechanical job that runs as an agent spends tokens on reasoning that adds nothing. Match the tool to the actual judgment the task needs.

**One provider, one model, one point of failure.** When the fleet routes all its judgment through a single inference path, a provider outage takes the whole estate down at once. Add a fallback chain where failure is expensive, and let cheap routine jobs fail into an alert instead.

**A broken job that fails silently.** An unattended job that stops and tells nobody is an estate the client discovers is dead by noticing the output stopped. Make failures alert, and make the alerts reach someone before the client notices.

**Unbounded runs.** A job with no reasoning ceiling can spend many times its normal cost on a single messy input. Cap the worst case so no one run can blow the week's budget.

**An unpinned fleet.** Jobs that follow a changing global default can silently inherit a paid model or provider switch that was never meant for them. Pin the fleet's model and provider deliberately, so an unrelated change cannot move your spend.

## Try it

Take the delivery you hardened in Lesson 9, or a live estate you actually operate, and run the cost and reliability pass over it.

1. List the fleet and its firing pattern. For every recurring job, write down what it does, how often it fires, and whether that work needs judgment or is mechanical. Mark the mechanical ones.
2. Compute the weekly burn. For each judgment job, estimate its per-run inference cost and multiply by frequency. Sum the estate. Write the number down, and ask whether it matches what you or the client expected to pay.
3. Route the mechanics off the model. For every job you marked mechanical, write what script would replace it, and confirm the replacement is deterministic. Move them to no-agent mode so they run free and cannot drift.
4. Tier the judgment. Split the judgment jobs by difficulty and stakes. Assign the appropriate model and reasoning effort to each, and make sure the highest-stakes work keeps the strongest guarantee while routine work runs cheap. [^3]
5. Check for single points of failure. List what one thing, if it broke, would stop the whole estate. For each, decide whether the cost of a missed run justifies a fallback, and add redundancy only where failure is expensive.
6. Define the health contract. Write what signal proves the estate is working, what alert fires when a job fails, and who receives it. Test the alert path before you rely on it.

If you can hold all six, your estate is not just working and affordable. It is predictable in cost and defensible in reliability, which is what a client who is paying to run a system for a year actually bought.

## What's next

You now know how to keep a running estate correct, affordable, and standing: a golden set that holds the quality bar, tiered spend with mechanical work routed off the model, fails-closed behavior that protects both budget and honesty, and redundancy placed only where failure is expensive. The next lesson turns to the moment all of that design still gets tested: the incident. It teaches incident discipline for troubleshooting a broken agent under client pressure, how you diagnose a failed run methodically when the client is waiting and the clock is real, how you separate the agent's failure from the platform's and from your own, and how you recover and explain it without losing trust. Where this lesson kept the estate alive, the next one walks you through the discipline for when, despite everything, it breaks.
