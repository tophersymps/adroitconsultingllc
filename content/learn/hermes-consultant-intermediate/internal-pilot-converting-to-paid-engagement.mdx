# Lesson 8: Running an Internal Pilot That Converts Into a Paid Engagement

Every engagement in this course so far started with a client who had already decided to pay. That is the comfortable world, and it is not the world where most consulting careers are made. The harder sale, and the one that separates the practitioner who lands work from the one who keeps writing free proposals, is the sale that happens before the client has decided you are worth paying for. The client has a problem, they suspect an agent could help, and they are not sure enough to sign. The tool for that moment is the internal pilot: a real slice of the prospect's problem, delivered as a bounded proof, designed from the first hour to convert into a paid engagement.

The word internal is doing real work here, and it is worth being precise about it. An internal pilot is not a demo and it is not a free consulting engagement. A demo shows capability: here is what the tool can do, in a clean room, on data you chose. A free engagement is a full delivery with the price removed, which is how consultants quietly give away their best work. The internal pilot sits between them. It is a real slice of the prospect's actual problem, run on real data, measured against a real baseline, and bounded so tightly that it cannot become a free engagement by accident. The pilot is the discovery phase done for free, but with a fence around it, and the fence is what makes it convert.

This lesson teaches the craft of that fence. You will learn why the pilot must be a hypothesis test and not a demo, how to scope a slice that is representative without being the whole engagement, how to measure the before and after so the proof is undeniable, how to set the conversion gate before the pilot starts, and how to price the paid engagement from the evidence the pilot produced. Hermes Agent stays the concrete working example, and the skill transfers to any tool you ever run.

## The pilot is a hypothesis test, not a demo

Here is the mistake that turns most pilots into free work. The consultant treats the pilot as a chance to show off what the tool can do, and the client treats it as a chance to watch. The pilot runs, the client is impressed, and then nothing happens, because being impressed is not the same as deciding to pay. The pilot produced a feeling, and a feeling does not survive the budget meeting.

The pilot that converts is built on a different premise. It is a hypothesis test. Before the pilot starts, you write down the specific claim you are testing, in a form that can be measured and falsified. The claim is not "an agent can help with this." That is a demo claim, and it is true of almost anything. The claim is specific: "an agent can produce this deliverable at this accuracy, in this time, at this cost, and the result is worth more than the human time it replaces." That is a testable claim, and the pilot is the experiment that either confirms it or does not.

The discipline of writing the hypothesis first is what separates the pilot that converts from the pilot that impresses. A hypothesis forces you to name the value before you build anything, which means you build only the slice that can prove it. It forces you to name the measurement before you run, which means you cannot fudge the result afterward. And it forces you to name the failure condition, which means you and the client both know what "the pilot did not work" looks like before it happens. A pilot with a written hypothesis is a consulting instrument. A pilot without one is a show.

In Hermes terms, the hypothesis is the prompt you would hand an agent, sharpened until it is testable. The agent toolset runs on a prompt plus a set of tools, and the quality of the output is bounded by how precisely the task is specified. [^1] A vague prompt produces a vague result, and a vague result proves nothing. The pilot's hypothesis is the precise prompt: the exact deliverable, the exact inputs, the exact acceptance bar. When you can write that, you can test it.

## Scope a representative slice, not the whole engagement

The second decision that makes or breaks a pilot is scope, and the instinct to fight is the instinct to do too much. The consultant wants to prove the whole thing works, so they scope the whole thing, and the pilot becomes a free engagement. The client wants to see the real problem solved, so they push for the whole thing too. Both instincts point the same wrong direction. The pilot must be a slice, and the skill is choosing the slice that proves the hypothesis without becoming the delivery.

The rule is that the slice must be representative, not comprehensive. It has to exercise the same data, the same tools, the same failure modes, and the same quality bar as the real engagement, but on a bounded portion of it. You pick the slice that is hardest to fake, the one where the agent either delivers or it does not. If the pilot is a weekly report, the slice is one real week on real data, not a synthetic sample. If the pilot is a reconciliation, the slice is one real account or one real month, not the whole ledger. The slice is small enough to deliver fast and cheap, and real enough that the result means something.

The boundary is the fence. Before the pilot starts, you write down exactly what is in the slice and exactly what is not. The in list is the proof. The out list is the paid engagement. This is the sentence that converts: "the pilot proves the agent can do this one thing at this quality; the paid engagement is everything else." When the pilot succeeds, the out list becomes the scope of the signed engagement, and the client is already sold on the mechanism because they watched it work on their own data.

The representative slice also protects you from the pilot that succeeds for the wrong reason. If you pilot on clean synthetic data, the result proves nothing about the real problem, and the client knows it. If you pilot on the easy part of the problem, the result flatters the tool and the client quietly discounts it. The slice has to be the part where the agent is genuinely at risk, because that is the part whose success the client will believe.

## Measure the before and the after

A pilot converts on evidence, and evidence is a before and an after. The before is the baseline: how the client does this work today, in time, in cost, in error rate, in people. The after is the pilot result: how the agent does the same work, in the same units. Without the before, the after is a number floating in space. With both, you have a delta, and the delta is the value you are selling.

The baseline has to be measured, not estimated, and it has to be measured before the pilot runs. You ask the client how long the current process takes, and you watch it happen, and you write down the real number. You count the errors in the current output, and you write down the real rate. The baseline is the client's own reality, and it is the thing they cannot argue with because they lived it. The pilot's job is to beat that baseline, and the pilot's result is only meaningful against it.

The after has to be measured in the same units, and this is where the agent's own tooling earns its keep. A scheduled agent run produces a record: what it did, what it returned, how long it took, what it cost. [^2] The pilot captures that record for every run, so the after is not a claim but a log. The accuracy of the output is checked against the baseline's own standard, by a human reviewer, not by the agent grading itself. The time and cost are read from the run record, not estimated. When the after is a log and the before is a measurement, the delta is a fact.

The discipline of measuring in the same units is what makes the conversion honest. If the baseline is hours of human time and the after is hours of agent time, the delta is hours saved, and hours saved is a number a finance team can price. If the baseline is error rate and the after is error rate, the delta is quality, and quality is a number an operations team can defend. You choose the unit that the client's own decision makers care about, and you measure both sides in it. The pilot that converts is the one whose delta is in the client's own currency.

## Set the conversion gate before the pilot starts

The most important decision in a pilot is made before the pilot runs, and it is the decision about what happens when the pilot succeeds. This is the conversion gate, and it is the difference between a pilot that becomes a paid engagement and a pilot that becomes a permanent free dependency.

The gate is a pre-agreed trigger. You and the client agree, before the pilot starts, that if the pilot meets its hypothesis, the pilot converts into a paid engagement on defined terms. The terms are the scope of the paid work, the price, and the timeline, all sketched before the pilot proves anything. This is not a commitment to sign a blank check. It is a commitment to have a real conversation, on a real scope, at a real price, if the pilot delivers. The gate turns the pilot from a favor into a transaction with a defined next step.

The reason the gate has to be set before the pilot is that after the pilot, the power shifts. If the pilot succeeds and there is no gate, the client has the proof and no obligation, and the consultant has done the work and no leverage. The client can take the result, thank the consultant, and run it themselves, or shop it to a cheaper firm, or ask for "just one more week" of free work. The gate, agreed in advance, is the consultant's protection against the success that gets stolen. It is the fence that makes the pilot convert instead of being harvested.

The gate also protects the client, and it is worth saying so. A client who agrees to a conversion gate is not being trapped. They are agreeing that if the pilot proves the value, they will pay for the value, which is a fair deal. The gate removes the ambiguity that makes clients nervous about pilots, the fear that they are being drawn into an open-ended commitment. A defined gate, with a defined scope and price, is easier to say yes to than a vague "let's see how it goes." The gate is not pressure. It is clarity.

## Price the paid engagement from the pilot's evidence

When the pilot converts, you do not price the engagement from a guess. You price it from the evidence the pilot produced, and this is where the estimation discipline from Lesson 6 pays off. The pilot gave you real numbers: the recurring cost of a run, the human time it replaces, the error rate it holds, the setup it took. Those numbers are the raw material of a defensible price.

The price has two layers, and the pilot measures both. The build layer is the one-time work to take the pilot slice and turn it into the full engagement: the additional scope from the out list, the hardening, the integration, the handover. The recurring layer is the ongoing value the agent produces every week: the hours saved, the errors avoided, the reports generated. The pilot proves the recurring layer, because it ran the recurring thing. The build layer is estimated from the out list, using the critical-path discipline from Lesson 6. [^3]

The pricing insight is that the client is not paying for the build. They are paying for the recurring value, and the pilot has already shown them that value in their own units. So the price is anchored to the recurring delta, not to the cost of the build. If the pilot showed the agent saves twelve hours a week, the price is a fraction of what twelve hours a week is worth to the client, and the build is priced as the cost of getting there. The pilot made the value concrete, and the price follows the value.

The recurring cost also has to be honest, and the pilot is where you learn it. An agent run has a real cost: the model, the provider, the tokens, the scheduled runs. [^2] The pilot's run record tells you the actual per-run cost, and you price the recurring layer to cover it with margin. A consultant who prices the recurring layer without knowing the run cost is pricing a guess, and the guess will come back as a loss when the engagement runs at volume. The pilot is the cheapest place to learn the real unit economics, because it is the one place you run the thing before you have to price it.

## Worked example: the weekly reconciliation pilot

Take a concrete case and run the whole discipline through it. A prospect runs a weekly reconciliation between their sales system and their accounting system. It takes a senior analyst about six hours every Monday, and it has a measurable error rate that the finance team has been complaining about for a year. The prospect is interested in an agent that could do it, but they are not sure enough to sign a multi-week engagement. This is the moment for an internal pilot.

First, the hypothesis. The consultant writes it down before anything else: "an agent can take the prior week's sales and accounting exports, reconcile them, and produce a variance report that matches the analyst's output on accuracy, in under an hour, at a per-run cost under the analyst's hourly rate." That is testable. It names the deliverable, the accuracy bar, the time, and the cost. It is a demo claim no longer. It is a hypothesis.

Second, the scope. The slice is one real week: the prior week's actual exports, the actual reconciliation rules, the actual variance format. The out list is everything else: the other weeks, the edge cases, the automation of the analyst's other duties, the integration into their ticketing system. The consultant writes the in list and the out list before the pilot runs, and the out list is the future engagement.

Third, the baseline. The consultant watches the analyst do the reconciliation, times it, and counts the errors in the last quarter's reports. The baseline is six hours a week and a documented error rate. It is measured, not estimated, and the analyst and the finance team both agree it is real.

Fourth, the pilot. The consultant builds the agent to do the one week, runs it on the real data, and captures the run record: the time, the cost, the output. A human reviewer, not the agent, checks the output against the baseline's standard. The after is a log, not a claim.

Fifth, the gate. Before the pilot, the consultant and the prospect agreed: if the pilot meets the hypothesis, they will scope and price the full engagement on the out list. The gate is written down. The pilot is a transaction with a defined next step, not a favor.

Sixth, the conversion. The pilot meets the hypothesis: the agent reconciles the week in forty minutes at a per-run cost well under the analyst's hourly rate, and the reviewer confirms the accuracy. The consultant brings the before, the after, and the delta to the prospect. The delta is in the client's own currency: hours saved, errors avoided, cost per run. The gate triggers, and the consultant prices the full engagement from the evidence: the recurring layer anchored to the weekly value, the build layer estimated from the out list. The prospect signs, not because they were impressed, but because they watched the value on their own data and the price follows the value they saw.

## The pitfalls that kill a pilot's conversion

A demo instead of a hypothesis. The pilot shows what the tool can do and produces a feeling. Feelings do not survive the budget meeting. Write the testable claim first, and build only the slice that can prove it.

The whole engagement for free. The pilot scopes everything, because the consultant wants to prove it all and the client wants to see it all. The pilot becomes a free delivery. Scope a representative slice, and write the out list that becomes the paid work.

A baseline that is estimated, not measured. The after is a number and the before is a guess, so the delta is meaningless. Measure the baseline before the pilot runs, in the client's own units, and watch it happen.

An after that is a claim, not a log. The agent's output is graded by the agent, or the time and cost are estimated. Capture the run record for every run, and have a human reviewer check the output against the baseline's standard.

No conversion gate. The pilot succeeds and there is no pre-agreed next step, so the client takes the proof and the consultant has no leverage. Set the gate, the scope, the price, and the timeline before the pilot runs.

Pricing a guess. The recurring layer is priced without knowing the real run cost, and the guess becomes a loss at volume. Learn the unit economics in the pilot, where it is cheapest, and anchor the price to the recurring value the pilot proved.

## Try it

Take a prospect you are currently courting, or a problem you know a client has, and design an internal pilot that would convert.

1. Write the hypothesis as a testable claim: the deliverable, the accuracy bar, the time, and the cost. Make it falsifiable, so you and the client both know what failure looks like.
2. Scope the representative slice. Write the in list, the one real portion that proves the mechanism, and the out list, everything that becomes the paid engagement.
3. Define the baseline. Name the unit the client's decision makers care about, and write how you will measure the before in that unit, before the pilot runs.
4. Design the measurement of the after. Write how you will capture the run record, and who the human reviewer is who checks the output against the baseline's standard.
5. Write the conversion gate. State the pre-agreed trigger, the scope of the paid work, the price anchor, and the timeline, all before the pilot proves anything.
6. Price the engagement from the evidence. Anchor the recurring layer to the value the pilot will prove, and estimate the build layer from the out list using the critical-path discipline from Lesson 6.

If you can hold all six, you are no longer writing free proposals. You are running a consulting instrument that turns a prospect's uncertainty into a signed engagement, and the proof is the value the client watched on their own data.
