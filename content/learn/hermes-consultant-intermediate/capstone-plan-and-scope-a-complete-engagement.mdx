# Lesson 25: Capstone: Plan and Scope a Complete Intermediate Engagement

Every technique lesson in this course taught you a move in isolation: how to capture requirements, how to price a fixed fee, how to draw a scope boundary, how to schedule a team. A capstone un-teaches the isolation. It hands you one engagement and makes you run the moves together, in order, against a fixed budget and a fixed calendar, where each decision constrains the next and an early assumption can quietly invalidate a downstream estimate.

This is the difference between Level 1 and Level 2 made concrete. Level 1 taught you the pieces. Intermediate taught you the judgment for each piece on a real engagement. The capstone is where you prove the judgment composes, because a competent scoper who is off by a factor of two on one input produces a plan that is not wrong in one place, it is wrong everywhere downstream.

The deliverable is not a system. The deliverable is three artifacts that fit together: a scope statement, a plan, and a defensible estimate. You will work a real engagement through all three here, then be handed a clean canvas to produce them yourself.

## What "plan and scope" means at intermediate depth

Scoping is the act of deciding what is in, what is out, and what the acceptance bar is, on the record, before work starts. Planning is the act of turning that scope into a sequence of work with dates, gates, dependencies, and buffers. Estimation is the act of putting a defensible number on the plan. The three are not independent. A scope that cannot be tested produces a plan nobody can gate. A plan without buffers produces an estimate that is a lie. An estimate that does not name its assumptions produces a scope that comes apart the first time one of those assumptions is wrong.

At the intermediate level, the test of your work is that each artifact makes the other two better. A scope statement that forces you to test the client's data in week zero makes the estimate honest and the plan safer. A plan with an explicit buffer makes the estimate defensible. An estimate that names its three biggest risks makes the scope statement self-aware. If any artifact could be deleted without hurting the other two, you have not composed them, you have stacked them.

The scope, plan, and estimate are also your defense in writing. When scope creeps, the scope statement is what you point at. When a week slips, the plan and its gates are where you identify it early enough to act. When a client challenges the fee, the estimate and its assumptions are your evidence. None of that works if the three were written in isolation.

## The capstone engagement

Linehaul Logistics, a 240-person regional freight broker, engages you for a fixed fee. The stated problem: their quoting team of five takes up to a day to turn a request for a lane and a rate into a quote, because the request arrives as free text, the rate table is maintained in a spreadsheet that is updated by a single director, and the quoting team re-enters everything. The sponsor is the COO, who wants turn-around under two hours. You have eight weeks and a budget that you set, because in this model you own pricing. The contact is the operations manager. There is an existing customer portal on the client's network, a rate spreadsheet that is the actual source of truth, and an IT director who has been burned by a failed integration a year ago and wants to see your security posture before anything touches the network.

This is an intermediate engagement because of what it is not. It is not a greenfield build, so you inherit a stack you cannot redesign. It is not a two-week pilot, so you must live with the consequences of your scoping for two months. It is not a single stakeholder, so you must satisfy the COO who owns the outcome, the ops manager who lives with the daily reality, and the IT director who holds every access gate. And the true difficulty is hiding inside the rate spreadsheet, which you will discover in week zero if you scope for it, or in week four if you do not.

## Step one: the discovery that prices the engagement

You cannot estimate until you have tested the two things that decide the estimate: the client's data and the client's willingness to supply it. Both get tested in week zero, because both are more expensive to learn late.

Your first move mirrors the one that separates a competent intermediate from a strong beginner: you ask for a sample of the real work before you quote. Sixty real quote requests with the quoting team's final answer for each, plus the rate spreadsheet. That one request does three jobs at once. It gives you a golden set you can grade against from day one. It surfaces the data quality problem early. And it tells you whether the client can actually supply the material a fixed-fee engagement depends on.

The sample arrives in two days. That speed is a good market signal, and what you find inside changes the shape of everything. The sixty requests are free text, not forms. Nine of them are ambiguous in a way a junior agent would guess at wrongly, which is exactly the failure you design against. The rate spreadsheet is the truth, but it is a spreadsheet with 11,000 rows, duplicate lanes with different rates under slight name variations, and three special customer contracts that override the base rate. There is no API to the portal, only a nightly extract, and the extract's schema is undocumented.

None of this makes you quote higher by reflex. It tells you what to build and what to exclude. The extract's undocumented schema is an input risk, so you price it as uncertain and you put a discovery spike in the plan for it. The nine ambiguous requests tell you the system needs a clarify step, not a guess step, so a request that is ambiguous is flagged to a human rather than quoted automatically. The duplicate lanes tell you the rate matching needs a lookup strategy that is correct on the clear rows and loud on the ambiguous ones.

The discovery output is a scoping decision, not a paragraph of findings. By the end of week zero you should be able to write one sentence that states what you will build, the boundary of what you will not, and the acceptance bar that a stranger could test.

## Step two: the scope statement

The scope statement that goes into the engagement is short and brutal. It has three parts: the deliverable, the boundary, and the acceptance bar. Each is a testable claim, not an aspiration.

The deliverable: an agent that reads each incoming quote request, extracts the lane and the load characteristics, looks up the matching rate from the source spreadsheet rules, flags anything ambiguous or unmatched to a review queue, and drafts a quote into a queue where an operator approves, edits, or rejects it. The agent does not send anything, and it does not set a rate that a rule did not produce.

The boundary, written as exclusions: the system will not touch the customer portal or send email; it works from a defined input mailbox and writes to a defined output queue; rate changes stay owned by the client's rate director and the system reads the current spreadsheet each run rather than storing a copy it can go stale on; and no network-adjacent deployment or new infrastructure will be built. The most important exclusion is the one you are most tempted to absorb: cleaning up the duplicate lanes. The director has been maintaining that spreadsheet by hand for years, and the duplicates are a data governance problem that predates you and outlives your project. Name it with evidence, refuse to own it in this engagement, and price around it. The system must be correct on clear rows and loud on ambiguous ones, which is a matching behavior you control, not a data cleanup you inherit.

The acceptance bar, in one testable sentence: a request passes when every lane in the source message appears in the draft either as a matched rate or as a flagged exception, no rate reaches a draft that a rule did not produce, an ambiguous request is flagged rather than guessed, and nothing leaves the system without an operator's approval. A stranger with no context and the test set can grade every one of those clauses. If a clause needs judgment to evaluate, it does not belong in an acceptance bar, it belongs in a risk note.

![The scope statement has three testable parts: the deliverable describes what the agent does, the boundary is written as exclusions including the duplicate-lane cleanup you are most tempted to absorb, and the acceptance bar is one sentence a stranger could grade](</diagrams/capstone-plan-and-scope-a-complete-engagement/4-scope-statement-three-parts.png>)

This is the artifact that makes the rest possible. Every gate in the plan traces to a clause here. Every change request maps to a clause this protects. The estimate exists to deliver exactly this, and nothing more. When the director, a week into build, suggests you also dedupe the rate table since you are in it anyway, the scope statement is not a bureaucratic obstacle, it is the sentence that saved the schedule.

## Step three: the plan that matches the scope

Now you turn the scope into a calendar. The scope gave you the work; the plan gives it order, dates, dependencies, and buffers. The single most important planning habit at intermediate depth is to put buffers in the plan where uncertainty actually lives, not as a flat cushion at the end.

A plan with one big buffer at the end is a plan that has moved all its risk to the final week, where it cannot be recovered from. A plan with small, positioned buffers absorbs surprises where they occur. For Linehaul, the buffers belong in three places: after the extract schema spike, because the undocumented schema is a genuine unknown; after the first full golden-set evaluation, because that is the first moment the matching behavior can be measured; and before handover, because training and dry-run always take longer than the optimistic version.

The provisional week-by-week shape, with the five lifecycle phases mapped onto the eight-week calendar:

- Week zero, discover: half a day watching the quoting team work, not interviewing managers, because people describe the process they think they follow and watching reveals the one they actually run. The sixty-request sample arrives, is studied, the duplicate-lane and ambiguity findings land, and the data-risk test determines scope. Exit test: the scope statement above is written and agreed.
- Week one, discover and design: the extract schema spike runs against the real nightly extract, exposing the undocumented schema. You map the rate lookup rules from the spreadsheet into a decision spec the implementer can build against. The evaluate design and the golden set are finalized. First buffer holds here.
- Weeks two and three, design and implement: the extraction, matching, and review-queue logic are built against the golden set. This is where the intermediate reality shows up: the build is not the hard part, the matching behavior on ambiguous and duplicate rows is.
- Week four, implement: full golden-set evaluation, graded by a script, not by vibes. The system is measured against the acceptance bar clauses, and the failure modes that produce plausible output instead of stopping are redesigned to fail loudly. Second buffer holds here.
- Weeks five and six, implement and handover: the review queue is wired to the operator workflow, the security review with the IT director runs against the deployment's real config, and the runbook is written while you build, not on the last day.
- Week seven, handover: the dry run with the client's own operators driving, credentials handed to their owner, support documented with an explicit end date. Third buffer holds here.
- Week eight, support and close: measured outcomes are reported against a definition that predates the change, exclusions are honored, and the engagement closes with the client's staff owning the system.

![The eight-week plan maps the five lifecycle phases onto the calendar, with the three buffers positioned where uncertainty lives: after the extract schema spike in week one, after the first golden-set evaluation in week four, and before handover in week seven](</diagrams/capstone-plan-and-scope-a-complete-engagement/2-eight-week-plan-buffers.png>)

The plan is defensible only because each gate is a clause from the scope statement re-expressed as an event. The evaluate gate in week four exists because the acceptance bar has testable clauses. The extraction spike in week one exists because the extract schema is an exclusion-adjacent risk you priced for. If a plan step has no corresponding scope clause, it is either gold-plating or hidden scope, and both are how engagements die.

## Step four: the estimate that names its assumptions

The estimate is where most intermediate scope work lives or dies, and it is not a point, it is a range with named assumptions. You set a fixed fee, so the range belongs to you, which is exactly why the assumptions have to be written down.

Pricing this engagement means deciding what the fixed fee buys you and what it does not. The fixed fee includes discovery, design, the extract spike, the build, the golden-set evaluation, the security review, the dry run, and a defined window of support. It prices around three named risks rather than pretending they are zero: the extract schema may reveal more integration surface than expected, the ambiguity rate on real incoming volume may exceed the golden set's nine-out-of-sixty, and the rate matching may need a boundary decision on duplicate lanes that costs extra build.

The estimate's job is to make these risks visible, not to promise they cannot happen. So you hold a contingency, sized to the two risks that are genuinely uncertain rather than applied flat, and you name in the fee what a second clarification round or a schema that reorganizes more than the spike showed would cost as a change order. Writing that down beforehand is not pessimism, it is what makes the fixed fee fixed rather than a negotiation that reopens in week four.

![The estimate prices around three named risks: the extract schema may reveal more integration surface, the ambiguity rate may exceed the golden set's nine-out-of-sixty, and rate matching may need a boundary decision on duplicate lanes, with contingency sized to the two genuinely uncertain risks and the rest named as change orders](</diagrams/capstone-plan-and-scope-a-complete-engagement/3-estimate-named-risks.png>)

The scope, plan, and estimate are one artifact the moment you stop treating them as three. The estimate is only as good as the plan it prices, the plan is only as good as the scope it schedules, and the scope is only as good as the estimate it survives. Change any one and the other two have to move with it, which is exactly the reasoning you apply when a change request arrives.

## What makes this a complete engagement

Linehaul is complete not because it runs through all five phases, but because every phase decision traces to a scope clause, every gate has a date and an owner, and every risk has a price and a home. The COO's two-hour goal is honored as an outcome measured on a definition that predates the change. The ops manager's daily reality is honored by the review queue that keeps a human in the loop. The IT director's burned trust is honored by a security review that happens before the network is touched, against the deployment's real config.

Hold the pieces together, and the three artifacts become a single contract: scope says what is in and out, plan says when each piece lands and where the buffers are, estimate says what it costs and what it does not cover. That is the complete intermediate engagement. And it is the last time this course walks one through for you. The canvas below is yours.

## Try it: run your own capstone

Choose an engagement you have run, or one running now, and produce the three artifacts in writing, in this order. Do not reuse this worked example; pick a real or close-to-real one so the reasoning is forced.

1. Write the scope statement in three parts: the deliverable as one testable sentence, the exclusions as the list you are most tempted to absorb, and the acceptance bar as clauses a stranger could grade. If any clause requires judgment to evaluate, rewrite it until it does not.
2. Write the plan as week-by-week phases with a gate and an owner per phase. Name the three places you carry buffer and explain why each buffer lives where it does instead of at the end.
3. Write the estimate as a fee with a named contingency and three written assumptions. For each assumption, state what would happen and what it would cost as a change order if it proved wrong.
4. Check the three for composition: can you delete one artifact without weakening the other two? If yes, fix whichever artifact the deletion did not depend on. A scope that adds no gate to the plan, or a plan that prices nothing new, is not a capstone, it is three documents.

Then grade it like the IT director would: does the plan prove the system touches the network only after a security review, and does the estimate say exactly what the fixed fee does not cover? Those two sentences are where scope work either holds or unravels on a real engagement.
