# Lesson 17: Working with IT, Compliance, and Legal on Agent Rollouts

You can build a flawless agent and watch the engagement die in an inbox. The agent is scoped, built, tested, and secure. Then the rollout stalls: IT has not provisioned the environment, or the data that the agent needs sits behind an approval that takes three weeks, or legal has not agreed to the model provider's terms, or procurement cannot process the invoice because the statement of work never said what you are actually delivering. None of those failures is a technical problem, and all of them are your problem, because you are the one who promised the client a working agent by a deadline.

The last lesson gave you the security review. This lesson is the wider gear it slots into: working with the three teams that control whether a rollout ever reaches production. IT, compliance, and legal are not a single wall you run into. They are three stakeholders with different incentives, different vocabularies, and different gates, and the intermediate consultant treats each one as a relationship to manage from the first scoping call, not as a hurdle to clear at the end.

## The rollout is a stakeholder channel, not a technical milestone

The failure this lesson exists to kill is the build-first, coordinate-later habit. You spend the engagement inside the construction of the agent, because that is the work you are good at and the work you can see. The rollout, by contrast, lives in other people's workflows: an IT change board, a compliance intake form, a legal review queue, a procurement system. None of it is visible on your critical path until it suddenly is, and by then the date has moved.

The reframe the working practitioner carries is that a rollout is a stakeholder channel. You are moving a working system through a series of owners, each of whom can say no for reasons that have nothing to do with whether the agent is good. Your job is to know, before you begin, who those owners are, what they need to say yes, and how long yes takes. You sequence that work in parallel with the build, so that by the time the agent is ready, the channel is ready too. A security review is one gate in that channel. IT provisioning, compliance sign-off, legal agreement, and procurement each sit in the same channel, and they all have lead times you can estimate and influence.

The reason this feels like scope creep is that most consultants were never taught to see it as part of delivery. It is. When you miss the IT deadline, the client does not blame IT. They blame you for not having told them the environment would take the whole window to provision. When legal holds up the model agreement, nobody remembers the agent was done on time; they remember the rollout that did not land. The stakeholders are part of your deliverable because the working system is the deliverable, and a system that cannot be switched on is not working.

## Who holds which gate

Start by naming the three teams precisely, because the mistake is to blur them into one vague "they" you cannot plan against.

IT controls the environment. IT owns the servers, the network, the identity system, the approval for what software runs where, and the change management that governs when production changes are allowed. IT asks questions like: where will this run, what resources does it need, who can access it, how does it authenticate, and does it touch anything already in production. IT's incentive is operational stability. They are not asked to be fast; they are asked to be reliable. That means their gate is a process, and the process has a cadence you need to understand in week one.[^1]

Compliance controls the rules the organization has committed to. Compliance owns the standards the company must meet: an external framework like SOC 2 or ISO 27001, a data-residency requirement, a retention policy, an audit expectation. Compliance asks where the data goes, what is stored, who can see it, and how long it lives. Its incentive is defensibility under audit. Compliance is not persuadable by enthusiasm; it is satisfiable by evidence, and you learned in lesson 16 that evidence means documentation, not assurances.

Legal controls the contractual frame. Legal owns the agreements the organization signs: the master agreement with the client, the terms you accept from a model provider, the data-processing addendum, the liability and indemnity language. Legal asks who is responsible if the agent does something wrong, who owns what, and what happens in a dispute. Legal's incentive is to limit the organization's exposure to liability. It moves on the speed of contract review, which is the slowest and least predictable of the three.

These three are not interchangeable, and a single point of contact is not a substitute for understanding each gate. When a client tells you "our security team needs to review it," the useful question is which review: the IT deployment review, the compliance framework review, or the legal terms review. They have different owners, different timelines, and different artifacts, and knowing which one is in front of you tells you what evidence to prepare and how long it will take.

## Triaging stakeholders into the engagement, not around it

The intermediate move is to bring these teams into the timeline early, as participants you sequence rather than obstacles you discover. Early has a specific meaning: before you finalize the architecture, not after you finish the build. That is the point where their constraints are still cheap to absorb.

You can do this without being the person who works inside the client's IT and legal. The channel runs through two points of leverage. First, the single sponsor: the person on the client side who has authority across these teams, usually the one who hired you or the one who will own the agent after you leave. You use that sponsor to open doors and to hear which team is nervous before they become a blocker. Second, intake questions: in week one you ask who owns the environment, who owns data governance, and who reviews contracts. You name those owners, and you take a meeting with each one before you build, even a short one, so that every constraint lands on the design table instead of being discovered at go-live.

A concrete habit that pays for itself: spend the first meeting on the stakeholder map, not the feature list. Draw the three teams, name the owner and the lead time for each gate, and get the sponsor to confirm it. You are estimating the rollout channel the same way you estimate the build, and an estimate only becomes trustworthy when you have named its components. A client that cannot name who owns IT or legal on day one is a client whose rollout you should assume will be slower than anyone's guess.

## Backward planning the channel

Once you know the gates, you plan the rollout backward from the go-live date. The principle is simple: the date does not move because an IT or legal gate was always going to take six weeks and you discovered it on the last day; the date moves because you plan from the gate's real lead time and schedule the build to land when the channel is open.

Worked example in a single line: if the model provider agreement needs legal review and legal turns contracts around in four to six weeks, then the agreement is not something you kick off after the build. You start it in week one, in parallel, so that the signature lands around the time the agent is ready. If IT only approves production changes in a monthly window, you target your go-live for the approval window that follows your build, not the Friday you happen to finish. Backward planning means every sticky gate has a start date earlier than you would naturally give it, because the lead time is the constraint and the build can flex around a channel that will not flex.

This is where the intermediate consultant earns the fee. The junior version finishes the build and then asks how long the rollout will take, which is a question that has no good answer because the gates were never named. The intermediate version runs the gates in parallel with the build and knows the go-live date before the build is done, because the slowest gate is the schedule. The difference is not intelligence; it is treating the rollout channel as first-class deliverable work with its own calendar, its own risks, and its own tracking, the way you would track any other thread of the engagement.

## Speaking the language of each team

The same technical decision gets presented differently to each of the three teams, not because you are being evasive but because each one is making a different decision. Your job is to translate, and translation is the skill.

To IT, the agent is a component that must operate inside their environment. You present it the way IT presents deployments: where it runs, what it needs, how it authenticates, what it can reach, what it changes, and how it is rolled back. Hermes gives you concrete answers here. The agent runs as a profile, and a profile is a self-contained home with its own config, memory, skills, and cron jobs, which is exactly the boundary IT wants to see named.[^2] The terminal context can be constrained, and the approval posture decides how much the agent can do on its own, which answers IT's core question about autonomy.[^3] You hand IT a deployment answer, not a demonstration of how clever the agent is. IT does not need to be impressed; IT needs to be able to operate the thing safely.

To compliance, the agent is a process that must be demonstrable. You answer the data-flow, retention, and audit questions from lesson 16, and you add the operational ones: what runs on a schedule, what it collects, and what the audit trail looks like. Compliance wants to see that behavior is legible. Hermes records provides the exhibits: scheduled jobs keep a run history with status and delivery, and sessions keep records, so there is a defensible record of what the agent did and when.[^4] You present the evidence compliance needs, not your confidence that the system is fine.

To legal, the agent is a source of potential liability that must be contractually bounded. You answer who is responsible if it errs: what the agent is instructed to do, what gates stand in its way, what it cannot reach, and what the agreement says about liability for its output. Legal is the venue where your scoping discipline from earlier in the course becomes legally load-bearing. A statement of work that names what the agent will and will not do, what data it touches, and under what approvals protects you, because legal assigns risk based on what is written. If you refused scope creep in delivery on the merits, refuse it here for the same reason and then a stronger one: the written word becomes the box in which liability lands.

The skill in all three is the same. Name the constraint, map it to a real, provable capability of the delivery, and hand them the artifact they can act on. The moment you stop explaining and start producing the thing each team asked for, the channel moves.

## Procurement and the written scope

One gate sits where consultant skills often fail the hardest: procurement, because it reads your statement of work literally. The problem is not that procurement is unreasonable; it is that the statement of work was written for a different audience. The document described the agent, and procurement has to turn it into a deliverable, a milestone package, and a payment against it. An SoW that cannot be read back as "here is what we deliver, here is when, here is what you get at each point" fails procurement, and the engagement stalls on an invoice.

The discipline is to write the commercial scope the way you would want to read it if you were paying. Deliverables named concretely, not abstractly. A deliverable is not "a support automation agent"; it is "an agent that triages inbound support tickets and routes them with a confidence score, delivered with runbooks and a one-week pilot." Milestones named. Payment tied to milestones, so every time you ask for money, there is a named thing you delivered. Acceptance criteria named, so both sides know what evidence closes the gate. The consulting discipline you already know, applied to the document that moves money.

This connects to the other teams. IT can only provision against a scope if the scope says what you are provisioning. Compliance can only review against a scope if the scope says what data is touched. Legal can only bound liability against a scope if the scope says what is in and out of the work. The statement of work is the artifact where all three channels converge, and a sloppy one is the fastest way to stall every gate at once.

## Governance after go-live

The rollout does not end at go-live, and the working practitioner keeps the channel open for the life of the delivery. Governance is the running version of what you did at the start: who owns changes, who reviews them, how the agent's behavior is audited, and how it is retired when the engagement ends. This is where lesson 12's change management meets lesson 16's audit trail and this lesson's stakeholders all at once.

For a scheduled agent this has real teeth. Hermes cron jobs are configured work that fire on their own, and a change to what a job runs on, or how it is approved, is a change to a system that acts without you watching. The platform fails closed here by design: an unpinned job does not silently run on a new model or provider because the global default changed; it skips, alerts, and waits for an explicit decision, so an unattended change nobody reviewed cannot quietly land.[^5] That is a governance model worth naming in front of the client. You do not rely on remembering to review a change; the system refuses to apply an unapproved one, which is exactly the guarantee an IT and compliance audience wants to hear.

You also plan for the end. An agent leaves behind access it was granted, credentials it held, and a record of what it did. Part of professional delivery is a documented offramp: revoke the access, rotate or destroy the credentials, hand over or archive the run history, and transfer whatever operating knowledge the client needs to keep running without you. A rollout that ends cleanly is the difference between a recommendation and a repeat engagement, and it is a governance deliverable you can name in the statement of work and charge for.

## Worked example: the rollout that nearly died in the channel

Put the whole method on one engagement. A mid-size financial company has hired you to deploy an agent that reconciles a daily finance report and files it for the accounting team. You scoped it, built it, and secured it from lesson 16. The rollout is where the lesson is.

Week one, you run the stakeholder map. The sponsor, the finance director, names IT's change board, which approves production changes on a monthly cadence; compliance, which answers to an external audit; and legal, which turns the provider agreement around in four to six weeks. You write the three gates with their owners and lead times into the engagement plan, and you kick off two things in week one that a build-first consultant would have left for the end: the provider agreement goes to legal immediately, and you take IT to the deployment review with a one-page environment plan before you have finished building.

Through the build you present to each team in its own language. To IT you hand the profile-based deployment answer: the agent runs as a self-contained home, its terminal context is constrained, its approval posture is documented, and its rollback is the pinned configuration it runs against.[^2][^3] IT asks where the report file writes, and you answer with a named path and a write gate. To compliance you hand the data-flow and retention answers plus the legible record: the nightly job's run history and status, so an auditor can see what ran and what it produced.[^4] To legal you name what the agent is instructed to do, what it cannot reach, and what gates stand in its way, and you point at the statement of work for the box in which liability lands.

The go-live lands in IT's next monthly window, which you targeted from week one. Legal's agreement arrives two days after the agent is ready, because you started it a month earlier. The nightly reconciliation runs on the pinned model and provider you agreed, fails closed on any unattended change, and posts a legible record compliance can audit. The rollout goes live on schedule, not because nothing went wrong but because every gate that could have stalled it was sequenced in week one. The client asks you to run the second reconciliation too.

## The discipline that transfers

Strip away Hermes and the same moves apply to every agent rollout you run.

**Name the three gates in week one.** IT owns the environment, compliance owns the rules the company has committed to, legal owns the contractual frame. Write who holds each gate, the lead time, and put it in the plan.

**Start the sticky gates before the build.** Legal review, procurement, IT provisioning all have lead times measured in weeks. Start them in parallel with the build so the channel is open when the agent is ready.

**Translate, do not explain.** Same technical decision, three audiences. To IT a bounded deployment. To compliance a legible, evidentiary process. To legal a contractually bounded scope.

**Write the commercial scope literally.** Deliverables by name, milestones, acceptance criteria. Procurement reads the SoW to pay you, and every team reads it to know what is in the work.

**Keep the channel open after go-live.** Governance is the running stakeholder relationship. Review changes against the approvals, keep an audit trail, and plan the offramp that ends the engagement cleanly.

## The pitfalls that stall a rollout

**Treating the three teams as one obstacle.** Blurring IT, compliance, and legal into a single "they" means you cannot plan against any of them. They have different owners, timelines, and artifacts.

**Building first, coordinating later.** Finishing the agent and then asking how long the rollout takes. The gates were always going to take weeks; plan from their lead time, backward.

**Presenting the same deck to everyone.** Compliance does not want a feature demo; legal does not want a threat model; IT does not want your enthusiasm. Give each one the artifact it acts on.

**Writing a statement of work about the agent.** Writing it about what you are building instead of what you are delivering, on what schedule, for what payment. Procurement and every team read the SoW literally.

**Stopping at go-live.** Ending the rollout the day the agent starts and leaving the stakeholder channel, the governance, and the offramp adrift to be someone else's problem later.

## Try it

A client has asked you to estimate an agent that drafts and sends follow-up emails to a shared customer mailbox. Blocking on that request, they have mentioned only that "security" needs to sign off. Estimate the rollout, not just the build.

1. Draw the stakeholder map. Name the IT, compliance, and legal gates, who holds each one, and the likely lead time, and write which of the three "security needs to sign off" is most likely to mean.
2. Say which two gates you start in week one, before you build, and why.
3. For each team, write the one-page artifact you would hand them: the IT deployment answer, the compliance evidence answer, and the legal scope answer.
4. Draft the statement-of-work scope paragraph that names the deliverable, the milestone, and the acceptance criteria so procurement can read it back as a payment schedule.
5. Write the go-live governance notes: what is reviewed before a change, what record the audit relies on, and how access and credentials are revoked when the engagement ends.

If you can do all five, you can carry a rollout through the channel. You name the gates, you sequence them alongside the build, you translate to each audience, and you plan the commercial and governance frame that keeps the working system working. That is what separates a consultant who delivers a build from one who delivers a rollout that actually lands.

## What's next

You now know how to carry a secure, well-built agent through the IT, compliance, and legal gates that control rollout momentum. You name the stakeholders early, translate into each one's language, sequence the sticky gates in parallel with the build, and keep the channel open through governance and handoff. But none of this settles what the engagement is worth, and how you get paid.
