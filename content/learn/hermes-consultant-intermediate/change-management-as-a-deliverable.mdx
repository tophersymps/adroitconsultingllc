# Lesson 12: Change Management as a Deliverable, Not an Afterthought

By this point in the course you have delivered a running agent and held it together when it failed. The client is no longer watching you build. They are watching the thing work, every day, and they have stopped thinking about how it works. That is exactly when the next discipline matters, because a live delivery does not stay still. The client will ask for a new output field. A faster or cheaper model will appear and look tempting. A reconciliation rule the agent got wrong in month two will need tightening. Each of these is a change to a system someone now depends on, and the way you handle the change is the difference between a delivery that keeps earning trust and a delivery that quietly rots under a stream of unexamined edits.

The mistake this lesson exists to kill is the drive-by change. The consultant is inside the agent every day, so a tweak feels small: adjust the prompt, flip the model, tighten the rule, push it, done. Nothing breaks visibly, so nothing gets recorded, and the delivery drifts one small edit at a time until nobody can say what the agent is actually configured to do. That is how a system that was validated and handed over becomes a system nobody trusts six months later. The alternative is to treat change as a deliverable in its own right: a bounded unit of work with a scope, a rationale, a test, a rollback, and a record, proposed to the client like any other piece of the engagement. That is what this lesson teaches.

## Why change is different from the build

During the build phase, you are creating a system, and the system being half-finished is normal and expected. The client reviews a draft, you change it, no harm done, because there was nothing yet depending on the old state. Change after handover is a different animal. The client has wired their week around the agent's output. Downstream systems read it, people act on it, and the previous behavior is now a baseline the client experiences as reality. A change is not an edit to your work. It is an alteration to someone else's daily operation, and it has to be treated with that weight.

The deepest reason change fails is not technical. It is that the person making the change is the same person who validated the original, so they trust their own judgment twice: once for the original design and once for the change on top of it. A consultant who built and validated a working agent will look at their own proposed tweak and see it through the lens of how well they know the system. That confidence is exactly what a change deserves suspicion about. The original was validated against a test. The change is being trusted because the author is smart. Those are not the same standard, and the discipline of change management is closing that gap by making every change earn its own validation.

## Scope the change like a mini-engagement

The consulting move is to treat each meaningful change as a small engagement with the same bones as the original: a scope, an acceptance bar, and a review. You do not run a full lifecycle for a one-line prompt tweak, but you do answer the same questions in miniature. What exactly is changing, and what is explicitly not changing? What problem does this change solve, and how will we know it solved it? Who has to approve it before it lands? How do we get back to the current state if it goes wrong? Answering those questions before you touch anything turns a drive-by edit into a deliverable the client can see and approve.

The scope discipline matters most because change has a tendency to sprawl. You set out to tighten one reconciliation rule, and while you are in the configuration you notice a prompt that could be clearer, a schedule that seems slightly off, a model that costs a little less. The temptation to fold these into the one change is strong, because it is efficient and the client will never know the difference. But every extra unrequested change is unrequested risk, bundled into a change the client thought they were approving on a narrow scope. Keep the change to what was asked and agreed. If you spot something else worth doing, propose it as its own change, with its own scope and rationale, instead of smuggling it in.

## Name the risk before you touch anything

Every change carries risk, and the honest consultant names it up front. The risk of a change to an agent is not uniform. Changing the display formatting of a report is low risk, because the failure is visible and reversible and does not corrupt anything downstream. Changing the model a scheduled production run uses is high risk, because model behavior is not deterministic and a new model can pass your spot checks and still differ in ways you did not test. Changing a reconciliation rule that decides whether a number is trusted is higher still, because the failure mode is a wrong number that looks right. The discipline is to classify the change before you make it, so you know how much validation, review, and rollback planning it earns.

The classification is not about refusing risk. It is about matching the weight of the process to the weight of the change. A low-risk change gets a light touch: make it, verify it once against known-good, tell the client, move on. A high-risk change gets the full treatment: a written proposal with the rationale, a review by the client, a test in a non-production environment or on recent real data, a staged rollout, and a rollback plan that is real before you start. When you classify honestly, you stop applying either too little care to the changes that matter or so much ceremony to trivial ones that the client stops reading your proposals.

## The rollback is the part that cannot be improvised

The single most important artifact of a change is not the change itself. It is the plan for undoing it. A change that cannot be rolled back cleanly is not a change you can afford to be wrong about, and you will be wrong about changes eventually, because an agent's behavior in production outruns your testing. The rollback plan has to be written before the change lands, and it has to be specific: exactly what you will revert, to exactly what prior state, and how you will confirm the revert worked.

This is where running on a tool with real state makes the discipline concrete. A scheduled agent job on Hermes carries its own configuration, including the model and provider it runs on, and those settings are what the job executes against at fire time. [^1] A change that swaps the model of a production job is reversible only if you can return that job to its prior pinned configuration, and a careful operator knows that pin is the rollback anchor before they change anything. More broadly, skills are versioned against an origin hash, and Hermes deliberately refuses to overwrite a skill you have modified, so your working version survives upstream syncs instead of being silently replaced. [^2] That protection is a change-control mechanism: it prevents an automated update from clobbering a change you made deliberately. A consultant who understands these mechanisms plans rollbacks around them instead of hoping nothing needs to go back.

## Changes to a system that runs itself need their own gates

An agent estate adds a wrinkle the classic change disciplines do not fully cover. A lot of the system does not wait for a human to act on it. Scheduled jobs run on their own, and they resolve their own configuration at fire time. That automation is exactly where unexamined change does its quiet damage, because there is no human in the loop to notice that a job started behaving differently. Hermes names this risk explicitly in its scheduling model. When an unpinned job's model or provider would change because the global default changed, the job does not silently run on the new configuration. It fails closed: it skips the run, makes no inference call, and alerts the operator once, rather than letting an unattended job quietly inherit a switch it was never approved for. [^1] That guard exists because a change nobody reviewed, applied to a job nobody watches, is the worst kind of change.

The lesson transfers straight to the consultant. An agent that changes its own behavior on a schedule needs gates that do not depend on you remembering to check. Before a change to anything that runs unattended, ask who or what will notice if the change is wrong. If the answer is nobody, the change needs an automated guard, an eval, or a verification step that runs after the next scheduled execution, not a hope that you will spot it. The difference between a healthy change and a dangerous one is often not the change itself. It is whether anything is watching after it lands.

## Communicate the change like a change, not like a status update

The consulting half of change management is that the client has to know a change is coming, why, and what it means for them, before it lands. This is not the same as telling them after it happened. A change to an agent the client depends on changes the client's expectations, even when the output looks the same, because the new behavior might differ in a case they care about. The trust-preserving move is to tell them in advance: here is the change, here is why, here is the risk I see, here is how I will test it, here is how we get back if it does not hold. You give them the chance to say "wait, that case matters to us," which is information you cannot get any other way.

The form follows the risk. A low-risk change gets a brief note. A high-risk change gets a short written proposal and an explicit approval before you touch the system, the same respect you would give the scope of the original build. What the client is really approving when they approve a high-risk change is not just the edit. It is the plan around it: the test, the rollout, the rollback, the verification. When you present change as a deliverable with those parts, the client stops fearing your edits and starts trusting your judgment, because you have shown them you think about what could go wrong before you act.

## Worked example: swapping the model on a live weekly run

Put the whole discipline on a concrete case. You delivered a weekly reconciliation agent that runs unattended every Monday morning, reads the sales and accounting exports, and posts a variance report for human approval. It has been stable for two months. A new model becomes available that claims better accuracy at lower cost, and the client's operations lead, who likes saving money, asks you to switch the agent to it.

First, scope it. The change is narrow: the model and provider the scheduled run fires on, and nothing else. You write down that the prompt, the reconciliation rules, the schedule, and the approval flow are all unchanged. The scope is one line, and it protects you from the client or your own instinct treating this as license to tidy other things.

Second, name the risk. This is a high-risk change, because you are replacing the engine that produces the numbers the client trusts, and model output is not deterministic. You say so plainly: a new model can pass spot checks and still differ on edge cases the old one handled. The classification earns the change a real test and a real rollout, not a flip and a hope.

Third, build the test before you flip anything. You run the new model on the last four weeks of real, already-reconciled data, and you compare its output against what the agent actually produced and what the human reviewer approved. You are not testing whether the new model is generally good. You are testing whether it reproduces the behavior this specific client depends on, on their data. Any case where it differs is a reason to pause and decide, not a reason to shrug.

Fourth, plan the rollback. The scheduled job carries its own model and provider pin, which is the exact setting that resolves when the job fires. [^1] Before you change it you confirm you can return that job to its prior pin in one step, and you write that step down. The rollback anchor exists before the change is made, because the worst time to discover you cannot revert is after the new model posts a wrong number.

Fifth, communicate and get approval. You send the operations lead the change as a mini-proposal: what changes, why, the risk you named, the test you ran, and the plan to watch the first live run closely and revert if it does not hold. They approve, because you gave them the whole picture instead of a vague "I'll switch the model."

Sixth, land it and watch. You make the change in a moment the client is prepared for, you watch the first real Monday run yourself instead of trusting the schedule, and you confirm the output still reconciles to the human-approved standard before you call it done. You keep the rollback warm for the first few runs, because the cases that break appear at real volume, not in your four-week sample.

Seventh, record it. The change goes into the delivery log the client can see: what changed, why, the test result, the rollout, the outcome. Six months from now, when someone asks why the agent runs on this model, the answer is in the record, not in a consultant's memory. That record is the deliverable that makes the next change and the eventual handover possible.

## The pitfalls that turn a change into an incident

**The drive-by edit.** You tweak the running agent without scoping, testing, or recording it, because it feels small. It is not small, because something depends on it. Every change earns a scope and a record, no matter how trivial it looks.

**Bundling unrequested changes.** You fold extra edits into the one change the client approved, because they are efficient and nobody will notice. Each one is unrequested risk inside an approved scope. Keep the change narrow and propose the rest separately.

**Trusting your own judgment over a test.** You validated the original, so you trust the change without validating it. The original earned a test. The change earns one too, against recent real data and the behavior the client depends on.

**Improving the rollback.** You make the change with no plan for undoing it, hoping it works. A change that cannot be rolled back is a change you cannot afford to be wrong about, and you will be wrong about one eventually. Write the revert before you start.

**Forgetting the system runs itself.** You change a scheduled job and trust that you will notice if it is wrong. Nobody is watching, so nothing notices until the client does. Add a guard, an eval, or a check after the next run for anything that runs unattended.

**Telling the client after the fact.** You make the change and mention it later, because the output looks the same. The client's expectations changed without their knowledge. Communicate high-risk changes before they land, with the rationale and the rollback, and let them flag the case you missed.

## Try it

Take the agent you have been delivering through this course, the one that now runs in production, and pick a change it genuinely needs: a new output field, a cheaper model, a rule that needs tightening, a schedule that should shift.

1. Scope the change in one line. State exactly what changes and, just as important, what explicitly does not.
2. Classify the risk. Say whether this is low, medium, or high, and justify it against how visible, reversible, and consequential the failure would be.
3. Name the test. Describe how you will validate the change on recent real data against the behavior the client depends on, before it lands.
4. Write the rollback. State the exact prior configuration you would return to and how you would confirm the revert worked.
5. Decide the communication. Say who approves this change, what you tell them before it lands, and what form the approval takes.
6. Plan the watch. Describe how you will confirm the change holds after the next real run, and what automated guard or eval catches it if nobody is looking.

If you can hold all six, you are no longer making changes to a delivery. You are managing changes as deliverables, which is what keeps a working agent working, and it is the reason a client keeps trusting a consultant's hand on the system long after the build is done.

## What's next

You can now take a live delivery and change it deliberately: scoped, risk-named, tested, reversible, communicated, and recorded. But every change you make adds to a body of knowledge about how the system actually works, and that knowledge currently lives in your head. The next lesson is about getting it out. It teaches handover that makes the client self-sufficient: operating docs and runbooks, how you write down how the agent is configured, how to change a rule, how to recover from a failed run, and how to transfer ownership so the client can run the system without you in the room. Where this lesson made you a safe pair of hands on a live system, the next one makes your absence the point.
