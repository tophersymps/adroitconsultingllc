# Lesson 11: Incident Discipline: Troubleshooting a Broken Agent Under Client Pressure

You have been delivering. The engagement is live, the agent is running, and the client is watching. Then something breaks. Not a wrong answer that Review catches and you fix quietly, but a failure mid-delivery: the agent stalls, produces nothing, or produces something you cannot verify, and the client can see it. Everything you learned about scoping, quality gates, and environments is now being tested in real time, and the pressure changes the quality of your thinking. This lesson is about surviving that moment and, more importantly, about not making it worse with the first thing you reach for.

The failure mode that this lesson exists to kill is the rush to patch. When an agent is down and a client is watching, the instinct is to change something quickly and claim it is fixed. That instinct is wrong twice. It is wrong because a patch on top of a wrong diagnosis does not fix the failure, it entangles it. And it is wrong because a claim of fixed that you have not verified is a lie you are telling the client, and the client will discover it the next time the agent runs. The discipline you need is the opposite of speed: slow down, establish what is actually true, and only then change something. That discipline is the incident skill, and it transfers to every delivery you ever run.

## The incident is not the bug

The first thing to learn is the distinction that changes how you behave. A bug is a defect in the output: the agent returns a wrong answer, a malformed file, a bad suggestion. Bugs are normal, they are expected with quality gates, and your eval discipline from earlier lessons is built to catch them. An incident is different. An incident is a live failure where the agent is not producing output you can use at all, where the client can see the failure or its absence of output, and where time pressure is real. A bug asks "how do I catch this." An incident asks "how do I get production producing again without making it worse."

The reason the distinction matters is that it changes the priority of the first answer you want. Against a bug, the right first answer is a correct diagnosis, because you have time. Against an incident, you want a working system, but you cannot buy working with a wrong diagnosis. The two pressures pull against each other, and the incident skill is holding a line between them: restore output, but only through a change you actually understand. The professional dies slowly on the wrong patch. Not because the patch is bad, but because the next failure, an hour later or a week later, now has two causes tangled together and neither one is diagnosable.

Establish ground truth before you touch anything. In Hermes Agent terms, that means you do not start editing the workflow when one fails. You read the history. You look at what the agent actually did in its logs and session records before the failure, because the answer to "why" is almost always visible in the trail of what already happened, not in the code you suspect. [^1] An incident investigation begins with reading the record of what ran, not with changing what runs.

## Diagnose before you patch

Here is the transferable discipline: the fix is a conclusion, not a first action. The order is always diagnosis first, then patch, then verify. Skip diagnosis and you are guessing, which is exactly the behavior pressure invites. When the client asks why the agent is down and you are not certain, the honest answer is "I do not know yet, and I am finding out," not a confident wrong explanation that sounds better in the moment.

The diagnostic move is to reproduce, or at least to bound. You want to know: does the failure happen every time, or once? Does it happen only in the client's environment, or in yours? Does it happen with one input class, or all of them? Hermes has the tools for this built in. You can run the agent's steps yourself in a controlled way, you can inspect the tool calls and responses, and you can test a narrower re-run. The point is to narrow the failure to a reproducible width, because a failure you can reproduce is a failure you can study, and a failure you can study is a failure you can fix with evidence instead of hope.

The component the intermediate consultant learns to check is the provider layer, because an agent is not a single program, it is a chain. There is the orchestration, the model behind it, the tool calls it makes, and the environment it runs in. Each link can break, and they break for different reasons. A sudden stall is often not the agent's logic at all. It is the model endpoint timing out, a provider that changed a default, an authentication token that expired, or a quota that ran out. [^2] The discipline is to check the whole chain in a disciplined order, not to assume the failure is where the code is most interesting. Most incident time is wasted debugging the wrong layer because the consultant assumed the software was at fault when the infrastructure was.

## Establish ground truth the client can trust

Under pressure you communicate, and what you communicate shapes whether the client trusts you. The rule is brutal and simple: status reflects actual current state, not a hopeful prediction. "It should be back up soon" is not a status. "It is down, I have reproduced the failure, and I am fixing the model timeout now" is. The client does not need your optimism. They need to know what is true, what you are doing, and when you will tell them again.

This is harder than it sounds, because the temptation when a client is worried is to give them a confident recovery time you do not actually have. Resist it. A confident wrong timeline destroys more trust than a humble correct one builds. The intermediate discipline is to say what you know, label what you do not, and give the client a commit you can keep: "I will report back in 30 minutes with a status, and I will tell you the moment I know what caused this." A recurring commitment to report is worth more than a single invented number, because it makes you accountable to a stream of truth instead of one hopeful claim.

There is a related skill that separates the people who learn from incidents from the people who repeat them: you write down what you are learning as you learn it. When you discover the failure mode mid-incident, capture it in the vault or a note, not after the fact when the details have blurred. [^3] The incident is when your memory of the sequence is freshest, and a written record made under pressure is the raw material for the review that keeps it from happening again.

## The fix that works, and the fix you claim works

The last discipline, and the one where most incidents go wrong in hindsight, is verification. A fix is not a fix until it has run and produced a verified result. Hermes teaches this by separating the act of running from the act of confirming. You run the change, you observe the output, and you confirm it against a known-good expected result, which is exactly the eval mindset you already built in earlier lessons. [^4] The intermediate consultant does not say "it should work now." They run it, watch the output, and say "it works, here is the output that proves it."

The failure mode that replicates incidents is the unverified fix. The agent runs once after the change and produces output, and the consultant declares it fixed, and then it fails again two hours later on a slightly different input because the real cause was never addressed. The discipline against this is to test the fix against the reproduction, not just against one happy run. You changed something because of a specific diagnosis. Prove the change resolves that specific failure, and then prove it does not break the normal path. Both proofs, cheap to run, are what turns a guess into a verified repair.

## Worked example: the agent that stalled under a client deadline

Bring it together on a concrete engagement. You are delivering an agent that clears the routine third of a support queue, and the client is watching a live dashboard that shows throughput. On a Tuesday at 3pm, the dashboard flatlines. The agent has stopped producing output. The client calls within five minutes. This is the incident.

Your discipline starts before you touch anything: you do not edit the agent yet. You read the record of what ran. Hermes keeps session and task history, so you pull the agent's log trail around the failure point and read the last successful actions before the stall. [^1] You see the last tool call was a model generation that never returned a completion. That narrows the failure to the model leg of the chain, not the agent's orchestration logic. Good. You have a hypothesis and it is grounded in the record, not in a guess about the code.

You bound it. You run a minimal completion against the same model endpoint yourself. It times out. So the failure reproduces at the provider layer, not in your agent at all. You check the provider status and find the model endpoint is degraded globally, and further, you notice your job was pinned to a single provider with no fallback, so the outage took you down with it. [^2]

Now you talk to the client honestly. You tell them the agent is down, that the cause is a model provider outage you have reproduced, and that you are switching the routing to a healthy endpoint and will report back in 30 minutes. You do not invent a full recovery time. You route around the degraded provider, rerun the agent against a healthy endpoint, confirm the expected output against your gold set, and then, and only then, tell the client it is working, with the evidence that it ran. [^4]

Then you make the incident not recur in the same way. You write the finding down in the vault: the agent was single-provider pinned with no fallback, and that is what turned a downstream outage into a client-facing failure. [^3] You change the routing to use a provider fallback so the next outage is absorbed instead of delivered to a client. The incident is over not when the agent runs again, but when the record shows why it failed, the fix is verified, and the prevention is in place.

## The discipline that transfers

Strip away Hermes and the same five moves apply to any failing delivery, any time, under any pressure.

**Establish ground truth before touching anything.** Read the record of what ran before you change what runs. The cause is almost always visible in the history of the failure, not in the code you suspect.

**Diagnose before you patch.** A fix is a conclusion, not a first action. Reproduce or bound the failure, check the whole chain in order, and never apply a patch on top of a diagnosis you have not made.

**Report true status, on a cadence you can keep.** Say what you know, label what you do not, and commit to a stream of honest updates rather than one invented recovery time.

**Verify before you claim.** A fix is not a fix until it has run and produced output you confirmed against known-good. Prove the change resolves the reproduction, and prove it does not break the normal path.

**Write it down as you know it.** Capture the failure mode during the incident, while it is fresh, so the prevention is not lost to hindsight the next week.

## The pitfalls that turn a bug into an incident

**Rushing to patch.** Changing something quickly because pressure demands action, before you know what is wrong. A patch on a wrong diagnosis entangles the failure and makes the next one undiagnosable.

**Assuming the software is the cause.** Checking only the agent's logic when the failure is in an infrastructure or provider link of the chain. Check the whole chain in a disciplined order.

**Giving a confident recovery time you do not have.** Optimism under pressure buys nothing and costs trust when it is wrong. Commit to a cadence of honest updates instead.

**Claiming you fixed it without running it.** Saying "it should work" when you have not reproduced and verified. Run it, watch the output, confirm against known-good.

**Repeating the incident because nothing was written down.** The unrecorded failure mode recurs on the next similar outage because the prevention never survived the moment.

**Forgetting the client is a person under pressure too.** The incident is not only a technical problem. The client needs truth and a cadence, and how you handle them during the failure is part of the deliverable.

## Try it

The client has an agent in production and the routine first third of the support queue has stopped moving. It ceased producing output 20 minutes ago and the client has already asked for a timeline.

1. Write the exact ground-truth step you do first, before changing anything, and how you read the record of what ran.
2. Walk the failure through the full chain: orchestration, model provider, tool calls, environment. List a concrete cause for each link and how you would check it.
3. Draft the honest status message you send the client now, including what you know, what you do not, and the cadence you will keep.
4. Describe the verification that turns a proposed fix into a confirmed fix, and say which known-good result you would confirm against.
5. Write the note you would capture during the incident so the failure mode does not recur, and the prevention you would put in place.

If you can do all five, you have incident discipline: you diagnose before you patch, you report what is true on a cadence you can keep, and you never claim a fix you have not run. That is the calm that a client pays for, and it is the reason one consultant resolves a live failure while another turns it into a crisis.

## What's next

You can now hold a delivery together when it fails live: establish ground truth, diagnose before you patch, report true status on a cadence you can keep, and verify a fix before you claim it. But an incident is a moment, and a delivery is a shape that changes over time. When the failure is fixed, the work of the engagement is not to return to the exact prior state, because the environment, the requirements, and the client's understanding may have moved. The next lesson teaches change management as a deliverable rather than an afterthought: how you propose, sequence, and land changes to a live engagement the client can see, without letting the handoff, the scope, or the trust the incident preserved erode as the work evolves.
