# Lesson 7: Team Delivery: When It's More Than One Consultant, How the Work Splits

The first six lessons were written for a delivery one consultant can carry end to end. That is the honest ceiling most engagements start well under. But multi-week engagements grow, deadlines compress, a client asks for two tracks at once, and the work becomes too big to hold in one head, one context, and one calendar. At that moment a single consultant is not the delivery model anymore. The skill that matters next is recognizing when that happens and splitting the work so more than one owner can hold it without it coming apart.

The core claim is this. Splitting work is not divide and conquer. It is interface design. When one person does a job, the seams between its parts live inside that person's head, and nobody else needs to know where they are. When two or more people do the job, those seams become the whole problem. Each part gets easier; the seams get hard. Nearly every team that fails is failing at a seam, not at a part.

## The ceiling of the single consultant

Name the conditions that signal one person can no longer carry a delivery. They usually arrive together, but each one is independently enough.

One: the work spans domains that one person does not genuinely hold. A stakeholder conversation, an architecture, a data migration, a build, and a QA pass are different crafts, and no single practitioner is the real owner of all of them at full depth. Two: the calendar physically cannot fit the work, even done well, inside the client's timeline. Three: the deliverable grew large enough that no single context can hold its state, and every decision references decisions made ten documents and ten tools ago.

The mistake practitioners make at this boundary is to keep doing it alone, only faster. They compress review, skip handoff, work from memory, and the engagement degrades until the client finds the gap. The right move is the opposite of doubling down on solo speed. It is to admit the seam exists and to make it explicit and durable. That is the substance of this lesson.

## The two axes of the split

When you split work across more than one owner, every piece lands on two axes, and you choose both deliberately.

The first axis is who owns it, split by specialization. Different parts of a delivery need different expertise, and each part goes to the person who is the genuine best owner of it. The person who understands the client's data owns the migration. The person with the strongest systems judgment owns the architecture. The person wired for detail owns the QA pass. The rule is that the split follows the real seams of the domain, not convenience and not title. A person handed a piece they are not the right owner for will either do it badly or quietly hand it back, and both show up later as a seam failure.

The second axis is in what order, split by dependency. Some work must run in sequence: you cannot test a requirement that was never captured, and you cannot deploy before the code clears review. Other work is genuinely independent and can run at the same time: two unrelated features, a stakeholder call and a build, a security pass and a documentation pass. Dependency decides sequence; independence decides parallelism. The skill is telling the two apart, because ordering is where most team deadlocks live.

These two axes are not cosmetic. A split that gets the who right but the order wrong produces a team that cannot move, because everyone waits on someone upstream. A split that gets the order right but the who wrong produces a team that moves and then finds the pieces do not fit. You set both, and you set them before the first piece starts.

## The seam is a contract, not a memory

Here is the load-bearing idea of the whole lesson. The place where two owners' work touches is the seam, and the seam must be a written, durable thing that both sides of it can read. It cannot be a memory, a verbal agreement, or someone in the room. Memory lives in one head and dies with the conversation. A team that runs on shared memory is really running on a single person's memory, which is exactly the constraint you left.

Think of the seam as an interface with three parts. It says what this piece delivers, in terms concrete enough that the downstream owner does not have to guess. It says what this piece needs from the pieces feeding it, so it fails loudly and early instead of silently. And it says the acceptance bar, what has to be true for the handoff to count as done. When every seam carries those three parts, the team can route work from owner to owner, and each one reads the seam it needs without living in anyone else's head.

In Hermes Agent this is the design of the multi agent board. The board is a durable task store shared across all the independent agent profiles you run, so coordination does not depend on any single agent staying alive. [^1] Every task is a durable row, every handoff is a row anyone can read and write, and every worker is a full OS process with its own identity. [^1] The comment thread is the inter agent protocol: when a worker starts it reads the whole thread as part of its context, so the record on the seam survives restarts and changes of owner. [^1] A completion is not done until it carries structured handoff metadata, the fields the next owner needs, so the transfer is machine readable instead of a paragraph someone parses by hand. [^1] The durable, written seam is not a nice to have in this system. It is the system. Copy that posture in any team, human or agent: if the seam is not written down and durable, it does not exist.

## A durable team or in process parallelism: knowing which you need

There is a tempting shortcut that looks like teaming and is not. If each team member is an AI agent, you can spawn parallel subagents, collect their answers, and call it a team. Every Hermes agent profile can do this: a delegation primitive spawns isolated child agents for parallel workstreams, each child given the context it needs and only its summary returning to the parent. [^2] This is real parallelism, and it is the right tool for short, reasoning heavy subtasks that feed a parent's next step: parallel research, a code review pass, a quick second opinion. [^2]

But in process parallelism is not a team, and conflating the two is the intermediate mistake this course exists to prevent. The deciding difference is durability and ownership. A delegated child is an anonymous subprocess: it returns an answer, that answer lands in the parent's context, and if the parent's run ends, the work and its record end with it. [^1] A team deliverable is different. It crosses owner boundaries, it survives restarts and interruptions, it can be picked up by a different role, and it has to be discoverable after the fact, because a client asks about it next week and a second reviewer needs the record of who decided what. [^1] When the work has those properties, you have crossed the line from delegating a subtask to running a delivery team, and the tool you need is the durable board, not the spawn of a parallel child. [^1]

Each member of a durable team is a named agent profile with its own config, memory, and role, which is what makes that member's behavior and record reproducible run after run. [^3] An anonymous spawned child cannot hold a role across a delivery; a named profile can. [^3]

The practical rule is short. Use in process parallelism when the parent needs a short reasoning answer before it can continue, no humans involved, and the result lives only in the parent's context. [^1] Use the durable team when the work crosses agent boundaries, needs to survive a restart, might need a human input, might be picked up by a different role, or must be findable later. [^1] They coexist: a worker on a durable team can still spawn a parallel child internally for a short subtask mid run. [^1] The skill is naming which of the two you are doing, because the wrong choice looks productive and then quietly loses work.

## Ordering and the deadlock trap

Once the seams are written, the next thing to get right is ordering, and this is where real teams, human and agent, stall. A dependency is a statement that one piece cannot start until another is done. You record every dependency as a link, and the system releases a piece only when every piece it depends on is done. [^1] That is the straightforward part. The trap is in the wiring.

The first wiring mistake is a dependency that points the wrong way. A fix needed to unblock a review has to be wired as the thing the review waits on, not as a child of the review. If you wire the fix as a child of the piece it is meant to unblock, the review sits blocked and the fix never runs, because its parent never completes. [^1] The whole team hangs on a loop that looks like progress and moves nothing. Read the dependency graph as a directed flow and check that every edge points from what must be done first to what waits on it.

The second wiring mistake is a review gate that dies silently. A review is a human moment, a set of eyes on the work before it ships. If the record that a review is required lives only as a status you set by hand, then the moment the dependency above completes, the flow can sweep the piece straight past the gate and ship work nobody looked at. In Hermes this is why a review is a durable block with the reason attached, the kind the promoter will not auto release, so the work physically cannot advance past it until a reviewer acts. [^1] Any team needs the same guarantee: a review noted in a status column is a hope, a review in a gate that blocks the flow is enforcement.

The third ordering mistake is giving everything a dependency. Work that is genuinely independent, an unrelated feature, a side audit, a documentation pass, does not need to wait on anything, and wiring it into a queue makes the team slow for no reason. The skill is separating the sequential spine from the parallel branches and letting the branches run instead of chaining them end to end.

## Routing to the right owner

A team only works if each piece is owned by the right specialist, and the right owner is a function of the piece's type, not of who has spare capacity. This is the maker-checker split, and it is non-negotiable at delivery scale. The person who builds a piece cannot be the sole judge of whether it is done; the acceptance bar has to be applied by a separate set of eyes, a reviewer whose job is to validate against the bar that was set before the work started. [^1] When the maker and the checker are the same person or the same agent, the acceptance bar becomes optional, because nothing external exists to hold it.

Routing also means not handing a piece to the wrong specialist just because that specialist is free. A QA pass assigned to the person who built the feature is not a QA pass. A build assigned to a reviewer is not a build. In a multi agent system this is the board's assignee field, and it is worth getting right before dispatch, because a piece routed to a profile with the wrong role either stalls the team or ships unreviewed. [^1]

## The orchestrator does not do the work

Somewhere in every team that forms, one person has to run the team: read the incoming request, decompose it into pieces, assign each to an owner, order the pieces, and watch the seams. That person is the orchestrator, and the single discipline that makes or breaks them is this: the orchestrator decomposes, routes, and observes, and does not execute the pieces themselves. [^1]

This is harder than it sounds, because the person with the best big picture is often also the fastest at the work. But an orchestrator who starts executing is an orchestrator who stops watching the seams, and the seams are exactly the failure surface the whole team exists to manage. [^1] The moment you are the orchestrator, your deliverable is the shape of the team and the integrity of its seams, not any single piece of production work. [^1] If a piece is small enough that you would just do it, there is a second question worth asking, and it is the real decision of this lesson.

## Route whole or decompose

Not everything has to be a team. The discipline includes the judgment to know when a piece is better owned end to end by one person than split. Two wrong directions bite here.

Splitting work that is actually one owner's domain creates coordination overhead with zero benefit, and it burns the team's capacity on seams nobody needed. Trying to run a genuinely multi domain piece as a single task hands someone a job bigger than their context can hold, and it either fails or gets done shallowly.

The rule is a filter. Decompose, and make it a team, when the piece spans multiple domains, when the workload is too large for one owner's view, or when it has an inherent sequential spine that needs specialist hands at each stage. [^1] Route whole, keep it one owner, when the piece is small and one specialist can own it end to end, when it is a single domain task, or when the request is explicitly scoped to one person. [^1] Naming which of these you are in before you create the cards is the difference between an engaged team and over engineering.

## Worked example: one consultant grows into a shipment

Take the logistics engagement from earlier lessons and turn it into the moment the consultant stops being one person. The scope has grown: the client wants the reconciliation system, a client-facing dashboard, and a data migration from their legacy warehouse, all inside one eight week window. One consultant cannot hold all three to the standard, so the consultant becomes a delivery lead and forms a team.

First the who. The consultant names the real seams of the domain. One owner, the strongest in the client's data model, takes the migration, because that is where the client's data quirks live. One owner takes the reconciliation logic and the rules, because that is where the correctness bar sits and it needs systems judgment. One owner takes the dashboard, because it is a different craft, interface, interaction, and accessibility, not data rules. A separate reviewer takes QA, and it is emphatically none of the people who built the pieces. [^1] Four roles, four assignments, and each follows the domain, not convenience.

Then the order. The consultant draws the dependency flow before anyone starts. The migration is the spine; the reconciliation feeds on the migrated data, and the dashboard feeds on both. A security pass and an accessibility pass are independent branches that run beside the build rather than after it, because they bind to their own bars and do not wait on the same queue. Every dependency is recorded so a piece only releases when what it needs is done. [^1]

Then the seams. Every handoff is written: what the migration delivers in terms the reconciliation owner can act on, what the reconciliation must return for the dashboard and the report, and the acceptance bar each reviewer applies. [^1] The reviewer gate is a durable block, so nothing ships until a separate set of eyes signs it. [^1] No one on the team lives in anyone else's head. Everything is on the seam.

And the lead does not pick up a shovel. They decompose, assign, order, and watch the seams, and they leave execution to the owners. [^1] That is what lets eight weeks of three-domain work, plus a separate set of review eyes, actually come together. The consultant who used to run engagements alone is now running a delivery team, and the skill that made it possible was not working faster. It was splitting the work and making the seams explicit and durable.

## The pitfalls that break a delivery team

One head holding all the memory. The moment seams exist, memory stops working as a coordination mechanism. Write the seams down, make them durable, and route through the written record, or the team is quietly a single person again.

Confusing parallelism with a team. Spawning parallel children for short subtasks is not the same as running a durable team. Know which you need: fork and collect for reasoning that feeds your next step, durable board for work that crosses owners and must survive.

Wrong way dependencies. Wire every fix as what its review waits on, never as a child of the thing it unblocks. A reversed edge is a deadlock the team hangs on while looking busy.

A review that is a wish. A review recorded in a status column is not enforcement; a gate that actually blocks the flow is. If a piece can ship past the review, you do not have a review.

A maker grading their own work. The acceptance bar exists to be held by an independent set of eyes. Self-review makes the bar optional and leaves the client as the only real checker, which is the review happening in the worst possible place.

An orchestrator who picks up a shovel. Running the team and doing the work are different jobs, and the moment you do both you stop watching the one thing the whole team depends on, the seams.

## Try it

Take the engagement you scoped and estimated in earlier lessons and set it free of the one consultant ceiling.

1. Name the signal that this delivery now exceeds a single owner: which domain split, which calendar constraint, which context limit. State it plainly.
2. Draw both axes for the whole scope. Who owns each piece, split by real domain expertise, and in what order each piece runs, split by dependency. Separate the sequential spine from the parallel branches.
3. Write the seam for three adjacent pieces. For each, write what it delivers, what it needs, and its acceptance bar, in terms the other side can act on without asking.
4. Decide, for two specific subtasks, whether each should be a parallel fork or durable team work. Name the deciding property each time.
5. Draw the dependency graph and check it twice for reversed edges: every edge must flow from what is done first to what waits on it. Mark where the review gates sit, and confirm none can be swept past.
6. Assign each piece to its right owner, and confirm no owner both builds and accepts their own work.

If you can hold all six, you can take a delivery too big for one person and turn it into a team that does not drift apart.
