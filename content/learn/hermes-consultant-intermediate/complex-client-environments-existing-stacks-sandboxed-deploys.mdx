# Lesson 5: Complex Client Environments: Existing Stacks, Enterprise Constraints, and Sandboxed Deploys

Lesson 4 gave you the toolkit: packaged, trigger-first skills that let the next engagement start from a running start instead of a blank page. This lesson is about what happens when you bring that toolkit into a company that already exists. The engagements so far, even the ones hardened for production, all happened in a world where you had a clean runway: you chose the data sources, the model, the guardrail set, the environment. That is the exception, not the rule. Most real work happens inside infrastructure, security rules, and processes you did not choose, and it happens inside a stack that was running before you arrived and will keep running after you leave.

The name for that world is the complex client environment, and it has three forces you have to meet. The existing stack: a codebase, a warehouse, a vendor, an identity system, all already chosen and already working. The enterprise constraints: security review, change windows, approval chains, compliance, network rules that were written to protect the company, not to make your delivery easier. And the sandboxed deploy: the way you land your agent next to all of that without touching anything you were not cleared to touch. Each force is a different reason the environment fights back, and each one is a skill you can learn. This lesson teaches all three, with Hermes Agent as the concrete vehicle.

## The false premise: you get to choose the environment

Here is the assumption that quietly breaks most consultants on their first real engagement, and it was safe to hold in Level 1 and the early lessons here. The assumption is that the delivery gets to define its own environment. You build in a clean space, then present the result and the client runs it. In a complex client environment that is backwards. The environment is given and the delivery has to fit it. You do not get to say "this system needs a Postgres cluster and a GPU box." You get to say "here is what the agent needs, and here is how it fits inside the infrastructure you already have."

The shift is not subtle. It changes what you do first. In the clean world you start by designing the system. In the complex world you start by mapping the environment, because the environment will reject a design that ignores it, no matter how good the design is. The tool that the consultant brings is not the freedom to choose. It is the skill to adapt: to land a working delivery inside constraints that were never designed for it.

This is why Lesson 4's toolkit matters here. The toolkit makes you fast, but it does not make you immune. A packaged skill written for one client's stack may assume data sources, tooling, or permissions that do not exist in the next client's environment. The intermediate consultant does not fight that. They treat the environment as a requirement set and adapt the toolkit to it, the same discipline you used in Lesson 2 to adapt requirements to the enacted reality. The toolkit bends. It does not break. Knowing how to bend it without breaking it is the whole game.

## Existing stacks: adapt, do not replace

The first force is the existing stack. The client runs a system that was built over years, by teams that may have left, with decisions nobody remembers and a few that everyone regrets. You are not there to replace it. You are there to make an agent work alongside it. The difference between a proposal that fails and one that lands is often exactly this: the successful consultant positions the delivery as an addition to the stack, not a replacement of it.

The discipline has three parts.

**Inventory the stack before you design.** Before you draw a single diagram of your agent, you list what already exists: the applications, the data sources, the identity provider, the vendors, the message buses, the approval tool, the ticketing system. You write down what each one is, who owns it, and how you reach it. You do this because the design decisions you would otherwise make from scratch, the choice of where data lives or how a workflow triggers, are largely already made for you. Your agent integrates with what is there. The inventory is the map of what you can integrate with, and it comes before the design, not after it.

**Work to the version that exists, not the one in the docs.** The stack has a documented interface and a running one, and they are not always the same, for exactly the reasons Lesson 2 described. The export format in the vendor's documentation may not be the format the running system actually emits. The API may have a new auth flow nobody updated the wiki for. So you verify the interfaces against the running system, not the documentation, and you design your agent against the version that exists. This is the as-built discipline applied to the stack itself, and it is the thing that stops a working integration from failing in week one.

**Name the interfaces, not the whole system.** You do not need to understand the client's entire architecture to integrate with it, and trying to will stall you. You need to understand the interfaces you cross: how you read this source, how you write to that system, how you authenticate, how you trigger the workflow. You bound your understanding to the seams. Everything inside the existing stack is the client's domain and their team's job. Your job is the boundary: the few precise points where your agent meets their system. Naming those interfaces precisely is what makes a large, intimidating stack tractable.

The existing stack is not an obstacle to work around. It is a set of constraints that, once you map them, tell you exactly where your delivery has room to move. The work starts there.

## Enterprise constraints: the rules you did not write

The second force is enterprise constraints, and this is where most consultants lose the engagement even when the design is right. A complex client environment is governed by rules that exist to protect the company, not to make your life easier. Security review before anything touches production. Change windows that decide when a deployment is allowed. Approval chains that route every decision through several people. Compliance requirements that dictate who can see what data and where it can live. Network rules that block the traffic your agent assumed it could use. None of these were written with your agent in mind, and they will not bend for it.

The consultant's instinct, when the environment fights back, is to treat the constraint as an obstacle and argue against it. That instinct is wrong, and here is why. The constraint is not an obstacle. It is a requirement set, exactly the kind of requirement set you learned to capture in Lesson 2. A security review is not a delay to be minimized. It is a requirement with a rule, an exception, and a boundary, and you capture it like any other requirement. Who must approve the deployment, what the agent is allowed to touch before approval, what it must never touch. Change management is not bureaucracy. It is a boundary with an owner: the change window belongs to the operations team, and they own when your agent ships. Compliance is not red tape. It is the rule that the agent may only see the data the client's policy permits it to see, and it decides what your integration can and cannot read.

When you reframe constraints as requirements, three things follow.

**You inventory them up front.** Before you promise a timeline, before you design the integration, you list every constraint that will touch the delivery: the security review, the change window, the approval chain, the compliance rule, the network restriction. You write them down, each with its owner and its process. This inventory becomes part of your project plan, because each constraint has a cost in time and effort that the plan must account for.

**You design within them instead of around them.** A design that assumes a rule away is a design that will not ship. A design that incorporates the rule, that puts the security review on the critical path as a first-class step rather than an afterthought, that builds the approval chain into the workflow instead of hoping it will not apply, is a design the client's own process can approve. The consultant who fights the security review loses. The consultant who treats it as a requirement set wins, because the security team becomes an ally instead of an adversary.

**You never promise what a constraint forbids.** The fastest way to lose trust is to tell the client the agent will do something the environment structurally prevents. So you check. Before you promise that the agent can reach the production warehouse, you verify the network allows it. Before you promise a weekly deploy, you confirm the change window exists that frequently. Every promise you make is checked against the constraint set, and the ones that fail become either a negotiation or a redesign, made explicitly rather than discovered at delivery.

Enterprise constraints are not the enemy of a good delivery. They are the delivery's conditions, and the consultant who masters them delivers work the company can actually accept.

## Sandboxed deploys: land without touching production

The third force is the sandboxed deploy, and it is the answer to the question the first two forces raise. The client's stack is live, its security rules are strict, and you still need to get your agent working next to the real systems. The way you do that, in any serious environment, is through a sandbox: an isolated execution environment where the agent runs and integrates without a write path to production. The sandbox is the seam that lets you land a delivery safely inside a system you do not control.

In Hermes Agent, the concrete example here, the seam is the terminal backend. The terminal backend decides where the agent's shell commands actually execute, and it is where you control the sandbox boundary. [^1] You can run the agent's commands on your local machine, inside a persistent Docker container, on a remote server over SSH, in a cloud sandbox such as Modal, Daytona, or a Vercel Sandbox, or in a Singularity container. [^2] Each choice changes where the agent's files and commands live and how much of the host it can see, which means each choice is a decision about the sandbox boundary you present to the client. [^3]

The discipline of the sandboxed deploy has three parts.

**Choose the sandbox by what it isolates.** The point of the sandbox is to give the agent a home that cannot reach the things it must not touch. In a complex client environment you typically want an isolated backend that can reach the integration targets, the data sources you were cleared to read, but has no path to production writes. Docker is a strong default because it gives you a persistent container that survives tool calls and subagents while keeping the agent off the host filesystem. [^4] SSH lets you place the agent on a remote worker with network access to the integration targets and no local footprint. [^5] Cloud sandboxes add serverless persistence, an environment that hibernates when idle. [^1] The choice is not which is newest. It is which isolates the boundary the client cares about.

**Understand what the sandbox does not do.** Here is the sharp edge. A cloud sandbox isolates the agent from the host, but the Hermes security docs are explicit that the cloud backends skip the dangerous command check. [^6] Isolation is not approval. A sandbox keeps the agent from reaching your laptop, but it does not stop the agent from doing something dangerous inside the sandbox, and it does not stop it from writing to an integration target it has credentials for. So the sandbox is not a substitute for the guardrails from Lesson 3. It is a layer. You use the sandbox to control where the agent runs, and you use the guardrail set to control what it is allowed to do there. The two work together, and you do not drop the second because you added the first.

**Let the environment enforce its own constraints.** The enterprise will often want to pin certain settings so no operator can override them. In Hermes Agent this is managed scope: an administrator can pin specific config and secret values that a standard user cannot override. [^7] That is the client's version of the sandbox, applied to configuration instead of files. It lets the security team guarantee that the agent always uses the approved model, the approved network path, the approved credentials, regardless of who runs it. You support this rather than fight it, because a constraint the environment enforces structurally is one you never have to trust someone to remember.

The sandboxed deploy is the pattern that makes the other two forces survivable. You map the existing stack, you accept the enterprise constraints as requirements, and you land your agent in an isolated environment that respects both, with the guardrails still holding inside it. That is a delivery the client can accept and keep running.

## Worked example: the logistics agent lands in a real company

Take the logistics engagement from Lessons 1 through 3 and bring it into the world this lesson describes. The six-week build went well, the agent passed its parallel run and its evals, and the client wants to move it from the pilot into the company's real operations. But the pilot ran in a clean environment the consultant controlled. Production is a different company.

The existing stack turns out to be three systems and an ERP, all owned by different teams. The warehouse system from the pilot is real, and the consultant's first surprise is that its documented export format is not what the running system emits. The team who maintains it built a custom export years ago and never updated the wiki. The consultant, applying the as-built discipline, verifies the interface against the running system, captures the actual format, and adjusts the integration before it ever ships. The inventory also reveals an approval tool the client insists all report signoffs go through, so the Monday report draft posts there instead of the team channel.

The enterprise constraints are substantial. The security team requires that any vendor tool run inside an isolated Docker container, with no write access to the ERP, reachable only through the corporate network. [^4] The change window for production integrations is a four-hour slot once a week. [^8] The compliance rule is that the agent may read the warehouse data but may not see payroll figures, which share the warehouse database. Each constraint becomes a captured requirement with an owner. The security team owns the container boundary. The operations team owns the change window. The compliance officer owns the data rule. The consultant puts all three on the project plan as first-class steps, not afterthoughts.

The sandboxed deploy is where it comes together. The consultant runs the agent inside a Docker container, isolated from the ERP, with no production write path. [^4] The deny rules from Lesson 3 become container-level facts: the agent physically cannot write to the ERP because the container has no route to it. [^9] The change window gates the deploy: the agent ships in the weekly four-hour slot, with the security review signed off before the window opens. [^8] The compliance rule is handled by the integration design: the agent reads only the views it was granted, which exclude payroll. The guardrails still hold inside the sandbox, because the sandbox controls where the agent runs and the guardrails control what it does there.

Notice what changed between the pilot and production. In the pilot the consultant chose the environment. In production the environment chose the terms, and the consultant adapted the toolkit to them. The core agent, the capture discipline, the eval suite, all of it carried over, but each one bent to the existing stack, the enterprise constraints, and the sandbox. That is the intermediate skill in action: not designing a clean system, but landing a working one inside the conditions the client already has.

## The skill that transfers: adapt, do not fight

Run the three forces together and one pattern emerges, and it is the transferable skill this lesson is really teaching. The pattern is the same whether the client is a logistics company, a bank, or a government agency, and it has five steps.

**Name the stack.** Inventory what already exists and work to the running version, not the documented one.

**Map the constraints.** List every rule that will touch the delivery, each with its owner and its process, and treat the list as part of the plan.

**Find the seam.** Choose the sandbox that isolates the boundary the client cares about, and be honest about what the sandbox does and does not stop.

**Land small and prove it.** Ship through the sandbox, through the change window, and validate against the eval suite before you ever claim production readiness.

**Bend the toolkit, never break it.** Your packaged skills carry the discipline forward, but they must adapt to the environment. The toolkit that cannot bend is the toolkit that fails in week one.

This is the difference between the consultant who delivers in a clean room and the one who delivers in the world the client actually lives in. The clean-room consultant designs a system. The intermediate consultant maps the environment, accepts its rules, and lands a system inside it that the client's own process can approve and its own team can maintain.

## The pitfalls that break a complex-environment delivery

**Assuming you choose the environment.** The clean-room instinct, safe in Level 1, fatal here. You do not get to define the stack, the rules, or the deploy path. Map the environment first and design to it.

**Trusting the documentation over the running system.** The stack's docs and its reality diverge, and designing to the docs ships an integration that breaks in week one. Verify every interface against what actually runs.

**Fighting the security review.** The constraint is a requirement set with an owner, not an obstacle. The consultant who treats the security team as an adversary loses. The one who treats the review as a first-class step wins.

**Using the sandbox as a substitute for guardrails.** A cloud sandbox isolates the agent from the host but skips the dangerous command check. [^6] Isolation and approval are different layers. You need both, and the second does not disappear because you added the first.

**Promising what a constraint forbids.** Telling the client the agent will do something the environment prevents is the fastest way to lose trust. Check every promise against the constraint set before you make it.

## Try it

Take the production agent you built in Lesson 3, or the toolkit you packaged in Lesson 4, and run it through the complex-environment discipline.

1. Invent the client environment: a plausible existing stack with three named systems, each with an owner, and write the inventory.
2. Name at least four enterprise constraints that would touch your delivery, each with its owner and its process, and state which one has the power to stop you.
3. Choose the sandbox: which Hermes terminal backend isolates the boundary your client cares about, and what does it actually isolate versus only appear to isolate? [^2]
4. Write the two-layer answer: what the sandbox stops, and what the guardrail set must still stop inside it.
5. Verify every interface in your design against a hypothetical running version that differs from the docs. Write the divergence and the adjustment it forces.
6. Write the landing sequence: the order in which you inventory, map constraints, choose the seam, ship, and validate, and where the change window and security review sit in that sequence.

If you can defend all six, you are no longer designing in a clean room. You are landing a delivery in the world the client already lives in, and that is where the intermediate consultant earns their keep.
