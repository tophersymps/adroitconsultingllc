# Lesson 15: Vendor-Agnostic Translation: Adapting the Playbook to Other Agent Tools

For thirteen lessons you built a playbook. Scoping, as-built capture, routing, evals, guardrails, runbooks, pricing, handover. Every one of them I taught through Hermes Agent, and that was deliberate: a concrete tool makes an abstract discipline learnable. The risk is that the two become fused in your head. You start thinking the discipline is Hermes, when Hermes is only the place you happened to practice it.

This lesson is the separation. Clients do not buy a tool. They buy an outcome, and they often already own the tool they want it delivered on. The consultant who can only express their craft in one agent's vocabulary is a consultant who cannot be dropped into the client's existing estate. The consultant who can translate the playbook is the one who gets the engagement, because they meet the client where the client already is.

Here is the core claim you should leave with. **Your expertise is the playbook, not the tool. The discipline transfers; the mechanics do not.** Everything after this is about knowing which of the two you are looking at in any given moment, and how to move the one without dragging the other.

## Why the playbook gets trapped inside the tool

Nobody fuses a craft to its tool on purpose. It happens because the tool gives you the vocabulary you think in. When you have spent weeks in Hermes, "skill" means a specific thing: a SKILL.md file with progressive disclosure, a description under sixty characters, a slash command, a version. [^1] When you say "guardrail," you mean the approval modes, the deny list, the hardline blocklist. [^2] When you say "eval," you mean a scheduled skill-backed cron job against a gold set. [^3] The words are precise, and precision is good, until the day you sit in front of a client running a different agent and the words stop meaning anything.

The failure shows up as one of two habits.

The first is **re-transcription dressed up as translation.** You take the Hermes artifact, the SKILL.md or the deny-rule list, and you copy it into whatever format the new tool reads, assuming that because the file landed in the right place the discipline landed with it. A rules file is not a skill. A system prompt is not an eval gate. Putting a checklist in a markdown file the agent loads and having the agent actually follow it under pressure are different things, and the second is the one the client pays for.

The second is **forcing the tool to be Hermes.** The new agent has no kanban, no profiles, no skill manager, and instead of asking what the discipline looks like in this tool you try to approximate Hermes inside it, building fake structures to house concepts the tool never had. That does not adapt your playbook; it drags a foreign body into the client's environment and calls the friction a feature.

![The two habits that trap the playbook in the tool: re-transcription dressed up as translation, and forcing the tool to be Hermes. Both come from confusing the discipline with the artifact that carries it.](</diagrams/vendor-agnostic-translation-adapting-the-playbook-to-other-agent-tools/4-two-failure-habits.png>)

Both habits come from the same error: confusing the discipline with the artifact that carries it. The fix is to pull the two apart on purpose, which is what the rest of this lesson is.

## The three-layer model of any discipline

The way to keep a discipline portable is to stop thinking of it as one thing and think of it as three things stacked. When you translate, you change the bottom layer and leave the top two alone.

![The three-layer model of any discipline: intent (what it is for), mapping (which tool capability carries it), and implementation (the syntax). Translation changes the bottom layer and leaves the top two alone.](</diagrams/vendor-agnostic-translation-adapting-the-playbook-to-other-agent-tools/1-three-layer-model.png>)

**The intent layer is what the discipline is for.** Why does this discipline exist, and what bad outcome does it prevent? Scoping prevents you from promising a fixed price on work you have not understood. An eval gate prevents a silent regression. A guardrail prevents a destructive action on a system you do not own. The intent is the load-bearing part. It survives any tool, any vendor, any model.

**The mapping layer is which capability of the tool carries it.** Once you know what you are trying to prevent, you ask: does this tool have a primitive that does this? Hermes expresses an eval gate as a scheduled skill-backed cron job. [^3] Another tool might express the same intent as a CI check that runs the agent in a sandbox and fails the build on a wrong answer. A third might have no built-in mechanism at all, in which case the mapping is to an external system, a cron on the client's infra or a stage in their pipeline. The mapping layer is where your judgment lives. It is the part you cannot copy from a template, because it depends on what the specific tool can actually do.

**The implementation layer is the syntax.** The exact YAML, the exact file the tool reads, the exact command. This is the only layer that is genuinely throwaway. It is the cheapest to replace, the most likely to change across tool versions, and the least worth memorizing. A good translator spends almost no time here and a lot of time on the middle layer.

Every discipline in your playbook decomposes this way. Take routing from Lesson 3. The intent is "the workload runs on the capability that is right for it, and the bill is deliberate." [^4] The mapping in Hermes is per-job model pins and a fleet default. [^4] The mapping in a tool with no per-task model control might be nothing, because the tool gives you one model and you cannot split tiers. That does not make the discipline vanish. It changes the mapping: you either route at the tool boundary, sending the cheap jobs to a different agent entirely, or you tell the client honestly that this tool cannot do tiered routing and that is a capability decision to make, not a config detail to hide.

## How to actually translate: the discipline, not the artifact

The method has five steps, and you run them in this order so you never reach for a file before you know what you are moving.

![The five-step translation method: name the intent in one sentence, list the tool's primitives, map each intent to the primitive that carries it, build the minimal implementation, then verify the discipline held.](</diagrams/vendor-agnostic-translation-adapting-the-playbook-to-other-agent-tools/2-five-step-method.png>)

**First, name the intent in one sentence.** Not "a skill for reconciliation." That is an artifact. The intent is "the agent follows the reconciliation procedure, including the refusal case, every time, without being retold." Write that sentence. It is your North Star and it does not mention any tool.

**Second, list the tool's primitives.** What does this agent actually give you for shaping behavior? Every agent tool has a small set of real levers: instructions the agent loads, tools it can call, hooks or steps it runs, a scheduler if you are lucky, a permission model, a way to define tasks. Write them down as a short inventory. Do not assume; verify against the tool's docs, because the whole point of this lesson is that you cannot trust your mental model of a tool you have not used.

**Third, map each intent to the primitive that genuinely carries it.** This is the real work and it is where you earn the fee. An intent about "follow this procedure every time" maps to whatever the tool uses for durable instructions the agent loads at the start of relevant work. An intent about "do not run this destructive command" maps to whatever stops a command, which may be a permission rule, a blocklist, a hook, or nothing at all. You are allowed to find that a tool has no primitive for an intent you care about. That is not a dead end. It is the most valuable finding in the engagement, because it is an honest capability gap the client needs to know about.

**Fourth, build the minimal implementation.** Write only what the intent needs, in the tool's syntax, and nothing more. Resist the urge to reproduce the richness of the Hermes version. The Hermes skill has progressive disclosure, a version, tags, a bundle. [^1] Those exist to solve Hermes-scale problems. The client's tool may not need any of them. Port the intent, not the plumbing.

**Fifth, verify the discipline held.** This is the step transcribers skip, and it is the one that separates translation from theater. Run the agent and confirm the intent actually fired. Did it refuse the mismatch case, or did it answer it? Did the guardrail block the command, or did the file land in the wrong place and nothing block it at all? A ported discipline that does not hold is worse than no discipline, because it looks shipped.

The rule that ties it together: **you know the translation worked when the bad outcome the intent exists to prevent is still prevented, in the new tool, under real use.** Not when the files are in place. When the behavior holds.

## What actually transfers whole

Before you over-rotate on tool differences, be clear about how much of the playbook does not care what tool you are on. The large majority of it transfers without a single change, because it lives entirely in the intent layer.

The engagement lifecycle, the week-by-week plan from Lesson 1, does not care what agent runs underneath. Scope definition, the acceptance criteria, the statement of work, the pricing decision from Lessons 6 and 18, all of that is consulting craft and it is identical whether the delivery agent is Hermes, a vendor's enterprise assistant, or a tool your client built internally.

The as-built register from Lesson 2 transfers whole, because it is a document, not a mechanism. You captured the rule, the exception, and the boundary so they survive handover. That document is tool-neutral. What changes is only how the boundary gets enforced in a specific tool, which is the mapping step, not the document itself.

The communication discipline transfers whole. The client-facing posture, the honest blocker reporting, the expectation-setting, the change management from Lesson 12, none of that has a tool primitive. It is how a professional behaves, and it is the same on any delivery.

So the picture is not "everything is tool-specific and you must rebuild constantly." It is the opposite. The bulk of what makes you good is already portable. The tool-specific sliver is real, but it is a sliver, and it is the part you now know how to handle. Most of the anxiety about switching tools is really anxiety about that sliver, and most of that anxiety is misplaced.

## Worked example: porting the guardrail and eval disciplines

Let me make this concrete with a single scenario, because translation is a skill you learn by watching it happen once before you do it yourself.

You are delivering to a client whose security team has mandated their own internal agent tool. They will not adopt Hermes, and they will not reconsider. The engagement covers the Monday report system you know from earlier lessons: reconciliation of three data sources, a report draft, and a mismatch case that must refuse rather than invent. You have built this before in Hermes with guardrails and an eval gate. [^2] [^3] [^4] Now you rebuild it on their tool, and you use the five steps.

Step one, the intents. You write three sentences. The agent never writes to the three source systems. The mismatch case is refused, never explained into an answer. And a change to a rule, a source, or a model cannot ship until the gold set still passes.

Step two, the primitives inventory. You read the client tool's docs, and you find it has three real levers: a project instruction file the agent loads, a plugin hook that runs before certain commands, and a CI step the team already uses for their normal builds. It has no scheduler of its own and no built-in approval concept.

Step three, the mapping. The "never writes to the source systems" intent maps to a blocklist enforced in the plugin hook, because the hook runs before the command and can abort it. It does not map to the instruction file, because an instruction is advice and you established back in Lesson 3 that advice does not hold under pressure; the enforced hook is the guardrail. The "refuse the mismatch" intent is harder. The tool has no rule engine, so the intent maps to a prompt contract plus a test, which moves it into the eval. The "no change ships without the gold set" intent maps to the CI step, because that is the only gate in this tool that runs automatically and can block a merge.

![Worked example: the three Monday report intents mapped to the client tool's primitives. Never writes to the source systems maps to a blocklist in the plugin hook, the mismatch refusal maps to a prompt contract plus a test, and no change ships without the gold set maps to the CI step.](</diagrams/vendor-agnostic-translation-adapting-the-playbook-to-other-agent-tools/3-worked-example-mapping.png>)

Step four, the minimal implementation. You write the plugin hook with the three blocked patterns. You write the CI step that runs the gold set and fails the build on a regression. You do not try to reproduce Hermes profiles or skill bundles, because this tool has none and the intents do not need them. [^1] [^5] The port is lean on purpose.

Step five, verification. This is where you find the hard truth. The CI gate fires on every merge, and you confirm the guardrail hook actually blocks a test write you deliberately trigger in a sandbox. But when you run the mismatch case against the agent, it answers instead of refusing, because in this tool the refusal depends on the model following the instruction, and there is no structural block to stop it from complying the wrong way. You have found the capability gap. You take it to the client honestly: on this tool, the refusal case is enforced by evaluation and review, not by construction, and if they need it enforced by construction, that is a requirement on their tool selection or a scope note, not something you can paper over.

Notice what you did not do. You did not copy the Hermes deny-rule YAML into the client tool and assume it worked, because this tool has no equivalent field and the YAML would have been dead text. [^2] You did not fake a Hermes-style skill system inside their agent, because their tool is not Hermes and pretending it is would have made the delivery fragile. You translated the intent, mapped it to the primitives that actually existed, built the minimum, and then verified, and the verification is what surfaced the one honest limitation. That is the whole craft.

## The honest capability gap is a deliverable

The hardest thing a translator learns is when to stop translating and report. Most of the time the intents map cleanly and you deliver the standard. But sometimes a client tool genuinely cannot hold an intent you consider non-negotiable, and your job in that moment is not to contort the tool until it almost works. Your job is to name the gap.

This is where the consultant earns trust, and it is where a less honest practitioner loses it. The temptation when the client's mandated tool cannot enforce the guardrail is to say it is fine, to file the blocklist as a text file and tell yourself the agent will behave. That is exactly the regression Lesson 3 warned about, a boundary that lives only in a document and does not hold. [^2] The client does not need your reassurance that the tool is adequate. They need to know, before something bad happens, that one intent on their required stack is enforced by review rather than by construction, so they can decide whether that is acceptable for their risk posture.

An honest capability gap is a deliverable with a price and a sign-off. It says: here is the intent, here is what the tool enforces, here is what it does not, here is what that means for the client, and here are the options. The options might be a different tool for the high-risk part, a human review step, or an explicit acceptance of residual risk. What they never are is "trust me, it will be fine."

## The pitfalls that break a translation

**Re-transcription instead of translation.** Copying the file format and assuming the discipline followed. A rules file is not enforcement, and a system prompt is not a gate. Map the intent to a primitive that genuinely carries it, then verify the behavior.

**Forcing the tool to be Hermes.** Building fake structures to house concepts the tool never had. If the tool has no primitive, that is a capability gap to report, not an excuse to improvise a fragile approximation.

**Skipping verification.** Assuming that because the files landed in the right place, the intent fired. It does not. Run the agent, trigger the bad case, and confirm the bad outcome is still prevented before you claim the discipline is ported.

**Treating a ported instruction as a ported guardrail.** This is the repeat of Lesson 3's lesson in a new costume. A behavioral instruction is advice. Only a structural mechanism holds under pressure. Know which one you built, and never let the client think you built the stronger one when you built the weaker one.

**Hoarding tool trivia.** Memorizing the exact syntax of every agent tool's config is a losing game, because the syntax is the throwaway layer. Spend your learning on intents and mappings. The syntax you can look up; the judgment you cannot.

## Try it

Pick an agent tool you have never used, or one a client runs that you have only read about. If you do not have one handy, use a generic internal agent or even the free tier of a vendor assistant you have been meaning to learn.

1. Write the three most important intents from the Monday report engagement, each as a one-sentence statement that mentions no tool.
2. Read the tool's documentation and list its real primitives: instruction files, hooks, scheduler, permissions, CI integration, task definitions. Do not assume from memory.
3. Map each intent to the primitive that genuinely carries it. Write down where you had no clean mapping, and be honest with yourself about it.
4. Implement the minimum. Port the intent, not the Hermes plumbing. Do not reproduce features the tool does not have.
5. Verify the discipline held. Run the agent, trigger the refusal case and the guardrail case, and report which held and which did not.
6. Write the capability-gap note: what the tool enforces by construction, what it enforces only by review, and what you told the client about the difference.

If you can do all six, you have stopped being a Hermes consultant and become an agent consultant, one whose playbook travels with them and whose value the client can keep no matter what tool they standardize on next. That is the endgame of the discipline, and it is what this lesson was really about.
