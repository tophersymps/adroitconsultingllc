# Lesson 18: Pricing Individual Engagements: Fixed-Fee vs Time-and-Materials in Practice

Lesson 6 taught you to size a delivery. Lesson 8 taught you to prove its value before anyone signs. This lesson teaches what you do with the number and the proof: you put a price on the engagement. That sounds like the simplest step in the whole course, and it is where more practitioners lose money than anywhere else, because pricing is not about picking a number. Pricing is about allocating risk. The contract mechanism you choose decides who carries the uncertainty of the work, and the person who carries the risk is the person who gets paid for it or eats the cost when the estimate is wrong.

This lesson takes the estimate from Lesson 6 and the value proof from Lesson 8 and turns them into the two pricing mechanisms a consultant actually uses: fixed-fee, where you name a price for a defined scope, and time-and-materials, where you name a rate and charge for the time the work takes. You will learn what each mechanism really allocates, how to choose between them when the two candidates are both on the table, how to price each one from a real unit economy instead of a guess, and how to build change control that keeps either model honest when the client's scope moves. Hermes Agent stays the concrete vehicle for the worked example, and the transferable skill is the risk reasoning, which applies to any delivery you ever price.

## Both are risk contracts, and the risk sits on one side

The cleanest way to think about pricing is to ignore the units and focus on the question each mechanism answers. A fixed-fee answers "what is the price of the defined deliverable?" A time-and-materials contract answers "what is the price of a unit of work, whatever the deliverable ends up being?" Those are different promises, and they load the estimation risk onto opposite sides of the table.

Under fixed-fee, you promise a deliverable at a price. If the work takes longer than you estimated, you absorb the overrun. If it takes less, you keep the difference. The estimation risk, the chance that the scope is bigger or the environment harder than you priced, sits on you. The client pays a known number and holds no estimation risk at all.

Under time-and-materials, you promise a rate and honest hours. If the work takes longer than either of you expected, the client absorbs the overrun, because they pay for every hour you actually spend. The estimation risk sits on them. You are paid for the true effort either way, so the failure mode that hurts you under fixed-fee, an estimate that comes in low, becomes a neutral event under time-and-materials.

Neither side of that allocation is better. Both are legitimate, and the consultant who can only operate one of them is missing half the toolkit. The skill is reading the engagement and choosing the mechanism whose risk sits on the side best able to manage that risk. That criterion, not comfort and not habit, is how you decide.

## When the risk belongs on you: the fixed-fee case

Fixed-fee is the right call when you genuinely know the work. That is the whole test. You price a fixed number when the scope is understood, the environment is known, the delivery is the kind you have run before, and the main uncertainty is whether you execute, not what the work turns out to be. When you can name the deliverable and the estimate with enough confidence that the overrun risk is small and manageable, you take that risk on yourself and charge for doing so.

Inspecting the mechanism, fixed-fee is really the estimate from Lesson 6 with a margin wrapped around it. You map the critical path, you range the pessimistic end, you add the explicit buffer, and then you price the buffer into the number. The client buys certainty, and you sell them certainty at a price that covers the cases in which the certainty costs you. A fixed-fee that does not contain the Lesson 6 buffer is not a fixed-fee, it is a gamble with your own margin as the stake.

Fixed-fee also aligns a certain kind of incentive well. The client wants the deliverable on a budget, and you want to deliver efficiently because your profit is the difference between the price and your real cost. Both of you want the work to finish. That alignment is real, and it is why clients who care about a predictable budget prefer fixed-fee. You and the client are on the same side of the effort question, and the only thing you must defend is the scope line, because the moment the scope moves past what you priced, the alignment breaks.

The failure mode you must respect is scope drift. A fixed-fee is only fixed if the scope is fixed. The client will ask for additions, and every addition that does not trigger a change control eats your margin. This is where the pricing discipline meets the scope discipline from the earlier lessons. You price the fixed scope, you define the boundary in writing before you start, and every request that crosses the boundary becomes a change order, not a silent adjustment. A fixed-fee without enforced change control is a fixed-fee that slowly becomes time-and-materials where you forgot to charge the time.

## When the risk belongs on the client: the time-and-materials case

Time-and-materials is the right call when you do not know the work, and the honest practitioner admits that this is most of the real world. The scope is exploratory, the environment is unfamiliar, the requirements are being discovered as you go, the client's own stack keeps changing under you. When you cannot name the deliverable at the start, you have no business naming a fixed price for it. You name a rate, and you bill the true effort, and both sides accept that the total is not knowable in advance.

This is not a weaker contract. It is an honest one. The client who wants a fixed number for work that has never been scoped is asking you to invent a certainty that neither of you has, and the number you produce will be wrong in one of two ways. It will be too high, and the client overpays for the speculative risk you loaded into it, or it will be too low, and you eat the overrun on work whose difficulty nobody could predict. Time-and-materials removes the fiction and lets the price track the reality.

The client holds the estimation risk under time-and-materials, and that is a fair trade only if you hold your end of the deal. You owe the client transparency in return for the risk they carry. You log the hours honestly, you surface the estimate changes as the scope resolves, you tell them the moment the project is drifting past the budget range they approved, and you never pad the time to make the invoice more comfortable. Time-and-materials will ruin you faster than fixed-fee if your timecards lie, because the client is paying for your honesty directly. The mechanism is only fair when the billing is.

The failure mode on this side is the opposite of scope drift. It is the runaway, the engagement with no top and no feedback, where the client keeps paying and the work keeps extending because nobody ever re-anchored the estimate. Time-and-materials needs a budget guardrail: an agreed range and a checkpoint where you stop, restate what you have learned, and get a fresh decision before spending more. That guardrail is what turns an open-ended T&M into a controlled one, and a controlled T&M is a professional product, not a blank check.

## The units you price come from your real cost, not a market guess

However you price, the number has to come from your own unit economy, and this is where Hermes Agent makes the lesson concrete. Under time-and-materials your unit is the hour, and the honest hourly rate is the one that covers your real cost per hour with margin. For an agent delivery, part of that cost is the inference: the model, the provider, the tokens, the scheduled runs. Hermes records what a scheduled run did, how long it took, and what it cost, so the per-run unit economy is a number you read from the log, not a number you estimate. [^1] That run record is the raw material of a defensible unit price.

The pricing-honesty habit this creates is the one from Lesson 8: you learn the unit economics in the pilot, where it is cheapest, before you have to defend a price. The pilot's run log tells you the actual per-run cost of the recurring layer, and you price that layer to cover the real cost with margin instead of guessing a number that will come back as a loss at volume. [^1] The consultant who prices an agent engagement without knowing the per-run cost is pricing the same way the fixed-fee gambler estimates without a critical path: from the air.

Provider choice is part of the same discipline. Hermes routes through multiple providers and models, and each has its own pricing. [^2] A model that is twice the quality on some tasks is also commonly several times the price per token, and the consultant who ignores the difference is building cost into the delivery that the client will not thank them for. You choose the cheapest model that holds the quality bar, you pin it, and you price the delivery on the unit economy of the actual pinned configuration. [^2] [^3] The price you quote is a claim about your cost structure, and a claim about cost structure is only honest when it is built from the real numbers.

## The hybrid is not a compromise, it is a third instrument

The two mechanisms are often presented as a choice, and for a single simple engagement they are. For the multi-week deliveries this course teaches, the professional answer is frequently a hybrid, and the hybrid is not a muddy middle, it is a third instrument with its own logic. The logic is simple: the part of the work you know is priced as a fixed-fee, and the part you do not know is priced as time-and-materials, and you put a visible seam between them.

The recurring layer from Lesson 6 is the natural fixed-fee candidate. You proved its unit economics in the pilot, you know what it costs per run and per week, and it recurs predictably. You price it as a fixed recurring number, a subscription or a standing rate, because it is the part you can name. [^1] The build that gets you there is the natural time-and-materials candidate when the client's environment is complex and the integration is exploratory, because it is the part where the surprises from Lesson 5 live. You run it at a rate until the scope resolves, then you re-anchor as the knowledge firms up.

There is a cleaner hybrid shape that deserves its own name: the fixed-fee with a cap on a T&M tail. You price the known core as a fixed number, you agree that discovery and integration run at a rate, and you cap the T&M portion at a ceiling so the client holds a bounded worst case. That cap is the best of both worlds. The client gets a predictable budget, because the total cannot exceed the fixed core plus the cap, and you get protected against the exploratory work you could not price, because the uncapped part of the estimate does not sit on you. The cap does the risk-management work that the Lesson 6 buffer does, but it puts the residual risk on the client, which is fair when the residual is the genuinely unknowable part.

![The hybrid prices the known core as a fixed number, runs discovery and integration at a rate, and caps the time-and-materials tail at a ceiling so the client holds a bounded worst case, with a visible seam between the fixed and rate portions](</diagrams/pricing-engagements-fixed-fee-tm/2-hybrid-fixed-fee-with-cap.png>)

The skill that makes any hybrid work is the visible seam. Whoever carries the risk on each portion, both sides must be able to see which portion is which, what the fixed number covers, what the rate covers, and where the cap sits. A hybrid with a hidden seam is a fixed-fee in disguise that the client thinks is a T&M, or the reverse, and either way it breeds the exact dispute the contract exists to prevent.

## Change control is what keeps the chosen mechanism honest

Every mechanism in this lesson fails the same way, and the failure is not the mechanism, it is the missing change control. A fixed-fee scope creeps over the boundary and eats the margin. A T&M engagement drifts past the approved range because nobody re-anchored. Both failures are the same underlying gap: the scope moved and the price did not move with it. Change control is the discipline that closes that gap, and it is inseparable from pricing.

Change control is a written rule, agreed before the work starts, about what happens when the scope changes. It names the boundary: the deliverable the fixed price covers, in terms specific enough that both sides can point at a requested addition and say "that is over the line" or "that is in." It names the price of crossing: a fixed-fee addition is a change order at a defined rate, a T&M addition is re-approved against the budget range. And it names the process: the request, the estimate, the client's written approval, then the work. No approval, no work, no ambiguity.

![Change control is a written rule agreed before the work starts: the client requests an addition, you estimate the price of crossing, the client approves in writing, and only then does the work begin, so no approval means no work and no ambiguity](</diagrams/pricing-engagements-fixed-fee-tm/3-change-control-flow.png>)

The reason change control is a pricing skill and not a scope skill is that the price of the engagement is only as stable as the change control that defends it. A consultant who prices a fixed-fee with discipline but then accepts every addition silently has not priced a fixed-fee, they have priced an open-ended T&M at a fixed-fee rate, which is the single fastest way to turn a profitable engagement into a loss. The mechanism you chose is only as real as the boundary you enforce.

## Worked example: pricing the logistics agent

Bring it together on the logistics engagement you have sized and proven through the course. The client wants an agent that clears the routine third of a support queue. In Lesson 8 the pilot proved the recurring value: the agent clears the routine tickets at the quality bar, in a fraction of the human time, at a measured per-run cost. In Lesson 6 you estimated the build along a critical path through the complex environment, with the integration and the incompressible gates priced in. Now you put a price on the whole thing.

You start with the unit economy, read from the pilot's run records. [^1] You know the per-run inference cost of the pinned model, the hours of review the recurring operation needs each week, and the through-processed first, the value the client's finance team will anchor to. [^1] [^3] That is the raw material of both prices.

Then you split the engagement at the seam that Lesson 6 drew. The build, the integration into the client's warehouse and approval systems, is the exploratory part. The client's environment is complex, the documented interfaces differed from the running ones in the pilot, and the integration work cannot be priced with certainty. That portion runs time-and-materials, at a rate built from your real cost per hour, with a budget range and a checkpoint at the point where the build re-anchors.

The recurring operation is the known part. The pilot proved its unit economics and its value, so you price it as a fixed recurring number: a monthly fee anchored to the weekly hours and runs it replaces, built so that it clears your real run cost with margin. [^1]

And you cap the T&M build. You price the known core of the integration as a fixed number, you agree the discovery runs at the rate, and you cap the rate portion at a ceiling so the client holds a bounded worst case while you stay protected against the integration surprises you cannot price. Both sides know which portion is which, where the cap sits, and what a change order costs. That is not a compromise. It is a delivery priced the way risk is actually distributed across it.

![The logistics agent splits at the seam: the exploratory build runs time-and-materials at a rate with a budget range and checkpoint, the recurring operation is priced as a fixed monthly fee, and the time-and-materials build is capped so the client holds a bounded worst case](</diagrams/pricing-engagements-fixed-fee-tm/4-worked-example-logistics-agent.png>)

## The pitfalls that destroy an engagement's price

**Picking the number from the market instead of your cost.** The price is a claim about your unit economy. Price the known parts from your real per-run and per-hour costs with margin, learned in the pilot, not from a guess.

**Using fixed-fee on work you cannot scope.** The test for fixed-fee is whether you genuinely know the work. Exploratory work priced as a fixed number is a gamble with your margin as the stake.

**Using T&M without a budget guardrail.** The client carries the risk, so you owe them transparency, a range, and a checkpoint. An unbounded T&M is a blank check no client should sign and no professional should imply.

**Letting scope drift past the boundary.** A fixed-fee is only fixed if the scope is fixed. Every addition that crosses the boundary becomes a change order, not a silent adjustment.

**Folding the change control into goodwill.** Accepting additions without re-pricing turns a disciplined fixed-fee into an open-ended T&M at a fixed rate. The mechanism is only as real as the boundary you enforce.

**Quoting a price that assumes a model choice you did not pin.** Different providers and models cost different amounts per token. Choose the cheapest that holds the quality bar, pin it, and price on the pinned configuration's real unit economy. [^2] [^3]

**Building a hybrid with hidden seams.** A hybrid works only when both sides can see which portion is fixed, which runs at a rate, and where the cap sits. A hidden seam breeds the exact dispute the contract exists to prevent.

## Try it

Take the multi-week delivery you sized in Lesson 6, the one with the estimated critical path and the recurring layer, and put a price on it.

1. Read the real unit economy: the per-run inference cost of the pinned model and provider, and the weekly hours the recurring operation needs. Write the per-week and per-run cost the monthly fee must clear. [^1] [^3]
2. Decide which portions of the build you genuinely know and which are exploratory. Justify why each portion carries its estimation risk on the side that can best manage it.
3. Write the change-control rule before the price: the scope boundary in specific terms, the price of crossing it, and the process from request to written approval to work.
4. Draft a fixed-fee price for the known core. Show the margin that covers the pessimistic end of the critical path and the explicit buffer from Lesson 6.
5. Draft a T&M rate for the exploratory portion, with a budget range and a re-anchor checkpoint.
6. Design a hybrid seam: the fixed core, the rate tail, the cap, and the visible line between them. State which risk each portion leaves on which side.

If you can hold all six, you are not picking a number, you are allocating risk with a mechanism and defending the boundary with change control. That is what it means to price an engagement as an intermediate practitioner.
