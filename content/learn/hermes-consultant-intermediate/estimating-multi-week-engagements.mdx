# Lesson 6: Estimating Multi-Week Engagements: Beyond the 30-45 Minute Task

The 30-45 minute task is the unit of execution. It is not the unit of estimation. You learned in the early lessons to scope every piece of work small enough that an agent, or a person, can execute it in a bounded window and report a clean result. That discipline is correct, and it is what makes a delivery run without stalling. But it produces a dangerous instinct when you face a multi-week engagement: the instinct to estimate the engagement the same way you estimate a task, by adding up the pieces. A four-week delivery is not four hundred excellent thirty-minute tasks, because the tasks are not independent. They depend on each other, they wait on other people, they hit reviews and change windows, and they get surprised. Add the pieces up and you get a number that looks precise and is wrong.

This lesson teaches the next step of the craft. It takes the engagement you learned to run in Lesson 1, inside the complex client environment you learned to land in Lesson 5, and shows you how to put a defensible number on it. You will learn how to break a large engagement into estimable pieces, why the estimate you defend is the critical path and not the total effort, how to place the constraints and reviews from Lesson 5 into the number, how to estimate the recurring layer separately from the build, and how to keep the whole thing honest when the environment changes the ground under you. Hermes Agent stays the concrete working example, and the skill transfers to any tool you ever run.

## The single-number trap

Here is the failure mode that this lesson exists to kill. A client asks how long the engagement takes. The consultant, confident and a little tired, says "about six weeks." That sentence is not an estimate. It is a guess wearing the clothes of one, because nothing behind it decomposes. Six weeks came out of the air, or out of the last similar engagement, or out of a number that sounded reasonable in the moment. None of those is a defensible estimate.

The 30-45 minute task discipline looked like it gave you the way out, and it only half did. If you scope the work into atomic tasks, you can point at the list and say "here is the work." The problem is that a good task list is not the same as a good estimate. A task list answers the question "what needs to happen." An estimate answers the question "how long does the longest chain of dependent work take, given the calendar, the constraints, and the chance of surprise." Those are different questions, and the second one is the one the client is asking.

So the correction is not to estimate more carefully at the task level. It is to stop estimating at the task level entirely and start estimating at the level of structure: workstreams, dependencies, the critical path, and the fixed-duration items that no amount of effort can compress. That shift is the whole lesson.

## Decompose into estimable pieces

The first move is to break the engagement into pieces you can actually size, which is a different decomposition from the one you use to execute. Execution decomposes into small tasks so each one is completable. Estimation decomposes into pieces so each one is estimable, and the two do not always line up.

A piece is estimable when three things are true. It is small enough that you can put a defensible range on it, meaning you can name the optimistic case, the nominal case, and the pessimistic case with some honesty. It is independent enough that its uncertainty does not silently cascade into the next piece, meaning you know what its output is and what consumes it. And it has a definition of done, meaning you know the exact point at which the piece is finished and the next piece can start. If a piece fails any of those three, split it again.

The discipline of independence matters most, because this is where parallelism enters the estimate. Hermes Agent's delegation is the concrete example. The agent orchestration toolset includes `delegate_task`, which spawns subagents in isolated contexts, each with its own conversation and terminal session. [^1] [^2] Independent workstreams can be delegated and run in parallel. So when you decompose, you are not just listing tasks. You are also discovering which pieces are genuinely independent and can run at the same time, and which ones are chained and have to wait.

That discovery changes your estimate, but not the way most people assume. Independent pieces running in parallel do not add their times together. They change the shape of the number from a sum into a longest-path problem, and that is the subject of the next section.

## Estimate the critical path, not the total

Here is the single biggest correction in this lesson, and it is the one that separates the intermediate estimator from the beginner. The estimate for a multi-week delivery is the length of the critical path, the longest chain of dependent work from start to finish. It is not the sum of all the effort. When you sum everything, you count the parallel work twice, or three times, and you produce a number that has no relation to how long the engagement actually takes.

Work through the logic with the delegation example. Say the engagement decomposes into three independent workstreams, each of which takes five days of focused work, plus one integration task at the end that takes three days and consumes all three. The total effort is eighteen days. But the wall-clock estimate is eight: the three workstreams run in parallel for five days, then the integration runs for three. Summing to eighteen would be a category error. The parallel delegation exists precisely so that the wall clock tracks the longest chain, not the total. [^1] [^2]

So the estimator's job is to map the dependency graph, find the longest chain, and estimate that chain. Every task that is off the critical path still has to happen, and its effort still has to be paid for and scheduled, but it does not set the end date. You schedule it, you staff it, and you do not let it extend the number unless it becomes part of the longest chain.

This is where three-point estimation earns its place, and it belongs on the critical path, not on every task. For each piece on the longest chain, you estimate three numbers: the optimistic case, if nothing goes wrong; the nominal case, if the usual minor frictions happen; and the pessimistic case, if the environment fights back the way Lesson 5 taught you it can. Then you aggregate the pessimistic numbers along the critical path, because that is the honest picture of how long the chain runs when the surprises that actually happen do not cancel out.

Why pessimistic, and why along the critical path? Because the surprises on a real engagement do not cancel. On the critical path they compound. An integration delay pushes everything after it, and the pessimistic case of an early task becomes the starting condition of the next. Aggregating the nominals produces the number you hope for. Aggregating the pessimistics produces the number you can actually hit, which is what a client can build a plan on. You present a range, nominal to pessimistic on the critical path, and you put your confidence behind the defensible end of it, not the hopeful one.

## Constraints and reviews are fixed-duration items

The second correction takes the Lesson 5 material and forces it into the number. Lesson 5 taught you to treat enterprise constraints, security review, change windows, approval chains, as requirements with owners. Here is what that means for estimation: those items are fixed-duration, incompressible calendar events, and they sit on the critical path.

A security review takes as long as the security team says it takes, and it cannot be shortened by adding people or parallelizing. A change window is a four-hour slot once a week, and the deployment waits for the slot. An approval chain routes a decision through three people, each of whom has their own calendar, and no amount of effort compresses the human latency. These are not tasks you execute. They are gates you wait for, and the waiting is part of the estimate.

This is the second biggest reason estimates slip, after summing instead of finding the critical path. The consultant sizes the build work accurately, the build finishes on time, and the engagement still runs late because the deployment sat in a queue waiting for the change window that comes once a week. The fix is structural. When you map the critical path, you put each constraint and review on it as a first-class item with a duration and an owner, exactly as Lesson 5 told you to write them down. You do not hide the security review inside a build task, because then the build task looks bigger than it is and the review still happens at the end. You surface it. A constraint you can see on the critical path is one you can account for. A constraint you hide is one that surprises you at delivery.

There is a corollary that matters for client communication. You never quote a delivery date that assumes a constraint will bend, because a constraint with an owner does not bend for your convenience. If the change window is weekly, the estimate lands on a weekly boundary. If the security review needs sign-off from a specific team, you get that team's calendar into the plan before you promise a date. The number you defend is the number that includes the gates, because those are the dates that are not yours to move.

## The recurring layer is a rate, not a build

A multi-week engagement is rarely only a build. There is the delivery, and then there is the ongoing thing: the maintenance, the support, the recurring automation that keeps running after handover. Estimating these two is not the same act, and confusing them is a common source of both under-pricing and overruns.

The build is a one-time effort, a pile of work that finishes. It gets a total. The recurring layer is a rate, an amount of work per week that sustains. It gets a weekly number and a run cost, and it never finishes. When you quote a multi-week engagement, you quote the build as a fixed delivery and the recurring layer as an ongoing commitment, and you keep the two numbers separate so the client can decide what to run and for how long.

Hermes Agent is a concrete model of this split. Its cron system schedules recurring jobs, and the no-agent mode runs a script on a schedule with zero LLM involvement and its output delivered verbatim. [^3] That is the shape of the recurring layer you are estimating: work that runs on a cadence, costs a bounded amount per run, and produces output someone reviews. The estimating question is not "how long does it take to build once." It is "how many hours a week, and how much per run, does the sustained operation cost," and you size the recurring automation accordingly. [^3] [^4]

The cost discipline belongs in the same place, because a recurring layer that runs unattended is where spend quietly grows. Hermes applies a model and provider drift guard to recurring jobs by default, so a job that was set up on a cheap model does not silently inherit a switch to a paid one; you pin the model and provider deliberately to control the run cost. [^4] That is the estimation-of-ongoing-cost habit: you do not just predict the weekly hours, you pin the inputs that keep the running cost where the client signed off, and you put the run cost in the proposal as an ongoing line rather than letting it become a surprise on an invoice.

## Buffer, and honesty under surprise

Every estimate that survives contact with a real engagement carries a buffer, and the skill is how you carry it. The honest way is explicit. You estimate the critical path, you add a named buffer line for the uncertainty you cannot foresee, and you show both to the client. Hidden padding is worse than no padding, because hidden padding turns into a number nobody can explain, and the first time the client asks why a line grew, the estimate loses credibility. Explicit buffer is a line the client can see and question, and an explicit line holds up under scrutiny better than a padded total.

The second half of honesty is re-estimation. An estimate is a claim you made at a point in time, not a contract with reality. When the environment reveals a surprise, the lesson 5 world in particular, you do not quietly slide the end date. You re-run the three-point on the affected critical path, you state what changed and why, and you show the new number. Lesson 1 gave you the gate and the slippage rule for this; the same discipline applies to the estimate itself. The surprise that stays hidden is the one that becomes a missed date. The surprise you surface immediately becomes a re-estimate, which is a thing a professional does and a client accepts.

This is the difference between an estimate and a number that got you in trouble. The estimate has a decomposition behind it, so when it changes you can say exactly which chain moved and by how much. The guess has nothing behind it, so when it fails it fails silently and completely. The whole of this lesson is building the thing behind the number.

## Worked example: sizing a multi-week agent delivery

Bring it together on the logistics engagement you have followed through Lessons 1 through 5. The client wants an agent that clears the routine third of a support queue, and the engagement is a build plus ongoing operation. Your job is to put a defensible number on it.

You start by decomposing. You map three independent workstreams. The capture workstream, from Lesson 2, records the enacted process, the rule, the exception, and the boundary. The eval workstream, from Lesson 3, builds the gold set the output is proven against. The integration workstream builds the connection to the warehouse data and the approval tool the client insists on. Each of the three is estimable: small enough to range, independent enough that its uncertainty does not cascade, and with a clear definition of done. Because they are independent, Hermes delegation runs them as parallel subagents, so their wall clock is the longest of the three, not their sum. [^1] [^2]

Then you find the critical path. The three workstreams land in five days, then the integration task consumes all three and takes three days, and the end of the integration is where the sandboxed deploy from Lesson 5 begins. That deploy runs on the constraint calendar: a security review that takes one week of sign-off, and a change window that opens once a week for four hours. Those gates sit on the critical path and are not compressible. So the longest chain reads: five days of parallel build, three days of integration, one week of security review, and the next weekly change window to ship. That chain, not the sum of all the effort, is your delivery estimate.

You range it. The nominal chain is about two and a half weeks of calendar. The pessimistic chain, which is what you defend, assumes the integration hits one of the environment surprises Lesson 5 described, the documented interface differing from the running one, and adds a week of rework before the review can even start. Your defensible range is nominal to pessimistic on that critical path, and you present the pessimistic end as the date you can hit, because the pessimistic chain is the one that holds when the environment fights back.

Then you split off the recurring layer. The ongoing operation is a cron-driven job that runs on a schedule and posts its output for review, sized as so many hours a week of review and so much per run in inference cost, with the model pinned so the run cost does not drift. [^3] [^4] That is a separate line in the proposal: a weekly rate and a run budget, not part of the one-time build number. [^4]

And you carry an explicit buffer line on the critical path for the surprises you cannot name yet, and you commit to surfacing and re-estimating when one lands rather than sliding the date silently. What you hand the client is a defensible number with a decomposition behind it: the parallel workstreams, the integration chain, the two incompressible gates, the recurring rate, and the buffer. That is an estimate, and it is the difference between a six-week guess and a number a client can build a plan on.

## The skill that transfers: estimate structure, not tasks

Run the whole method together and one pattern emerges, and it is the transferable skill this lesson is really teaching. It applies whether the tool is Hermes Agent, a different agent platform, or no agent at all, and it has five steps.

**Decompose into estimable pieces.** Split the engagement into workstreams that are small enough to range, independent enough not to cascade, and defined enough to know when each is done. Execution decomposition is for completing. Estimation decomposition is for sizing, and the independence you find here is what enables parallelism.

**Map the dependency graph and find the critical path.** Independent workstreams run in parallel, so the wall clock is the longest chain of dependent work, not the sum of effort. Summing double counts the parallel work and produces a number unrelated to the schedule.

**Put the constraints and reviews on the path.** Security review, change windows, approval chains are fixed-duration, incompressible calendar items with owners. Surface them as first-class items on the critical path, and never promise a date that assumes a gate will bend.

**Range the critical path, and defend the pessimistic end.** Three-point each piece on the longest chain and aggregate the pessimistics, because surprises on a real engagement compound rather than cancel. Present nominal to pessimistic, and stand behind the number you can hit.

**Split the recurring layer and price it as a rate.** The build is a one-time total. The ongoing operation is a weekly rate and a run cost. Keep the two separate, and pin the inputs that keep the running cost where the client signed off.

This is the difference between the consultant who estimates work and the one who estimates a delivery. The first adds up tasks and produces a number that fails under surprise. The second maps structure, finds the chain that sets the end date, prices the gates, ranges the risk, and hands over a number with a decomposition behind it, a number that stays honest when the world moves.

## The pitfalls that break a multi-week estimate

**Adding up the tasks.** Summing the effort double counts parallel work and ignores dependencies. The estimate is the longest chain of dependent work, not the total.

**Estimating the average instead of the deliverable.** Aggregating nominal cases produces the number you hope for. Aggregating the pessimistic critical path produces the number you can hit, which is what a client builds on.

**Hiding the gates in the build.** A security review or change window hidden inside a build task both inflates the build and still happens at the end. Surface each constraint and review as its own line on the critical path.

**Quoting a date that assumes a constraint bends.** A gate with an owner does not move for your convenience. Land the estimate on the constraint calendar, not on the calendar you wish you had.

**Folding the recurring layer into the build.** The ongoing operation is a rate and a run cost, not part of the one-time total. Keep the numbers separate, or the client cannot decide what to run and for how long.

**Hiding the buffer.** Padding a total into an unexplainable number is worse than no padding. Carry an explicit buffer line and show it, so it holds up when questioned.

**Letting a surprise slide the date silently.** The hidden surprise is the missed date. The surfaced surprise becomes a re-estimate, which is what a professional does.

## Try it

Take the production agent you built in Lesson 3, running inside the complex client environment from Lesson 5, and size a multi-week delivery for it.

1. Decompose the engagement into estimable workstreams. For each, write the optimistic, nominal, and pessimistic range, and state why it is independent enough not to cascade.
2. Draw the dependency graph and identify the critical path. Explain why the estimate is that chain and not the sum of the workstreams.
3. Add at least two incompressible constraints or reviews to the path, each with a duration and an owner, and show how they move the end date.
4. Range the critical path: aggregate the pessimistic chain and write the nominal-to-pessimistic range you would present.
5. Split off the recurring layer. Write the weekly rate and the per-run cost, and name the inputs you would pin to keep the run cost stable. [^3] [^4]
6. Add an explicit buffer line, and write the re-estimation rule you will follow when a surprise lands on the critical path.

If you can defend all six, you are no longer estimating tasks. You are estimating a delivery: mapping the structure that sets the end date, pricing the gates, ranging the risk, and standing behind a number that survives the environment. That is where the intermediate estimator earns their keep.
