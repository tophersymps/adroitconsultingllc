# Lesson 19: Writing Proposals and Statements of Work That Protect Both Sides

Lesson 18 taught you to price an engagement by allocating risk to the side that can best manage it. You chose fixed-fee or time-and-materials, you built the hybrid seam, and you committed to defending the boundary with change control. This lesson takes that decision and puts it on paper. The proposal and the statement of work are where the pricing reasoning you did in your head either becomes a contract both sides can enforce or evaporates into a vague document that no one can point to when the working relationship turns.

The framing mistake to discard up front is that a proposal is a sales document and an SOW is a legal formality. Both are risk documents. A proposal that reads like marketing has not done its job, because its job is not to get the client excited, it is to force the scope, the assumptions, the exclusions, and the acceptance tests out into the open where both sides can agree on them before work begins. An SOW that is a copy-paste of a template protects neither side, because the only protection that works in a dispute is specificity, and templates are specifically vague. This lesson teaches you to write both as precise risk allocations on paper, with the transferable skill being the drafting discipline, which applies to any professional engagement, agent delivery or otherwise.

## The proposal opens the deal; the SOW closes it

A useful mental model is that the proposal and the SOW are two passes over the same material, at different degrees of commitment. The proposal is where you state the problem as you understand it, the approach you will take, the deliverable you will produce, the price, and the assumptions your price depends on. It is the document you send when you are asking the client to decide whether to move forward. The statement of work is the tighter version: the specific deliverables with their acceptance criteria, the exclusions, the assumptions restated as conditions, the schedule, the payment terms, and the change control. It is the document both sides sign that governs the actual work.

The intermediate practitioner does not write them back to back as if they were the same thing. The proposal is where you surface every assumption and every exclusion that could later become a dispute, precisely so the client can push back on the ones they disagree with while it is cheap. The SOW then locks in the agreed versions. The discipline is that nothing material may live in one and vanish from the other. If the proposal says the pilot's integration into the client's warehouse system is exploratory and time-based, the SOW must say that integration runs at the rate with a cap and a checkpoint. If it does not, you have left a seam that a later argument will tear open, and it will tear open on the side that did not write it down.

## The scope boundary is the heart of the document

Every protection in the SOW depends on one thing: a scope statement specific enough that a stranger to the engagement can look at a requested addition and say it is in or it is out. A scope that says "deliver an agent that improves the support workflow" protects nothing, because the words can be stretched to cover almost any addition. A scope that says "deliver a scheduled workflow agent that clears the routine third of the support queue by applying the decision rules agreed in the discovery register and posting a variance report for human approval, against the acceptance criteria in Section 4" is a boundary both sides can actually enforce.

The raw material for this specificity is the as-built register you built in Lesson 2. That register holds the enacted process, the rules, the exceptions, and the boundaries in writing, and it is precisely the level of detail a scope line needs. When you write the scope, you are not inventing new precision, you are lifting the precision you already captured and stating that the deliverable is defined by it. [^1] The scope section also carries the counterpart that practitioners often skip: the explicit out-of-scope list. Naming what you will not do is more powerful than naming what you will, because the client cannot claim an omission they were warned about. "This engagement does not include re-engineering the existing warehouse integration, migrating historical ticket data, or training staff beyond a single handover session" is a fence, and the fence is what the fixed-fee relies on once scope creep starts to bite.

## Acceptance criteria convert the deliverable into a testable bar

The single most skipped section of an agent SOW is the acceptance criteria, and it is the section that does the most protecting. An acceptance criterion is a test, not a description. "The agent should be accurate" is a description and is worth nothing in a dispute because accuracy is in the eye of the party arguing. "The agent clears at least 90 percent of the routine tickets in the golden set without human intervention, and every ticket it does not clear is passed to a human reviewer" is a test, because you can run the golden set and count.

You already built this test in Lesson 9. The golden set you assembled, the eval gate you scheduled to run every case through the current system and report pass or fail, is the exact mechanical instrument an acceptance criterion needs. In Hermes, a scheduled eval job can load the golden set, run each case through the current system, and produce a pass or fail per case, and in no-agent mode a deterministic script can score the mechanically checkable cases for free. [^2] That means you can write acceptance criteria that are not wishes but executable checks: the agent produces the report in the agreed format, on the agreed schedule, within the agreed tolerance of the human-approved standard, and the eval gate passes on the agreed portion of the golden set. When the acceptance criterion in the SOW points at a test you can actually run, both sides know what "done" means, and that knowledge is most of the protection.

## Assumptions and exclusions are the second protection

The proposal and SOW both need a section that states the conditions your price and scope quietly assume. These are the factual premises you are relying on: that the client provides access to the warehouse and the approval system within a stated number of business days, that the pinned model and provider, and their costs, stay as they were priced, that the integration interfaces are available to the working agent, and that the staff participate in the scheduled checkpoints. Every one of these is a place where reality can diverge from the price, and the SOW's assumptions section is where you say so and make the dependence explicit.

The discipline is symmetrical: every assumption is a risk you are refusing to carry silently. If the price and the schedule depend on the interfaces being reachable, write that they are reachable as an assumption, and state that if they are not, the effect is a change order under Section on change control. Same for the exclusions: the out-of-scope list from the scope section and the assumptions section are cousins. The scope names what is not included, and the assumptions name what you are relying on. Both are ways of saying the same thing: the boundary, in the two forms a dispute actually takes.

## The SOW is the reference document change control runs on

Lesson 18 ended on change control being what keeps a pricing mechanism honest. The SOW is where that change control lives, and here the discipline becomes concrete and legal. The SOW's change-control clause has to name the trigger, the price of crossing, the process, and the approval. It names the boundary because the scope section already defined the deliverable in the specific terms that let you and the client point at an addition and classify it. It names the cost, that an in-scope change is handled within the work, and that an out-of-scope addition is a change order at the agreed rate, and that the rate and the process re-anchor against the budget range for the time portion. And it names the workflow: the written request, the estimate, the client's written approval, then the work, with no approval meaning no work and no ambiguity.

The clause protects both sides, and that symmetry is worth making explicit to the client rather than hiding it. It protects you, because an out-of-scope request no longer eats your fixed margin, and it protects the client, because a change to an in-scope deliverable is handled within the agreed price rather than billed after the fact. Change control that is fair and written is not an adversarial device. It is the mechanism that lets both sides say yes without fear, and the client who reads a fair change-control clause and objects to it is a client who was planning to move the boundary, which is exactly the information you want before you sign.

## Payment terms and the seam to the price

The SOW turns the pricing decision from Lesson 18 into the payment terms. The fixed core is billed as the agreed fixed price on the agreed milestones, and the time-and-materials portion is billed against the agreed rate with the agreed budget range and the checkpoint that re-anchors it. The hybrid seam from Lesson 18, the fixed core, the rate tail, the cap, has to appear on paper exactly as you priced it, because the payment terms are where the risk allocation becomes money changing hands. A payment schedule that does not match the risk allocation you chose is a second, conflicting contract hiding inside your contract, and it will win the argument.

The agent-specific detail belongs here because it is part of the unit economy the price runs on. The recurring layer is priced on the real per-run cost of the pinned model and provider, and the SOW should say that the fee assumes that the model and provider, and their cost, stay as pinned, and that a change to either is a change under the change-control clause. [^3] The run record from Hermes, which notes what a scheduled run did and what it cost, is the evidence both sides agree to use if the recurring cost is disputed. [^2] You are not putting a liability on the client, you are stating the factual basis of the price so that neither side can later argue from a different assumption.

## Worked example: the support-queue agent hits paper

Take the support-queue engagement you priced in Lesson 18 and write the SOW around it, because the worked example is where the drafting discipline stops being abstract. You start by lifting the scope from the as-built register: you deliver a scheduled workflow agent that clears the routine third of the support queue by applying the discovery rules and posting a variance report for human approval, defined by the as-built register and subject to the acceptance criteria. You state the out-of-scope list: no re-engineering of the existing integration, no migration of historical tickets, no staff training beyond a handover session.

Then you write the acceptance criteria as tests, not descriptions. The agent clears at least 90 percent of the routine tickets in the golden set without human intervention. The variance report is produced in the agreed format on the scheduled weekday run. The eval gate passes on the agreed portion of the golden set, scored in no-agent mode for the mechanically checkable cases. [^2] Every criterion points at a test you already built in Lesson 9, so "done" is a countable fact, not an argument.

You state the assumptions the price rests on: the client provides warehouse and approval-system access within five business days of signing, the interfaces remain reachable, the pinned model and provider and their cost stay as agreed, and the staff attend the checkpoints. You write the change-control clause with the trigger, the rate, the process, and the approval, and you note that a swap of the pinned model, of the kind Lesson 12 taught you to treat as high-risk change, is a change under this clause rather than a silent adjustment. You set the payment schedule to match the hybrid: the fixed core billed at the milestones, the time-based discovery and integration billed against the agreed range with the re-anchor checkpoint, the whole capped as you priced it.

Because the SOW points at tests that run and registers that define the scope, both sides sign the same document in their heads. That is the entire point. A signed SOW where the client thinks "done" means one thing and you think it means another is not a contract, it is a launchpad for a dispute. The document you wrote closes that gap before work starts.

## The pitfalls that turn a signed document into a fight

**Writing the scope in adjectives.** A scope that says "improve the workflow" is not a boundary, it is an invitation. Write the scope from the as-built register in terms specific enough that a stranger can classify any request as in or out.

**Collapsing acceptance into a vibe.** "The agent will be accurate" sounds fine and enforces nothing. Turn every acceptance criterion into a test you can run, ideally the golden set you already built, and state the pass bar as a number. [^2]

**Skipping the out-of-scope list.** Naming what you will not do is a fence; leaving it out invites the client to assume the delivery includes it. State the exclusions and the assumptions both sides rely on, and neither side is surprised later.

**Leaving the price assumptions silent.** The fee assumes the model, the provider, and their cost stay pinned. If you do not write that assumption down, a swap that moves the cost is an unstated basis of a price you now have to defend. State it and route it through change control. [^3]

**Letting payment terms contradict the pricing.** A payment schedule that does not match the hybrid seam from Lesson 18 is a second contract that will win over the first. Make the money match the risk allocation you chose.

**Treating change control as adversarial.** A fair, written change clause protects both sides, and a client who objects to it is showing you their intent. Keep it visible, and see the objection as information.

## Try it

Take the multi-week delivery you priced in Lesson 18, the one with the fixed core, the rate tail, the cap, and the change-control rule, and put the whole decision on paper.

1. Write the scope line from the as-built register, specific enough that a stranger can classify a requested addition as in or out.
2. Write the out-of-scope list and the assumptions section. State the factual conditions the price relies on, the model, the provider, the cost, the access, the participation.
3. Convert every deliverable into acceptance criteria that are tests, not descriptions, and point each one at the golden set or eval you can actually run.
4. Draft the change-control clause: the trigger, the rate, the process, and the approval. State plainly what happens when the pinned model swaps.
5. Set the payment schedule so it matches the hybrid seam you priced in Lesson 18. Confirm the money matches the risk allocation.
6. Cross-check the proposal against the SOW. Confirm nothing material lives in one and vanishes from the other.

If you can hold all six, you have taken the price and the risk allocation from Lesson 18 and made them enforceable by both sides. That is the difference between pricing an engagement well and protecting it, which is what a signed document is actually for.

## What's next

You can now write a proposal and statement of work that protect both sides: a scope specific enough to enforce, acceptance criteria the client can actually test, assumptions and exclusions stated so neither side is surprised, and change control that keeps a signed SOW honest. But on paper is not the same as in practice. The next lesson takes the protection you just wrote down and defends it when the work itself turns. It teaches managing scope creep and difficult clients: how you use the change-control clause you drafted, how you hold the boundary when a client keeps asking for more, how you handle a client who is unhappy, adversarial, or stalling on payment, and how you keep the engagement from becoming a fight that the written terms alone cannot win. Where this lesson made the boundary enforceable, the next one makes it survivable.
