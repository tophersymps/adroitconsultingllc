# Lesson 13: Handover That Makes the Client Self-Sufficient: Operating Docs and Runbooks

Lesson 12 made you a safe pair of hands on a live system. You can change a running agent deliberately and keep the client's trust while you do it. Every change added to a body of knowledge about how the system works, and that knowledge currently lives in your head. This lesson is about getting it out. The moment that decides whether a delivery succeeded is not the demo where it works while you narrate. It is the first Tuesday morning after you leave, when the client's operator opens the system, finds the Monday run never posted its report, and has to decide what to do with nobody to call but you. If that person recovers on their own, the handover worked. If they panic and call you, it did not, no matter how well the build went.

The hard truth this lesson rests on is that a consultant who leaves a client able to depend on the agent but not able to run it has not delivered a system. They have delivered a dependency on themselves, which is a liability dressed up as a success. The deliverable is not the agent. It is the agent plus the knowledge of how to operate it, transferred so completely that your continued presence becomes optional. Making the client self-sufficient is the transferable skill this lesson teaches, with Hermes Agent as the concrete vehicle for how it looks in practice.

## What handover means: files are not ownership

The word "handover" covers two very different things, and confusing them is where most handovers fail. The first is the file handover: you produce the artifacts, the config, the prompts, the skills, the eval suite, you hand them over, and you call it done. The client has everything you created. The second is the ownership handover: you transfer the ability to run, change, and recover the system, so that a named person on the client side can do all three without you in the room. File handover is easy and it is not the job. Ownership handover is the job, and it is much harder, because files only become operable knowledge when someone can act on them.

The failure mode that makes the difference vivid is the accurate, complete, useless document. A consultant finishes a delivery, writes a description of what they built, and leaves. The document describes the system the way the consultant sees it, from the inside, with all the tacit knowledge they no longer notice they have. The client's operator reads it and cannot answer the three questions that actually come up in week one: where does this thing live, what does a healthy run look like, and what do I do when it does not happen. The document passes every test the consultant could think to give it except the only one that matters, whether a stranger can use it.

The consulting implication is that handover is not the last hour of the build. It is a deliverable with its own scope, its own effort, and its own acceptance test, planned into the engagement like any other phase. If you leave it to the last day, you will hand over files and call it done, because there will not be time for the part that works.

## Two artifacts, two jobs: operating docs and runbooks

The knowledge a self-sufficient client needs splits into two artifacts, and conflating them is a common mistake. Each answers a different question, and each is read at a different moment under a different level of pressure.

The operating doc is reference. It answers "how is this system put together and configured right now?" It is the description of current state: what the agent does, which environment it runs in, what it is configured to run on, what scheduled work it performs, what data it reads, what limits it, where its credentials live, and how you change a rule. You read it when you need to understand the system or make a deliberate change. It is written calmly, read calmly, and it is where you go when nothing is on fire.

The runbook is procedure. It answers "something happened, what do I do now?" It is a set of ordered recovery procedures for the failures that will recur, written so a person who has never seen the system can execute them under pressure. An entry names the symptom, lists the likely causes, gives the steps in order with the expected result of each, and says what to do when a step fails and when to escalate. It is written calmly but read in a hurry by someone whose hands are a little shaky, so it has to be executable literally, without requiring the reader to improvise or to understand why each step works.

The two artifacts fail in opposite directions. An operating doc without a runbook tells the operator what the system is but leaves them staring at it when it breaks, a map with no directions. A runbook without an operating doc gives them steps but no understanding, so the moment the procedure drifts from reality, because a step changed or an error looks different, they have nothing to fall back on. The self-sufficient handover delivers both, kept in step with each other and with the running system.

## Write for the operator who has never met you

The discipline that separates a real operating doc from a description the consultant wrote for themselves is harder than it sounds. When you document a system you built and ran, you write from inside your own knowledge and you leave out the parts that are obvious to you. You do not write down which environment the agent runs in, because you live in it. You do not state what a normal output looks like, because you recognize it instantly. You do not explain that the Monday run reads the staging export rather than the live one, because you have not thought about it since you configured it. Every silence is a hole in the document, and the holes are exactly what the person inheriting the system needs most, because they are the things they cannot infer.

The fix is to write for a specific reader who does not exist yet: the operator who takes over cold, with no memory of the build and no context beyond what you leave. That person knows how to use the tooling but nothing about this delivery. So you are explicit about what feels obvious: you name the environment, the command to check a job's status, the schedule, the shape of a good output. You define your terms instead of assuming the client shares your internal vocabulary. The operating doc that reads as slightly over-explained to you, because you already know all of it, is usually exactly right for the person who has to act on it.

The proof that you got this right is not reading the doc yourself and finding it clear. You will always find it clear, because you wrote it. The proof is that a stranger can execute it while you stay silent. Until you have watched an unbriefed person run the recovery procedure from your runbook without asking you a question, you have not written the runbook. You have written a diary.

## The operating doc: what a running agent's reference must capture

An agent delivery is not a single file. It is a running estate of pieces, and the operating doc is the map of that estate. In Hermes terms, and this generalizes to any agent platform you deliver on, the map has to answer a specific set of questions, each easy to leave out precisely because it is structural.

The first question is where the system lives. Hermes isolates an agent into a profile, its own home directory holding its own config, environment, memory, skills, and scheduled jobs, separate from every other agent on the machine. [^1] If you run one client's delivery per profile, the single most useful fact in the whole operating doc is which profile the delivery runs in and what that separation protects. Without it, the inheriting operator does not even know where to start looking, which is the difference between a fix and a search.

The second question is what scheduled work the agent does and what it depends on. A delivery that runs itself, the Monday reconciliation job from earlier lessons, is a scheduled task with real configuration. In Hermes, that job resolves its model and provider at fire time from its own pin, and the pin decides what the job actually runs against. [^2] The operating doc records each scheduled job: what it does, when it fires, what it needs to succeed, what it is pinned to, and where it delivers. It also records the dependency, because the job needs its provider key to resolve, its attached skills to be ready, and its delivery target to be known. The platform checks all of this before a run and refuses to spend tokens on a job that is not configured to succeed. [^3] An operator who understands that will not ask why a blocked job did nothing. They will check the configuration the doc told them to look at.

The third question is the guardrail set. The agent is not unconstrained, and the constraints are why the client can trust it. Hermes layers its safety model, including user-defined deny rules that block matching commands unconditionally, the floor that holds even when other safety is relaxed, [^4] and a default that scheduled jobs deny dangerous commands rather than approving them. [^5] The operating doc records the guardrails as configured, because they are part of how the system behaves. When a future operator wonders why the agent refused to do something, the answer is in the guardrail record, not in a theory about the model.

The fourth question is what procedural knowledge the agent carries and where it lives. Much of what makes an agent useful is not in the model. It is in the packaged skills it loads, the encoded rules for the client's domain. Those skills live in the profile's skill directory, the source of truth, and the ones you wrote or modified are yours: the platform never silently overwrites or removes an edited skill during an update. [^6] The operating doc names the skills the delivery depends on and what each encodes, because a client who must change a reconciliation rule needs to know the rule lives in the skill, not in the prompt, and where that skill is on disk.

The fifth question is credentials and secrets, and the rule is precise: the operating doc records what exists and where it lives, never the value. The operator needs to know the delivery reads a warehouse export with a credential stored in the profile's environment, and who owns rotating it, so that when it expires they know what to refresh and whom to ask. A doc containing the actual secret turns a configuration note into a breach, so you document existence and owner and keep the value in the secret store the client controls.

The sixth question is what normal looks like. This is the one consultants omit most often and the one the operator needs most in a crisis. The operating doc describes a healthy run: the report that should arrive, its format, its cadence, the sign that it worked. It also records the eval or golden set from Lesson 9 that defines correct output, because that is the objective standard the operator compares a suspicious result against. Normal versus abnormal is not something a new operator can feel. It has to be written down.

Finally, the operating doc is a living record, which is the link back to Lesson 12. Every change you make, scoped, tested, and recorded the way Lesson 12 taught, must update the operating doc in the same step. The doc describes the running system as it is now. The moment it describes the system as it was two changes ago, it is worse than no doc at all, because the operator will trust it and act on the wrong state. The change record and the operating doc move together or both rot.

## The runbook: recovery procedures a stranger can run under pressure

Where the operating doc is the map, the runbook is the set of drills, and it is built from the failures that will actually recur, not the ones you imagine. You have run the delivery for months, so you have a private list of the ways it has gone wrong: the missed run, the expired credential, the rule that misfired, the model that drifted. That list is the runbook material, and the mistake is keeping it in your head. The discipline is to write each real failure as a procedure the client's operator can execute cold.

Every runbook entry has the same anatomy. It names the symptom precisely enough that the operator can match what they are seeing to the right entry. It lists the likely causes in the order to check them, most probable first. It gives the steps in exact order with the expected result of each, so the operator knows they are on the right path and can stop early when a step confirms the diagnosis. It says what to do if a step does not produce the expected result, because procedures fail and the operator needs a next move. And it states the escalation threshold plainly: the point where the operator has done the checks, the system still will not recover, and it is time to call support rather than keep improvising.

The runbook is where the platform's own failure handling becomes your ally, because the platform already distinguishes the reasons a run does not happen, and you can teach the operator to read those signals. Hermes is explicit that a scheduled run can fail to produce output in distinct ways: a job can be blocked because its configuration is invalid, a provider key can fail to resolve, a run can happen and fail, or it can happen and produce nothing it can deliver. [^3] A good runbook entry for "no report Monday morning" teaches the operator to check each in order, with a command and an expected result per check, so they walk a decision tree instead of guessing.

The deepest runbook material, though, is the wrong-but-plausible run. The report arrived, it reconciles to nothing, and it looks like a report. This is the failure that does not announce itself, and it is where a new operator is most likely to either miss it or panic. Lesson 11 taught you to establish ground truth before you patch. The runbook transfers that to the client: compare the suspicious output against the golden set, check whether the model or a rule changed recently, treat a wrong number that looks right as the highest-priority failure rather than the most comfortable one, and escalate when the output does not match the standard even if it looks plausible. The operator who has a written procedure for the quiet failure is the operator who catches it.

## The moment of truth: the dry run and the support taper

Writing the docs is necessary and not sufficient, because a document you wrote is unproven until someone else has followed it. The handover becomes real at the dry run, a deliberate scheduled event, not an afterthought. You take the runbook, you arrange a time when the client's named operator is available and you are present but silent, and you make the system fail in a controlled way, or you replay a real past incident, and you have the operator recover it using only the runbook. You do not answer questions. You watch. Where they stop, where they ask, where they make a wrong turn, those are the holes in your documentation, and you fix them afterward.

The first dry run almost always reveals holes, and that is the point of doing it before you leave rather than discovering them by phone call after you are gone. The operator will stop at the step you thought was obvious, because it was obvious only to you. They will read a procedure literally and miss the tacit step you never wrote. Every stop is a gift, because each one is a sentence you add that turns a future phone call into a future recovery. You run the drill until the operator completes it without a prompt from you, and that is your acceptance test: the handover passes when an unbriefed stranger can run the recovery procedure to a successful end while you say nothing.

Alongside the dry run, structure the exit as a taper, not a cliff. Handover is not a single day on which you vanish. It is a deliberate reduction of your presence on a schedule the client understands: a shadow period where the operator does the work and you catch mistakes, an on-call period where they run it and you are reachable but not watching, a review period where you only look at the record periodically, and a sunset where your access ends and further help becomes a separate contracted arrangement rather than an implicit promise. The taper converts "I am the only one who can run this" into "the process runs and I am optional," and it lets the client build confidence at each step before the safety net is removed.

Underneath the taper sits the single point of failure, and Lesson 12's bus-factor language applies here with force. Before you leave, you identify every way the delivery depends uniquely on you and you eliminate each one. The knowledge in your head becomes the operating doc. The recovery moves you know by instinct become the runbook. The credential only you can reach becomes one the client owns and can rotate. The environment only you know how to start becomes a documented, named one. Your departure should not be an incident, and the dry run plus the taper is how you prove that before the client has to live with it.

## Worked example: the reconciliation agent learns to run without you

Put the whole discipline on the engagement you have carried through the course, the weekly reconciliation agent whose changes the client's operations lead has been approving since Lesson 12. The build is stable and the engagement is ending. Your job now is to make the operations lead, who has watched you but never run it, self-sufficient.

You start with the operating doc, written for the operations lead reading it cold in six months. You record that the delivery runs in a dedicated profile, isolated from everything else on the machine, because that is the first thing anyone needs to find it. [^1] You record the Monday 7am reconciliation job: what it does, that it is pinned to the model and provider the client approved in Lesson 12, and that it delivers its variance report to the approval queue. [^2] You record the guardrails, including the deny rules that stop the agent from touching the production warehouse and the fact that the scheduled job denies dangerous commands by default, so the operations lead understands why the agent refuses rather than doing something risky. [^5] You record that the reconciliation rules the client keeps adjusting live in the packaged skill, not the prompt, and that an edited skill is never silently overwritten, so their changes are safe. [^6] You record the credential the agent uses to read the exports, that it sits in the profile's environment, and that the client's security team owns rotating it. And you record what a normal run looks like, the report that should arrive each Monday, its format and size, so the operations lead can recognize when something is off.

Then you write the runbooks from the failures you have actually seen. The first entry is the one you have handled a dozen times: Monday morning, no report. You write the decision tree in the order to check it, the job blocked on configuration, a credential that expired, a run that happened and failed, a run that happened and produced nothing it could deliver, with a command and expected result per check. [^3] The second entry is the quiet one: the report arrived but does not reconcile, and it looks like a report. You teach the operations lead to compare it to the golden set before believing it, to check whether the model or a rule changed recently, and to escalate when output does not meet the standard even though it looks plausible. The third entry is the change, and it points back at Lesson 12: the operations lead has been approving your changes, and now they must make one themselves, so the runbook hands them the mini-process, scope it, name the risk, test on recent real data, plan the rollback, land it and watch.

Then the moment of truth. You schedule a dry run with the operations lead, present but silent. You trigger a test Monday run that you have deliberately misconfigured and you hand them the runbook. The first time, they stop at the step where they have to read the job's status, because you never wrote the exact command, it was obvious to you. You add the command. The second time they get further and stop where the runbook says to check the credential, because you said "check the credential" without saying where it lives and who owns it. You add the location and the owner. By the third dry run, the operations lead recovers the failed run from the runbook alone while you say nothing, and that is the moment the handover is real.

Finally you taper out: a week of shadowing where they run the Monday job and you watch, a month on call where they run it and you are reachable, a period where you only review the record monthly, then the sunset where your access ends and further help is a separate arrangement they can choose to buy. What you leave behind is a working agent plus a document that explains it, procedures that recover it, a client who has proven they can use both, and a delivery that no longer depends on you being in the room. That is the difference between delivering a system and delivering a capability.

## The transferable skill: make your absence the point

Run the whole discipline together and one pattern emerges, and it is the skill this lesson is really teaching, the same whether the client is a logistics company, a bank, or an internal team. It has five steps, each a deliberate act, not a hope.

**Name the operating state.** Record where the system lives, what it runs on, what scheduled work it does, what it depends on, what limits it, and what normal looks like, as it is now.

**Write the reference.** Turn that state into an operating doc the person who has never met you can read calmly to understand or change the system.

**Write the runbooks.** Turn the failures you have actually seen into procedures a stranger can execute under pressure, with causes in order, expected results, and an honest escalation threshold.

**Dry-run it.** Have an unbriefed operator recover a deliberately failed run using only your documentation while you stay silent, and fix every hole the drill exposes until they can do it alone.

**Taper out.** Reduce your presence on a schedule the client understands, shadow to on-call to review to sunset, and eliminate every single point of failure that depends on you before you go.

This is the difference between the consultant who is trusted and the consultant who is depended on, and the second is a ceiling on your career. A client who depends on you for everything cannot take on the next engagement without you, and a portfolio full of dependencies does not scale, which is exactly the problem the next lesson takes up. The practitioner who makes themselves optional in each delivery, who leaves the client able to run the system and ready for the next phase, is the practitioner who can hold several clients at once instead of being held hostage by one.

## The pitfalls that turn a handover into a dependency

**The file dump.** You hand over everything you created and call it done. Files are not ownership. Hand over the ability to run, change, and recover, and prove it with a dry run.

**Documenting what you built, not what runs.** The doc describes the system you designed, not the system that exists after three months of Lesson 12 changes. Describe as-built reality, updated with every change.

**Writing for yourself.** You leave out the parts that are obvious to you, which are exactly the parts the inheriting operator cannot infer. Write for the operator who has never met you.

**The doc with no runbook, or the runbook with no doc.** Either one leaves the operator stranded, the first when it breaks and the second the moment reality drifts. Deliver both, kept in step.

**No dry run.** You hand over documentation you have never watched a stranger follow, so its first test is a real incident on the client's Tuesday. Run the drill while you can still fix the holes.

**Your departure is the incident.** You leave with credentials only you can reach, an environment only you can start, and recovery moves only you know. Identify each single point of failure that depends on you and eliminate it before the sunset.

**Secrets in the doc.** You write the actual credential into the operating doc to be helpful, turning a configuration note into a breach. Document the existence, location, and owner of each credential, never the value.

## Try it

Take the production agent you have been delivering through this course and hand it over on paper, as if you were leaving at the end of a support taper today.

1. Write the operating doc for a reader who has never met you: where the delivery lives, what scheduled work it does and what it depends on, what guardrails limit it, where its procedural knowledge lives, and what a normal run looks like.
2. Write one runbook entry for a real failure you have seen, with the symptom, the likely causes in order, the steps and their expected results, and an honest escalation threshold.
3. Write one runbook entry for the quiet failure, the wrong-but-plausible output, and state exactly how the operator decides it is wrong and when they escalate.
4. Name the single points of failure that would make your departure an incident, and state how you would eliminate each one before you leave.
5. Plan the dry run: what failure you would trigger, who the unbriefed operator would be, and what you would watch for and fix when they stop.
6. Lay out the taper: the shadow, on-call, and review periods, the sunset, and what support arrangement, if any, comes after.

If you can produce all six, you are no longer delivering a system the client runs in your presence. You are transferring a capability the client can run without you, and that is the delivery that lets you take on the next client, which is where the course goes next.

## What's next

You can now hand over a delivery so completely that the client runs it without you, and that changes what you can take on. A practitioner who is indispensable to one engagement cannot hold a second, and the next lesson is about the practitioner who finally can. It teaches managing a small portfolio of clients: how you keep scope from bleeding between engagements, how you set and hold expectations with each client when you are no longer giving any one of them your full attention, and how you budget your time across several active deliveries so that adding a client does not quietly sacrifice the ones you already have.
