# Lesson 20: Capstone, Build a Complete Go-to-Market Plan for Your Own Practice

Everything this course taught you, the service lines, the offers, the pricing, the delivery system, the portfolio, the hiring, exists so a practice can win work it can deliver well. The last lesson turned that delivery capability into a base of recurring revenue. This capstone turns to the other half of the equation: how work actually comes in the door in the first place. That is go-to-market, and it is the discipline most capable practices either skip or do by accident. A practice can be excellent at everything upstream, at scoping, pricing, delivering, and holding a portfolio, and still stall, because nobody at the firm has decided who to go after, what to say, and through which channels. This lesson is the capstone: it walks you through building a complete go-to-market plan for your own practice, end to end, and it gives you the working example of a Hermes Agent practice doing it so you can see the shape before you write your own. [^1]

Name the problem precisely before solving it. A go-to-market plan is not a sales burst a few weeks before you need revenue. It is the written strategy for how the practice consistently turns its delivery capability into signed engagements: which market it serves, whom it pursues, what it sells them, how it reaches them, how it prices and packages that reach, and how it measures whether the whole thing is working. It is the bridge between the practice you built and the clients who pay for it. Most consultants have a delivery strategy and a hope. The plan replaces the hope with a decision. [^2]

## Why delivery excellence is not enough

Start with the uncomfortable truth that motivates the whole plan. Excellent delivery does not produce inbound demand on its own. A practice that does brilliant work for a handful of clients gets referrals, and referrals are good, but they are also passive and unpredictable, and they reward past work rather than building toward a chosen future. The practice that runs on referrals alone is not running a go-to-market strategy. It is running a reputation, and a reputation is a fine asset and a fragile engine. It gives you the clients who already know about you and no control over the clients who do not. The second you want to grow into a market that has never heard of you, or to grow faster than your current happy clients can refer you, you need something deliberate to pull demand in. [^3]

The deeper reason delivery excellence is not enough is that the market cannot evaluate your delivery until you are already in the room. Nobody buys good delivery sight unseen; the buyer buys the promise of it, as it is represented in your positioning, your offer, and your proof of past results. If those representations are vague, unprofessional, or aimed at the wrong buyer, the excellence underneath never gets examined. A go-to-market plan is, at its core, the act of representing the practice convincingly to a chosen market, so that the delivery you built is given the chance to be seen. Weak go-to-market does not fail because the work is bad. It fails because the work is invisible to the people who need it. [^4]

## What a go-to-market plan actually contains

A complete go-to-market plan has a fixed set of parts, and you should be able to name all of them and write all of them down before you launch. The structure is stable even though the content changes with every practice.

![The seven parts of a go-to-market plan, each a decision that closes off an alternative: market and segmentation, target account profile and buyer, positioning and messaging, offer and pricing, channel and motion, team roles and capacity, and metrics and targets](</diagrams/go-to-market-plan-for-your-own-practice/1-seven-parts.png>)

Market and segmentation. Who are you going after, and how do you split the world into groups you can serve differently? A market is a set of buyers with a shared problem and budget. Segmentation is dividing that market into distinct segments that differ enough that a single offer and a single message cannot reach them all, the enterprise division with compliance constraints versus the mid-market firm that moves faster, for example. You pick segments deliberately, because each one you serve requires its own positioning, its own proof, and often its own offer. Trying to serve everyone with one message is not a go-to-market plan. It is a wish that the market self-segments for you, which it does not do.

Target account profile and buyer. Within a chosen segment, who specifically? A target account profile describes the customers you want, the industry, the size, the revenue band, the systems they run, the problems they have that match your capability. And for each, you name the buyer, the person inside the account who feels the pain, has the budget, and can say yes. A plan that names an industry but not a buyer is not actionable, because nobody at the firm knows whom to reach. A buyer is not just a title, procurement or the CIO, it is the specific role whose problem you solve and whose budget funds you. [^5]

Positioning and messaging. What do you say about the practice, and to whom, in what words? Positioning is where the practice sits in the buyer's mind relative to the alternatives, and messaging is the concrete words you use to claim that position. The message is not a slogan. It is how you describe the problem you solve, the outcome you deliver, and why you are the credible choice for that buyer, in language the buyer uses. A strong message is specific about outcome and proof. A weak one is generic about everything and is therefore indistinguishable from every other firm's weak generic message.

Offer and pricing strategy. What exactly do you sell, and at what price? This is Lessons 2 and 8 standing underneath the plan. You described the offer with a deliverable, an outcome, a scope boundary, a price, and entry criteria. The plan takes that offer and sets how it will be packaged to reach the segment you chose, and how it will be priced against the value for that buyer. A go-to-market plan whose offer is undefined is a plan to sell a conversation. Your catalog is the thing you are taking to market, and it has to be concrete before the plan makes sense. [^6]

Channel and motion. How does the practice reach the buyer? You can go outbound, where you initiate contact yourself. You can build inbound, where content and reputation bring the buyer to you. You can work through partners and referrals, where someone you already trust makes the introduction. Most practices run a combination, but the plan has to say which is primary and what the mechanics of that motion actually are, not just that the practice will "do marketing." A motion without mechanics is a resolution, not a plan, the difference between "we will generate leads" and "we will run one outbound campaign per quarter to a list of fifty named target accounts, with a defined sequence and a weekly review."

Team, roles, and capacity. Who does the go-to-market work? A solo consultant who spends all their capacity on delivery has no one to take offers to market, and this is the quiet killer of small practice growth. The plan has to say who owns the pipeline, who runs the campaigns, who writes the content, who prepares the proposals, and what share of each person's capacity is committed to it. Saying "everyone wears sales hats" usually means nobody does it, because everyone is busy delivering. Making one person accountable, even a principal who bookmarks delivery-owner time for it, is what turns a good intention into an operating rhythm. [^7]

Metrics and targets. How do you know the plan is working? You need leading indicators you can see before revenue, opportunities in the pipeline, responses to outreach, content readership, proposals out, and lagging indicators that prove the outcome, signed engagements, win rate, average deal size, cost per acquisition. The plan sets targets for both and a cadence for reviewing them. A go-to-market plan without metrics is an essay. It only becomes a plan the moment it has numbers someone is accountable to and a regular review that looks at them. [^8]

## The plan is decisions, not aspirations

This is the discipline that separates a plan from a vision, and it deserves emphasis because it is where most go-to-market work fails. Every section above is a decision, and a decision is a commitment that closes off alternatives. When you name a segment, you are deciding who you will deliberately not pursue. When you set a price, you are deciding the value you will hold and the deals you will walk from. When you choose a primary channel, you are deciding how the practice's go-to-market capacity spends itself. If a draft of any section reads as "we will do great work and word will spread," it is not a decision, it is an aspiration, and it will not survive contact with a quarter of real operations. [^9]

![A two-question test tells a plan from an aspiration: does the section close off an alternative, and can a competent stranger run it for a quarter](</diagrams/go-to-market-plan-for-your-own-practice/3-plan-or-aspiration.png>)

The test for whether you have a plan or an aspiration is brutal and useful: can you hand the document to a competent person who knows nothing about you and have them run it for a quarter? If the plan tells them which accounts to pursue, what to say, what to offer, at what price, through which channel, and how to tell if it is working, they can. That is a plan. If the plan tells them to "develop our presence in the AI space and win some enterprise deals," they cannot, because there is no decision for them to execute. A plan is executable by someone other than its author. That is the bar. [^10]

## The working example: a Hermes Agent practice

Put the whole structure on a concrete practice so you can see it in motion. A practice has spent a year building delivery capability on Hermes Agent. It runs isolated profiles per client so engagements stay clean and institutional knowledge accumulates safely. [^11] It has codified its delivery method into skills, so a competent team runs the same playbook on every engagement instead of reinventing it. [^12] It has a recurring operating cadence on cron that checks pipeline and portfolio health on a schedule so nothing drifts because someone forgot. [^13] Its memory accumulates what it learns, so it gets smarter the longer it runs. [^14] And its principal watches the whole thing on a single dashboard, the way a portfolio view replaces a single-project view. [^15] The delivery system is real and it works. What the practice lacks is a plan for who to sell it to. That is the capstone.

The principal runs the plan. Segment. They do not say "AI consulting for everyone." They choose one segment they can win: mid-market operations teams that run a high volume of repetitive knowledge work, customer service, order exception handling, claims, and that are being told to adopt agents but have no in-house capability to evaluate or build them. They name the target account profile: firms in a specific revenue band, with a documented process bottleneck, running legacy systems with no AI team. They name the buyer: the operations lead who feels the error rate and the labor cost, who owns the budget line, and who will champion a project that measurably removes work. [^16]

Positioning and offer. The message is specific. The practice does not say "agents," which the buyer has heard a hundred times. It says: we automate the repetitive exceptions eating your ops team's hours, against your legacy systems, and we hand you a running system and a runbook, not a demo. The offer is the packaged implementation from Lesson 2, priced from the value of the work it removes, with the entry criteria that the client supply data access and a named owner. [^6] The proof is the recorded client results the practice has accumulated, which it can cite because the delivery record lives in the profiles, not in one person's memory. That proof is what makes the positioning credible rather than aspirational.

Channel and motion. They choose outbound as the primary motion, because inbound would take months to build and they want revenue sooner. The mechanics are real: a list of fifty named target accounts built from the segment criteria, a quarterly campaign with a defined opening message, a case study or reference as proof, and a follow-up sequence. One person, the principal, owns the pipeline and has a scheduled block of delivery-owner capacity reserved for it. The cron cadence that already checks portfolio health gets a sibling that reviews the pipeline: which prospects have moved, which proposals are aging, which need a decision. [^13] Promising leads that fit the profile go to a real discovery call, run through the isolated per-prospect profile so the notes and evidence accumulate. [^11] The ones that do not fit the target profile are qualified out, quickly and without guilt, because serving them would be a different plan. [^9]

Metrics and cadence. The plan has leading indicators they review weekly: responses, qualified discovery calls, proposals out. It has lagging indicators they review monthly: signed engagements, win rate, average deal size, cost of acquiring a client. The plan sets targets for each, an agreed number of discovery calls per month, a target win rate, a target deal size, and the review cadence looks at the numbers, not the feelings. When the metrics are flat, the plan gets changed: the message, the list, the offer, the channel, never the resolve. A go-to-market plan is a hypothesis about the market, and a hypothesis is something you test and revise against what the market tells you, not a promise you defend to the death. [^8]

![The Hermes Agent practice running its go-to-market plan, from segment and target account profile through positioning, outbound channel, discovery and qualification, to weekly leading and monthly lagging metrics](</diagrams/go-to-market-plan-for-your-own-practice/4-practice-running-plan.png>)

## Where the plan meets the rest of the course

The capstone is not a separate skill. It is the front end that feeds the machinery the whole course built, and every part of the course shows up in it. The segment and buyer you choose are the same decisions you made when you picked service lines in Lesson 2: you pursue work that has demand, matches your competence, and repeats. [^6] The offer and price you take to market are the packaged, value-priced offers you built in the pricing lessons. [^6] The deal you win goes into the portfolio you manage, judged by the same expand, renew, churn discipline from Lesson 11, so a client brought in by go-to-market is not a yes, it is the start of a decision about how much of your scarce capability it deserves. [^17] The delivery system that keeps the promise sits underneath the whole plan, and the recurring revenue lessons turn the clients you win into a stream you can budget against. [^18] The go-to-market plan does not replace any of that. It is the intake valve that feeds it, and a practice that builds the delivery machine and the intake valve together is the complete article the course has been assembling. [^19]

## Why the plan is the principal's job

It is worth ending on the ownership question, because go-to-market is where the principal's role is most easily surrendered, and the consequences are most expensive. A principal who hands the whole go-to-market to a hired marketer or a junior and walks away has made two errors. The plan encodes judgments the principal has to have made, the choice of segment, the positioning, the price, the offer, and those are decisions of strategy, not of execution, so they cannot be delegated before they exist. And the plan is one system among the systems the practice runs; it has to connect to the delivery, the portfolio, and the recurring revenue loops, and only someone who owns all of those can keep it connected. Go-to-market is not a department. It is the practice's capacity, organized, aimed at a chosen market, and measured. Building it is the principal's job for the same reason building the delivery system is: it is the part of the practice that does not depend on individual heroics, and it is the part that keeps the rest of the machine fed. [^20]

## Worked example

Put the whole capstone on one firm so you can feel the before and after. Before the plan, a Hermes Agent practice is busy. It delivers well, it has happy clients, and it is living on referrals. The principal knows the go-to-market is a problem; there are quarters with long gaps between signed work, and the pipeline is a vague hope that "something will come in." The delivery team is excellent. Nothing is bringing them work except past relationships.

The principal spends a week writing the plan, the actual document, top to bottom. Segment: mid-market operations teams with repetitive knowledge-work bottlenecks and no AI capability. Target account profile: revenue band, documented bottleneck, legacy systems, no AI team. Buyer: the operations lead who owns the error rate and the labor budget. Positioning: agents that remove the exceptions eating ops hours, built against their systems, delivered with a runbook. Offer: the packaged, value-priced implementation from the catalog, with entry criteria named. Channel: outbound to a fifty-name target list, one campaign per quarter, on a cadence, owned by the principal with reserved capacity. Metrics: leading weekly, lagging monthly, with targets for both.

Then the principal runs it, mechanically. The fifty accounts get researched, the campaign goes out on schedule, the qualified ones get real discovery held in their own profile. [^11] The pipeline review fires on cron with the other health checks. [^13] The messaging changes when the first campaign underperforms; the principal notices the word "agent" still invites the buyer's past bad experiences, so the positioning leans harder on the concrete outcome instead. [^8] Deals that fit the segment get won and delivered by the machine. Deals that do not fit get qualified out, quickly, because every hour on the wrong account is a plan failing. [^9]

Eighteen months later the shape is different, and it was not luck. The practice does not live on referrals anymore; it has a quarter-by-quarter pipeline it can budget against, a portfolio it steers, and recurring revenue smoothing the gaps between projects. [^18] The go-to-market plan looks nothing like the first draft, because the market corrected it a dozen times, but the structure is intact and the plan is still a plan. That is the complete practice the course has been assembling. Delivery excellence gave it the ability to serve clients. The go-to-market plan gave it the ability to choose, reach, and win them.

## Try it

The capstone is not an essay to read. It is a document to write, and this is your shot to build the one thing the whole course has been pointing at: a complete go-to-market plan for your own practice. Work through it now, on paper. [^20]

1. Write your market and segmentation. Name the market you serve and the two or three distinct segments within it that need different offers or messages. Then pick the one you will pursue first, and name who you are deliberately not pursuing. The not-pursuing list is as important as the pursuing list.
2. Write a target account profile and a buyer. Give the profile: industry, size, revenue band, the systems they run, the specific problem that matches your capability. Then name the one buyer role whose pain you solve and whose budget funds you. Say the buyer's job and the outcome they personally need.
3. Write your positioning and message. In ten words, what problem do you solve, for whom, to what outcome? Then write the buyer-usable message, in the buyer's language, that a real person could say to a real account. If it reads like a slogan, rewrite it as a specific claim about outcome and proof.
4. Write your channel and motion with real mechanics, not resolutions. Pick outbound, inbound, or partners as primary, then write what that motion actually consists of: the list, the cadence, the owner, the reserved capacity. If you cannot name a concrete mechanism, the motion is a hope.
5. Write your metrics and a review cadence. Name two leading indicators you can see before revenue and two lagging indicators that prove it, and set a target for each. Name the day of the week you will review them and who looks.

If any section comes out as an aspirational sentence rather than a decision, you have found the gap, and the gap is the fun of the capstone: filling it is the difference between a practice that waits for work and one that goes and gets it. A plan that names the market, the buyer, the message, the offer, the channel, and the metric, written so a stranger could run it, is the complete go-to-market plan. Ship it, watch the numbers, and revise.
