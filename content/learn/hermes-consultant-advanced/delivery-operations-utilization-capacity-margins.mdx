# Lesson 7: Delivery Operations: Utilization, Capacity, and Margins

Lesson 6 put a team on the delivery system you have been building, and Lesson 5 made sure that system produces an asset instead of just output. This lesson asks the question the principal has to answer every week once the team exists: is the delivery system actually healthy? Healthy is not a feeling. It is three numbers, and learning to read them is what separates a principal who runs a practice from a principal who is run by one. Utilization, capacity, and margins are the operating dials. Most people treat them as a dashboard where higher is better and the goal is to push all three up. The whole craft is learning that they fight each other, and that the job is not to maximize any of them but to set the operating point where the practice can sustain itself and grow. [^1]

## The three numbers are not three targets

Start with the relationships, because the mistake that sinks scaling practices is treating utilization, capacity, and margin as separate goals to push independently. Push utilization to 100% and you drain the slack that capacity needs to absorb new work. Spend to add capacity and you compress the margin that pays for the addition. Price for a fat margin and you shrink the demand that keeps utilization healthy. They are one system. You cannot move one without moving the other two, and the principal's job is to choose where the system sits, not to chase each needle to its end.

That is the advanced frame, and it is worth holding onto, because almost everything below is a consequence of it. A utilization number means nothing without the capacity it is consuming. A capacity number means nothing without the margin it is allowed to cost. A margin number means nothing without the utilization that earns it. You read all three, in the same review, against the same period, or you are not running a practice, you are watching a scoreboard you do not understand.

## Utilization: the number that lies when it wins

Utilization is the share of the delivery system's available capacity that is spent producing billable output. For a solo consultant that is billable hours over available hours. For a practice running a delivery system of agents, it is the share of working capacity spent on client revenue work rather than on operating the system itself: the capture, the review, the maintenance, the failure recovery. The definition sounds clean. The measurement is not, and that is the first thing to learn about it.

Utilization is a chosen measure, not a discovered one. Every practice can report whatever utilization it wants by choosing its denominator. Two practices doing identical work can report 60% and 95% by disagreeing about what counts as available and what counts as working. One counts every waking hour and only pure client work. The other counts only the hours it bills and excludes everything else. Neither is lying, and neither number tells you anything until you know the denominator. So the first discipline of reading utilization is to ask, of any number you are handed, your own or a partner's: what did you count as available, and what did you count as working? Most disagreements about whether a practice is efficient are really disagreements about the denominator.

The second thing to learn is that 100% is not a win, it is a warning. When everything that can work is always working, nothing is left to absorb the unexpected. A client emergency arrives and there is no slot to take it. A delivery fails review and the rework has nowhere to go. A real opportunity needs a proposal this week and no one is free to write it. The system has no slack, and slack is not waste. Slack is the buffer that lets the system respond, and it is the only thing standing between a practice and a queue. The moment utilization hits 100%, every new demand becomes a queue, and queues turn into missed deadlines, rushed delivery, and rework. And here is the trap: the cost of over-utilization does not show up in the utilization number. It shows up weeks later, in the rework and the burnout, by which time the principal is explaining a failure instead of preventing one.

The third thing is the real question, and it is the one advanced principals actually read. Under-utilization is only idle capital if the spare capacity is doing nothing. If the spare capacity is going into IP capture from Lesson 5, into sales, into training the team from Lesson 6, into the deliberate slack that keeps the system responsive, then it is not idle, it is invested. The reading is not how full the calendar is. It is what the spare capacity is being spent on, and whether that spending is compounding. Two practices can both run at 70% utilization, and one is dying while the other is building, because one is running its spare 30% on nothing and the other is running it on the asset that makes every future delivery cheaper. [^2]

## Capacity: it is throughput, not headcount

Capacity is the amount of deliverable work the system can actually complete in a period. It is not the number of people or agents you have, and confusing the two is the most expensive mistake a scaling practice makes. Capacity is throughput, and throughput is set not by how much you have but by the binding constraint, the one stage that everything else queues behind.

A delivery system has a chain of stages. Scoping feeds into building, building feeds into review, review feeds into delivery. Throughput is the rate at which work comes out the far end, and that rate is set by the slowest stage. Everything that moves faster than the constraint simply builds up in front of it. The constraint could be a person, the one senior who can approve scope. It could be a specialized skill, the single person who can do a particular kind of work. It could be a review gate, the rule that every deliverable must pass review before it ships. It could be a hard ceiling, the concurrency or compute a delivery system is allowed to run. Whatever it is, until you have named the constraint, you do not have a capacity number, you have a guess.

The consequence that most practices learn the hard way is that adding capacity below the constraint does not add throughput. You hire three delivery people when the constraint is the single reviewer, and you get three people producing work that queues behind the same reviewer. Utilization of the new people climbs, throughput stays flat, and you are paying more to produce the same amount. The classic failure is the practice that responds to every bottleneck by hiring more of the thing that is not the bottleneck, because that is the thing it is easiest to hire. The only addition that raises throughput is an addition at the constraint. Find the constraint before you spend on growth, or the growth just buys a longer queue.

The same logic runs through an agent delivery system, which is why it is worth stating in those terms. Such a system does not run unlimited work in parallel. It runs a bounded number of workstreams against a fixed ceiling, and the rest wait. In Hermes, work runs as separable units in isolated profiles, so one engagement's state and failures do not bleed into another's, and the orchestration layer executes parallel workstreams against the ceiling the infrastructure allows. [^3] Push more work into the system than that ceiling can run and you do not get more done. You get a longer queue. And here is the exact same trap as the human version: utilization of the running slots can sit at 100% while throughput is flat, because the slots are full of queued work that is not finishing. High utilization, no throughput, and a principal who reads only the first number will celebrate exactly the condition that is about to miss deadlines.

The advanced habit is to watch the queue in front of the constraint, not the utilization of the people feeding it. A growing queue in front of the reviewer, or in front of the concurrency ceiling, is the earliest honest signal that the constraint is saturated and throughput is about to stall. It arrives weeks before the missed deadline does, and it is free to read. Read the queue, name the constraint, and spend your growth money there.

## Margins: the number that funds the future

Margin is the gap between what a client pays and what the delivery costs. It sounds like the simplest of the three and it is the one most principals get backwards, so it is worth being precise.

You do not control margin by raising the price. The next lesson makes this argument in full, but the part that matters here is that a price is set on the value of the outcome, not on your cost. So the price is not a lever you pull to widen margin. The lever you control is the cost of delivery, and the thing that lowers delivery cost is the IP from Lesson 5. Every engagement that runs on captured method, templates, and tooling costs less than one that reinvents them, at the same price. That gap is wider margin, and it is produced by the asset, not by negotiating harder. IP is the margin machine, and it is why Lesson 5 and this lesson are the same argument seen from two ends. Lesson 5 built the asset because it compounds. This lesson shows the compounding showing up as margin.

Margin matters because it funds the parts of the practice that do not bill. The spare capacity, the IP capture, the sales, the training, the deliberate slack, all of it is paid for by the margin on the work that does bill. A practice that prices its delivery so thin that it has no margin has no way to fund its own future. It is forced to run at high utilization forever just to stay still, and high utilization forever is exactly the state that produces the rework and burnout from the first section. This is the deepest link between the three numbers. The more margin each delivery earns, the less utilization the practice needs to fund its investment. That is why an IP-heavy practice can run at lower utilization and still grow, while a thin-margin practice has to run flat out and still falls behind. The margin buys the slack. The slack lets the practice invest. The investment widens the margin.

There is one more distinction the principal has to hold, because the word margin hides two different numbers. There is delivery margin, the price minus the direct cost of producing the deliverable. And there is practice margin, what is left after you also pay the un-billable operating cost, the slack, the sales, the capture, everything that does not invoice a client. A practice can show a healthy delivery margin and a thin practice margin, and the reason is that it is carrying too much operating overhead for the volume it runs. The gap between the two numbers is the efficiency of the operating layer, and it is the number that tells you whether the practice is paying for a future or paying for a leak. Read both, every review, and read the gap between them hardest of all. [^4]

## Setting the operating point

Bring the three together and the advanced frame from the opening becomes the operating rule. You are not maximizing utilization, capacity, or margin. You are choosing an operating point: how much slack you keep, what the spare capacity does, how wide the margin is, and whether the combination funds the future you want.

Two extreme portraits make the choice concrete. A high utilization, low margin practice sells its time thinly. It has no slack, so it cannot absorb a new opportunity or a failed delivery. It has no margin, so it cannot fund the IP capture and training that would make its deliveries cheaper. It is busy, and it is a job that happens to be called a practice, because nothing it does today makes tomorrow cheaper. The opposite portrait is the lower utilization, higher margin practice, where the spare capacity goes to IP and the margin funds it, and every quarter the same revenue costs less to produce. Neither portrait is right for every market or every principal, and the mistake is not choosing one of them. The mistake is refusing to choose, and drifting into the high utilization version by default, because busy feels productive and a full calendar is a comfortable lie.

The discipline that keeps the operating point honest is a scheduled review. Utilization, capacity, and margin drift, and they drift quietly, a few points a month, until a client emergency or a missed deadline makes the drift loud. The fix is not to wait for the loud event. It is to read the three numbers on a fixed cadence, so the reading happens whether the week was busy or not. In Hermes this is a cron, a scheduled job that fires the operating review on its own, because a review that depends on the principal remembering it during a busy week is a review that will not happen during the weeks it is needed most. [^5] The scheduled review is the mechanism that lets the principal correct the operating point before the drift becomes a crisis, and it is worth treating as seriously as any delivery milestone, because the practice's survival lives in the numbers it reads on a quiet Tuesday, not the ones it sees after a client cancels.

And the review only earns its keep if it is read against the constraints you named and the decisions you made. The question the review answers is never just is utilization up. It is whether the binding constraint is still the one you think it is, and whether the spare capacity is still doing what you planned. Constraints move. The senior reviewer leaves and the constraint moves to whoever can approve scope. The concurrency ceiling changes and the throughput math shifts. A productized offer from Lesson 5 turns a bespoke bottleneck into a template that no longer constrains. The scheduled review is how you notice that the constraint has moved before the system tells you by stalling.

## Worked example: reading the dials on a delivery system

Put the whole thing on a practice that has done the work of the earlier lessons. It has packaged offers, captured IP, a team, and it runs its delivery on an agent system with a bounded number of workstreams and isolated profiles for each engagement. [^3] Business is good. Revenue is up. The principal is not worried, until a scheduled operating review fires and the numbers disagree with the feeling.

Utilization reads high, 95% of working capacity. Capacity reads as full, every workstream slot occupied. Margin reads healthy on each delivery. On the surface, the portrait is a thriving practice. The scheduled review does not stop at the surface. It reads the queue in front of the constraint, and there the story changes. Work is finishing slower than it is starting. The slots are full of engagements that are taking longer than their estimates, which means the throughput number, work actually delivered per week, has been flat for a month while utilization climbed.

The diagnosis is the trap from the capacity section. Utilization is high and throughput is flat, and the reason is that the constraint is saturated. The practice is feeding the system faster than it can deliver, so the running slots are full of work that is not finishing. The principal names the constraint, and it is not the agent capacity, which has headroom. It is the review gate, the human stage where every deliverable must be checked before it ships. Everything queues there. [^6]

The fix follows from the constraint, not from the symptom. The principal does not add more workstreams, which would raise utilization further and lengthen the queue. They do one of two things: add review capacity at the gate, or pull utilization down by accepting fewer engagements until the gate clears. And they look at the margin to decide which. Because the delivery margin is healthy, they can afford to slow intake, run utilization down to a level with real slack, and spend the freed capacity on productizing more of the review work, turning the manual gate into a template and a checklist from Lesson 5 so it stops being the constraint at all.

Three months later the same numbers tell a different story. Utilization is lower, around 75%, and the principal reads it as a success rather than a failure, because the freed 25% went into productizing the gate and into deliberate slack. Margin is wider, because the productized review costs less per delivery. Throughput is up, because the constraint moved and work finishes instead of queuing. The practice is doing less busywork and delivering more, and every number in the review agrees, which is how the principal knows the operating point is finally right. A high utilization number would have been the lie. The low one, read correctly, was the truth. [^7]

## Try it

Read your own delivery system, whatever it is, as if you were a principal in the review above. You do not need a spreadsheet, just honest answers.

1. Pick a recent month and compute your utilization two ways, once with a generous denominator and once with a strict one. If the two numbers disagree by more than a few points, you have not defined your denominator, and every utilization number you have trusted so far is unreadable. Fix the definition first.
2. Name the binding constraint in your delivery chain. Be specific. If the answer is a stage and not a person or a rule, name the person, the gate, or the ceiling. Then answer this: if you added capacity tomorrow, would it be at the constraint or below it? Most of the growth spending in consulting goes below the constraint.
3. Read the queue in front of that constraint right now. Is it growing? If it is, throughput is about to stall, and you are seeing the warning weeks before the missed deadline. This is the earliest and cheapest signal you have.
4. Compute your delivery margin and your practice margin separately. The gap between them is the cost of your operating layer. If the gap is large, ask what the un-billable spend is buying. If it is buying IP and slack, it is investment. If it is buying nothing you can name, it is a leak.

If the diagnosis shows a busy practice that keeps nothing and has no room to respond, you have found the operating point this lesson corrects. Utilization is not the goal. Throughput, margin, and slack are the goals, and utilization is only the dial that trades between them.
