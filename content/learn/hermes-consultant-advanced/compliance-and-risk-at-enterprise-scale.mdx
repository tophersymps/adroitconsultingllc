# Lesson 10: Compliance and Risk at Enterprise Scale

Everything up to this lesson treated risk as something that happens inside an engagement: a slipping dependency, a blown date, a client decision that stalls. You governed that risk in Lesson 9, and governance worked because the exposure was contained. The client paid you to deliver, and you built a machine that made surprises arrive early. This lesson moves the lens outward. A solo consultant carries obligations that end when the engagement does. A practice carries obligations that bind it on its own account, whether or not any single client is in the room, and a practice that runs agents at scale carries a category of exposure that the delivery lessons did not have to face. When the thing doing the work is software you operate, the rules that apply to that software become rules that apply to you. This lesson is about meeting those rules and about deciding, deliberately, which risks the practice will accept.

## Compliance and risk are different machines

Before the practice can manage anything, it has to stop conflating two words that get used as if they meant the same thing.

Compliance is the set of obligations the practice must meet or it suffers a defined consequence. A contractual term about where client data is stored. A regulation about how long records are kept or how a breach is reported. An industry rule about who may see what. A certification the client requires before it will let you near its systems. Compliance is binary at the moment it is checked: you meet the obligation or you do not, and the consequence for not meeting it is set by someone else. You do not get to decide that a data-residency requirement is inconvenient and accept it as a calculated trade. The rule has teeth, and the teeth are not yours.

Risk is different. Risk is the chance that something hurts the practice even when it breaks no obligation. A practice with three clients, each a third of revenue, is not violating anything, but it is exposed: lose one client and a third of the income goes. A delivery method that depends on one senior person is not non-compliant, but it is fragile. A model provider that changes pricing overnight is not breaking a rule, but the practice's margins just moved. Risk is not binary and it is not someone else's call. It is measured in likelihood and impact, and the central act of managing it is deciding how much of it the practice is willing to carry. That decision is called the risk appetite, and it belongs to the principal, not to any checklist. [^1]

The two run on different logic and must not be mixed. Compliance is enforced from outside and is non-negotiable; the work is to know every obligation that binds you and to build controls that meet it. Risk is accepted from inside and is a judgment; the work is to know what could hurt you, decide what you will carry, and control the rest. A practice that treats a compliance obligation as a manageable risk will get burned by a rule it decided to accept. A practice that treats every risk as a compliance failure will choke its own growth refusing to take bets that were never forbidden. Separate the two and each becomes tractable.

## Where a practice's obligations actually live

A practice's obligations do not live in a policy document it writes about itself. They live in the instruments that bind it from outside: the contracts it signs and the regulations that reach it.

Start with the contract, because for a consulting practice that is where most obligations are actually written. Every master services agreement and statement of work carries terms that become standing duties: what client data the practice may touch, where that data may be processed, which subcontractors or subprocessors are permitted, how a breach is reported and within what window, how long work product and records are retained, what survives the end of the engagement. Most consultants sign these without a second thought, because on a single engagement the terms are easy enough to hold. At practice scale they multiply across every client and every term, and they become a map. The map is the list of every obligation, by client, by category, with the control that meets it. The principal who cannot produce that map cannot honestly claim to be in compliance, because they do not know what they have promised.

For a practice that delivers with software agents, two obligations deserve particular attention because they are easy to miss. The first is data handling. When an agent works on client material, that material enters the agent's context and state: what is read, what is remembered, what is written to a log. A client with a data-protection regime will have rules about this, and the rules are not theoretical, because the agent's storage is real and discoverable. The second is the supply chain underneath the software. An agent runs on models and providers. If the practice's obligation to a client says the work may only be processed by named subprocessors, then the model provider is a subprocessor, and the practice has to know where the model runs and who operates it, the same way it would know for any vendor it passed client data through. The principal who treats the model as an invisible tool has signed up their client to a subprocessor they cannot name. [^2]

Regulation reaches a practice the same way it reaches any firm that handles other people's data: through the jurisdictions and industries it works in. The point here is not to enumerate any specific law, because the list changes and a consultant should check current requirements rather than rely on a stale recitation. The point is the skill. The skill is knowing that every client and every market comes with a regulatory surface, and that a practice that builds agents for a regulated industry inherits the rules that govern that industry's data. The obligations are knowable. The practice is not exempt from them because the work is done by software, any more than a factory is exempt because the work is done by machines. If anything, the reverse: software that acts on data is exactly where regulators look, because it is where the data actually flows.

## The exposures that agents add

Agent-delivered work does not change the shape of compliance, but it adds exposures a human-only practice never had, and these are the ones a principal running at scale has to build for. There are five, and it helps to name them.

Data in context. On a human engagement, client data sits in the consultant's files and documents, and you can see where it is. When an agent works, the material is read into the agent's context and can be written into its persistent memory. The exposure is that client data ends up somewhere it was never meant to be, retained past the point the client agreed to. The control is to know what the agent keeps, to keep the memory bounded and curated rather than unbounded, and to scope each client so its material does not linger in a shared store. Hermes is explicit that its memory is bounded and that entries are screened before they are accepted, which is precisely the shape of control a compliance conversation needs: a retention model you can state out loud. [^3]

Cross-client bleed. A practice runs many engagements, and if they all share one agent and one memory, the risk is that one client's context leaks into another's. The control is isolation: each engagement runs in its own profile with its own memory, sessions, skills, and state, so the separation is a property of the system rather than a rule the operator remembers to follow. Profiles exist exactly to keep separate agents from mixing state. [^4] A principal who can say "each client runs in its own isolated profile, so context cannot bleed between them" has turned a hard-to-audit human discipline into a verifiable system property.

The audit trail. A human consultant's decisions live in their head and their notes, and reconstructing them later is archaeology. An agent's actions can leave a complete record: every session, every message, every tool call, searchable afterward. That record is a compliance asset, because it is the evidence that a control ran or that a decision was made and why. A practice that keeps its agent records can answer a client's or a regulator's question about what happened with specifics instead of with a shrug. The exposure is the reverse: if you do not keep the record, you cannot defend a process that ran on software you operated. The principal treats the session log as the practice's memory of what was done. [^5]

The output's provenance. When software produces a deliverable, the question of who is responsible for it is real. The agent proposed it, but the practice stands behind it, and the client relies on it. The skill is knowing that the agent is a tool that produces drafts and proposals, and that the practice is accountable for what it delivers. This is not a novel legal puzzle; it is the same responsibility a firm has for work any junior produces. The agent lowers the cost of producing, not the responsibility for the product. Provenance matters because it tells you which model, which prompt, which session produced a given result, so that when a client asks where something came from you can answer.

Autonomy and blast radius. An agent with permission to act has a wider blast radius than a human who asks before touching anything. The exposure is a single command, run without a human in the loop, that does more than anyone intended. The control is the permission layer itself: agents gate their own destructive actions behind approval, refuse a hard blocklist of catastrophic commands no matter how they are asked, and fail closed in unattended runs. [^6] The principal's job is to set that layer deliberately, tightening it for the work that touches real money and loosening it only where the risk is genuinely contained.

## Risk as a practice function

Set compliance aside, and the second half of the lesson is risk, managed as a standing function rather than an incident-to-incident reflex.

The instrument is the risk register: a living list of what could hurt the practice, each entry carrying its likelihood, its impact, and the control that reduces it. For each risk the principal records both the inherent exposure, what it would be with no control, and the residual exposure, what remains after the control runs. That gap, between inherent and residual, is the value of the control, and it is how the register earns its keep: a risk whose control reduces it from likely-and-crippling to unlikely-and-manageable is being handled; a risk whose control is cosmetic is a risk that has not been handled at all. The register is reviewed on a rhythm, not filed, because risk changes and a control that worked last quarter can rot silently.

Four risks deserve a place on every agent-running practice's register, and naming them shows the shape of the thinking.

Concentration risk. Revenue or capability concentrated in one place, whether one client, one sector, or one person. Not forbidden, but carried only on purpose. The control is diversification, and the appetite question is how much of the practice's income it is willing to rest on one bet.

Key-person risk. The delivery method depends on one person whose judgment or knowledge cannot be replaced quickly. This is where the earlier lessons earn their return: when method is captured into skills, the on-demand procedural documents an agent loads, the practice no longer depends on one head holding the whole method. [^7] Key-person risk is real but it is controllable, and the control is exactly the capture work the course has been doing all along.

Model and provider risk. The practice's delivery runs on models and providers it does not control, and pricing, availability, and capability can all move under it. This risk is structural to agent delivery, so it cannot be eliminated; it can only be made visible and hedged. The questions are whether the practice can run the same work on a different provider if one fails, and whether it has priced the service so a provider change does not wreck the margin. A practice that has asked and answered these can ride a provider shift; one that has not discovers its dependence at the worst moment.

Liability risk. A deliverable that does harm, or that a client relied on and that was wrong, lands on the practice. This is why provenance and the audit trail matter: they are the evidence side of a liability control. The practice that can reconstruct what it did and why, and that has scoped what the agent produces as a draft subject to review, has a far stronger position than one that cannot say what happened.

## Make compliance a property of the system

The recurring lesson across this course, from skills to governance to IP, is that a practice scales by moving judgment out of individual heads and into the system. Compliance and risk are the same move, and it is the most important sentence in this lesson. A control that depends on someone remembering to do the right thing is not a control; it is a hope. A control that is enforced by the operating system is a control.

This is where a practice built on Hermes Agent gets a concrete advantage, because the compliance-critical behaviors are not advisory, they are configured and scheduled. Client separation is not a rule the operators follow, it is the profile model: each engagement runs in its own isolated profile with its own state, so separation is structural. [^8] Destructive actions are not a matter of trusting the agent to be careful, they are gated by an approval layer with a hard blocklist that refuses catastrophic commands regardless of mode. [^9] Memory does not grow without bound, it is bounded and screened, so the retention story is a stated property rather than a hope. [^10] These are the difference between telling an agent "be careful with client data" and having an architecture that cannot easily do otherwise.

The same principle applies to the ongoing work, because compliance is not a state you reach, it is a cadence you run. A practice can schedule its own control checks the way it schedules any recurring job: a periodic review of the risk register, a check that each engagement's isolation is intact, a scan that no obligation has gone stale, delivered on schedule whether or not anyone is thinking about it. Scheduled tasks run on their own, unattended, and can deliver their results to the operator who owns the control. [^11] A control check that fires on a cron is a control that actually runs; one that waits for the principal to remember is a control that runs only when the principal is already worried, which is too late. This is the governance discipline of Lesson 9 aimed not at a single engagement but at the practice's standing obligations, and it is the same trust engineering. The check arrives on schedule, so when it flags a real problem the operator believes it.

## The principal's place

Put the two halves together and the principal's job becomes clear. They own the map of obligations, the list of every promise the practice has made and the control that meets each one. They own the register and the appetite, the written statement of which risks the practice carries on purpose and which it will not. They read the scheduled control outputs the way they read the status cadence of Lesson 9, and they reserve their judgment for the exceptions the system cannot decide: the new obligation that arrives with a new client, the risk whose appetite must change because the market changed, the compliance question that genuinely needs a human call.

What the principal does not do is the compliance work by hand or on hope. They do not keep the map in their head, because a practice map is too large for any head to hold and too important to lose when a person changes. They do not run the scheduled checks by memory, because checks that depend on memory do not run. They build the controls into the architecture and the cadence once, staff their operation, and spend their judgment on the parts that genuinely require it. That is the whole difference between a practice that is accidentally exposed and a practice that knows exactly where it stands, and it is the difference a client with a compliance department can feel within the first conversation. A practice that can say, plainly, which client data goes where, what it keeps and for how long, which providers it passes data to, and how it would prove any of it, is a practice a regulated client can buy from. One that cannot is a practice that will discover the gap when it is already too expensive to close.

## Worked example

Run it on a real shape. A principal runs a practice that has built a repeatable delivery method for deploying multi-agent operations teams on Hermes Agent, and they have sold it to a few mid-market clients. Now a regulated client, a regional bank, wants to engage them to automate a back-office reconciliation workflow that touches real customer records. The engagement itself is not new work for the delivery method. What is new is that the bank's procurement and compliance teams will ask questions the mid-market clients never asked, and the practice has to answer them before it can start.

The first thing the principal does is not technical. It is to build the map. They go through the bank's terms and its data-protection requirements and list every obligation the engagement creates: customer records may only be processed in named locations, the model provider is a subprocessor and must be disclosed and approved, records must be retained for a defined period and deleted after, a suspected breach must be reported to the bank within a fixed window, and the practice must be able to demonstrate each of these. Against every obligation they name the control that meets it. Client data runs only in the bank's own isolated profile, so it never mixes with any other engagement and its retention is scoped to that profile. The audit trail is kept, so the practice can show what the agents did with the customer records. The provider is disclosed up front as a subprocessor, named and approved by the bank before any data moves. [^12]

Then they go to the register, because the bank engagement changes the practice's risk picture. Concentration risk rises, because this client is larger than the others, and the principal decides consciously how much of the practice's income may rest on it. Provider risk is now real, because the work touches regulated customer data and the practice must be able to prove where the model runs; the principal confirms a fallback provider so the delivery is not hostage to one vendor's pricing or availability. Autonomy risk rises, because the agents touch real money-adjacent workflows, and the principal tightens the approval layer for this engagement so destructive actions and high-impact commands are gated rather than run freely, even in unattended scheduled runs, which fail closed. [^13]

Then they build the cadence. A scheduled review checks, on a fixed rhythm, that the bank's profile is still isolated, that no customer record has leaked into a shared store, that the audit trail is intact, and that the approved-subprocessor list still matches reality. It delivers its findings to the principal on schedule, whether or not there is a problem, so that a quiet report builds the trust that makes a flagged report believed. A new obligation, a provider change, or a control that has gone stale arrives in the principal's judgment rather than being caught at audit time.

Now the counterfactual, because it shows the value. Same bank, same engagement, no map, no register, no cadence. The practice signs the contract and starts the work, and the model provider, which the principal never disclosed as a subprocessor because it never occurred to them that a model counted as one, processes customer records under terms the bank never approved. The bank's compliance review, months later, finds the gap. The practice is not just embarrassed; it has broken a term it signed, and the consequence is set by the bank, not by the practice. Every hour of delivery work has been building exposure, not value, because nobody did the work up front that made the engagement safe to run. The compliance map and the register were not overhead; they were the difference between a practice that could take the bank's money and one that took the bank's money and then could not account for it.

## Try it

You do not need a regulated client to practice this lesson. You need an honest look at the practice or the engagement pipeline you are already running.

1. Build the map for your most consequential client. Write down every obligation the contract or its data requirements place on you, and against each one name the control that meets it. If you cannot name a control for an obligation, you do not meet that obligation, you are hoping.
2. Name where your client data actually lives. When an agent works on client material, what is read, what is kept, and what is retained afterward? State the retention model out loud. If you cannot state it, it is not a model, it is an accident.
3. Write your risk register. List at least four things that could hurt the practice even though nothing is forbidden, and for each record its likelihood, its impact, and the control that reduces it. Then separate inherent from residual so you can see what each control is actually worth.
4. Write your risk appetite in one sentence. Which of those risks are you carrying on purpose, and which have you not actually decided to carry, which is a different and worse situation?

If you cannot produce the map, the register, or the appetite, your practice is not managing compliance and risk, it is discovering them, and discovery always happens at the worst moment. The fix is not more vigilance. It is the architecture this lesson builds: obligations known and mapped, risks named and decided, and controls that run because they are properties of the system, not promises kept by people who are too busy to keep them.

## What's next

You can now run a practice that knows its obligations and has decided which risks it carries, with controls enforced by the system rather than by memory. The next lesson turns from protecting the practice to growing it, on managing a client portfolio strategically: expansion, renewal, and churn. Where this lesson asked which obligations bind you and which risks you accept, the next one asks which clients deserve more of you, which ones you should renew and which you should let churn, and how a principal allocates a scarce delivery capability across the accounts that will build the practice rather than just fill its schedule. Compliance keeps the practice safe to grow. The portfolio lesson decides what growth is worth pursuing.
