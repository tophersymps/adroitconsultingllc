# Lesson 14: Setting Up a Consultancy: Legal, Finance, Tooling, and Operations

Lesson 13 gave you the machinery that compounds: reference architectures that turn repeated work into proven patterns, and internal tooling that makes every subsequent engagement cheaper and more capable. That accumulation is the real product of a practice, but it does not float in the air. It needs something to belong to. A set of reference architectures, a folder of hardening scripts, a captured delivery method, these have to be owned by an entity, protected by a legal structure, funded by working capital, and operated by a system that keeps them alive after the founder stops holding every detail in their head. This lesson is about building that vessel. It takes the practice from being a person who does consulting, which is what the earlier lessons presumed, to being a company that does consulting, which is the only way the compounding of Lesson 13 survives contact with reality.

Most consultants never make this transition deliberately. They start as individuals, they sign contracts in their own name, they pay expenses from the same account that pays their rent, they hold the whole operation in their head, and they keep doing that long after the work has outgrown the frame. The cost of that frame is invisible until it is not. The moment a client with a serious procurement process asks who the contracting party is, the moment a dispute arrives and personal assets are on the line, the moment hiring becomes real and someone needs a W-2 or its local equivalent rather than a handshake, the lack of structure stops being a paper detail and becomes the thing that decides whether the practice survives. The discipline of this lesson is to make those decisions before they are forced, because every setup decision made in a hurry, under the pressure of a deal or a dispute, is made badly.

## The legal frame: the entity that owns the compounding

Start with the hardest question, which is what kind of legal entity the practice should be, because everything else hangs off it. The default answer for a solo consultant is to keep working as an individual, but that is usually the wrong vessel once the practice is real, and the reason is the word that gives the whole decision its shape: liability. When the practice has clients with real money, a deliverable that does harm, or software that touches other people's data, the question of who is on the hook matters, and the default, with no entity, is that the person is on the hook. Every contract the consultant signs binds them personally. Every dispute is a dispute with their personal assets. Every judgment lands on their house. A legal entity, in most jurisdictions, is the mechanism that separates the practice's obligations from the person's, so that the company can be liable without the founder losing everything they own. That separation is not a tax dodge and it is not optional window dressing. It is the whole reason the entity exists.

The precise structure varies by jurisdiction, and a real firm gets the specifics from a lawyer who knows where it operates. The skill this lesson teaches is not the law, it is knowing what to ask for. The structure has to do three jobs. First, it must separate personal from business liability, which is the limited-liability company or corporation family of structures in most places, as opposed to operating as a sole trader where the separation does not exist. Second, it must let the practice actually be a practice: hold contracts, employ people, own intellectual property, raise money or bring in partners, so that ownership can change without the founder being the business. Third, it must be a shape the clients recognize and can contract with, because an enterprise client cannot easily buy from a person, but it buys from a company every day. When you choose, you are not picking a tax label. You are deciding how liability attaches, how ownership can move, and how credible the firm is as a counterparty.

Alongside the entity comes the agreement backbone, the set of standard contracts the practice uses for every engagement so it is not reinventing terms each time a client appears. The four that matter are the master services agreement, which sets the standing terms for how the practice and the client work together; the statement of work, which scopes a specific engagement; the intellectual-property assignment, which makes clear what the client owns and what the practice retains (and this is where the reusable assets of Lesson 13 get protected, because the contract has to carve out the practice's own tooling and method from what transfers to the client); and the non-disclosure agreement. A practice with a clean backbone signs new work by dropping a statement of work onto standing terms, instead of negotiating the entire relationship from scratch for every deal. That is the legal analogue of the reference architecture: a proven structure with the reasoning attached, reused instead of rediscovered.

There is a second, quieter reason the entity matters for a practice that builds with software agents, and it connects to the compliance lesson of Lesson 10. The entity is who the obligations bind. When a client's data-protection terms say the practice may only process data in named locations, the obligation belongs to the company, and the company is the thing that can be audited, sued, and held to account. If the founder runs everything in their own name, the line between the practice's obligations and the founder's personal life does not exist, which makes a mess of the very compliance map that lesson told you to build. A clean legal frame gives the compliance map an owner with a name on the contract. It makes the practice a party that can be held responsible, which is uncomfortable until you realize it is also the thing that makes a client willing to trust it with real work.

## Finance: the practice as its own economy

With a legal frame in place, the second pillar is finance, and the mindset shift is the one that separates a practice from a well-paid freelancer. A freelancer has income. A practice has a company with its own books, its own costs, its own obligations, and its own reserves, and the money that flows out of it to the founder is one line in that company's accounts, not the whole story. Until the consultant thinks this way, they cannot answer the most basic questions a principal has to answer: does the practice make money as a company, what does it cost to run, and how much of what clients pay is actually available to the people who built it.

The first financial act is separation. Business money and personal money stop mixing, immediately, and every transaction of the practice goes through its own accounts so that the books tell the truth about the business. This is not puritanism. A practice whose expenses are buried in a personal account cannot know what it costs to deliver, which means it cannot price, which means every earlier lesson about margin and pricing is built on sand. The operating numbers of Lesson 7, utilization, capacity, and margin, are only computable if the revenue and the cost of delivery actually show up in the same set of books. Separation is what makes the numbers real.

The second act is recognizing that consulting cash is lumpy, and that the practice has to be built to survive the gaps. Revenue arrives when a milestone lands or an invoice is paid, not on a payroll schedule, and a practice that spends everything the month it comes in is one slow quarter from being unable to pay its own people. The discipline is a reserve: a buffer of working capital, sized to carry the practice through a defined number of slow months, so that a dry spell is a fact of the calendar rather than an existential threat. A principal sizes the reserve the way they size any risk on the Lesson 10 register, by deciding how much of a downturn the practice is willing to absorb without changing how it operates. The number is not arbitrary. It is the difference between a practice that can say no to bad work because it is not desperate, and one that takes any deal because the bank balance forces it.

The third act is pricing for the cost of being a company, not just for the hours of delivery. The earlier pricing lessons were about choosing between hourly, productized, and value-based models, and they were right. What this lesson adds is that whatever model the practice prices under, the price has to cover the full cost of the entity: the insurance, the legal and accounting fees, the tooling subscriptions, the time the principal spends that is never billable to a client, the office or the software that keeps the operation running. A solo consultant who prices only for delivery hours is quietly subsidizing the company from their own pocket. A principal prices the offer to carry the whole machine, and the Lesson 13 machinery is precisely what makes that possible, because it lowers the delivery cost enough that the price can carry the overhead and still leave a margin.

The fourth act is a cadence, a monthly operating review that asks the same questions every period and refuses to let the practice drift by accident. How much revenue came in and from which clients. What the delivery actually cost. What the margin was. How much is in the reserve. What is owed and what is owed to us. What changed in the pipeline. The point of the monthly review is not to produce a report nobody reads. It is to make the practice's financial state a thing the principal knows on a schedule, so that problems arrive as trends to be managed instead of as surprises that are already too big to absorb. A practice that reviews its numbers monthly is steering. A practice that only looks at the numbers when a crisis forces it is being steered.

## Tooling: running the practice on the same machinery as the delivery

The third pillar is tooling, and the insight that makes this lesson cohere is that the practice should be operated with the same discipline it uses to deliver. Lesson 13 built internal tooling to make delivery cheaper and more capable. This lesson extends that machinery to the firm itself, so that the running of the business, its standing obligations, its reviews, its knowledge, its handoffs, is not carried in the founder's head but run by the same kind of system that runs the client work. A practice that delivers with agents but administers itself by memory is living in two centuries at once, and the memory side is the one that will fail first.

Hermes Agent is the concrete example of how this works, and it is worth being specific because the capabilities map directly onto the running of a practice. The first is knowledge that outlives the moment. A practice accumulates the procedural knowledge of how it works, the delivery method, the review standards, the way it scopes, the mistakes it has learned not to repeat, and that knowledge is exactly the kind of thing that dies when it lives in one person's head. Hermes captures it as skills, the on-demand procedural documents an agent loads when a task calls for them, and as a bounded memory that stores the durable facts the practice needs across sessions. [^1]] The practice that writes its method down as skills, and reviews and refines them the way Lesson 13 said to treat any compounding asset, has turned its operating knowledge into a system asset instead of a founder's memory. A new operator onboards onto a captured method, not onto whatever the founder happens to remember that week.

The second is a schedule that runs whether or not anyone remembers. The running of a practice is full of recurring obligations, the monthly financial review, the quarterly risk-register check from Lesson 10, the client account reviews, the check that the reference architectures are still true, the follow-ups that keep a pipeline alive. These are exactly the jobs that get skipped when they depend on a busy principal remembering to do them, which is to say they get skipped. A practice on Hermes schedules them as cron jobs, recurring tasks that fire on their own and deliver their output to whoever owns them, so that a standing review runs on schedule whether or not the principal is thinking about it, and its findings arrive like clockwork rather than when someone gets worried enough to look. [^2]] The cadence is the point. A control or a review that runs on a schedule is a control that runs; one that waits to be remembered is one that runs only when it is already late.

The third is separation that is structural rather than remembered. A practice runs many engagements, and the discipline of Lesson 10 was that each client's material must not bleed into another's. That discipline is easy to state and hard to keep by hand. Hermes makes it a property of the architecture through profiles, isolated agent environments each with its own state, so that each client or each line of the practice runs in its own container and the separation is enforced by the system rather than by an operator's good intentions. [^3]] The practice that runs each engagement and each practice function in its own profile has turned a human rule into a fact of the machine. The same applies to security, where the permission layer that gates destructive actions and refuses a hard blocklist of catastrophic commands is what keeps an autonomous operation from doing more harm than anyone intended when it runs unattended. [^4]]

The fourth is delegation that scales the founder's judgment without scaling their hours. A practice that is one person cannot multiply itself by hiring a hundred copies of that person, but it can multiply its reach by handing bounded, well-scoped pieces of work to agents or subagents that carry a task from a defined brief to a defined output and return. [^5]] This is the leverage that Lesson 1 promised, delivered not by more bodies but by more of the work being run by the system. The principal's judgment goes into the briefs, the standards, and the reviews, and the routine execution is absorbed by machinery that does not need to sleep or be managed. The tooling pillar is how a practice stops being a person with clients and becomes a company that delivers.

## Operations: the rhythm that keeps the firm alive

The fourth pillar is operations, which is the layer that ties the legal, financial, and tooling pillars into a thing that actually runs day to day. Operations is the practice's operating rhythm: the standing cadences, the defined owners, the way work moves, the way problems escalate, and the handoffs that make sure nothing depends on a single person being in the room. A practice without operations is a collection of good intentions and capable people. A practice with operations is a delivery system that runs on schedule.

The heart of operations is a small number of standing rhythms, and they should be recognizable from the rest of this course because they are the same cadences the delivery lessons built, turned inward on the firm. There is the engagement rhythm, the kickoff, the status cadence, the review, the handoff, that every client engagement runs through. There is the account rhythm, the periodic review of how each client relationship is going, whether it is on track to renew, whether it is a candidate for expansion or for churn, which is the portfolio management of Lesson 11 applied on a schedule instead of by crisis. There is the firm rhythm, the monthly financial review and the quarterly risk-register check that the tooling pillar schedules. And there is the improvement rhythm, the periodic review of the practice's own machinery, the reference architectures and internal tooling of Lesson 13, asking the three questions that keep them honest: is it still true, is it still used, is it still the best way to do this. A practice that runs these four rhythms on schedule is operating. One that improvises them when someone remembers is hoping.

Alongside the rhythms comes the matter of ownership, which was the third property of compounding tooling in Lesson 13 and is equally true of operations. Every function of the practice has to have a named owner, someone who is responsible for keeping it correct, current, and running. The financial review has an owner. Each client account has an owner. The risk register has an owner. Each reference architecture and each piece of internal tooling has an owner. The owner does not have to do all the work, but they have to be the one who notices when something drifts, who absorbs the feedback from the last cycle, and who can answer the question of whether their function is healthy. A practice where every function has an owner can absorb change, because when a person leaves, their functions are already named and can be reassigned. A practice where ownership is implicit, spread across whoever happens to remember, is one departure away from discovering it did not actually know how its own firm ran.

Operations also has to include the handling of the exceptional, because even the best rhythm will surface things it was not built to decide. The question of how a problem escalates, who it reaches, and how quickly, is part of the operating design. Lesson 9 built this for a single large engagement, and the practice needs the same shape at the firm level: a defined path by which a problem that the routine cannot absorb arrives, quickly and unfiltered, at the person with the judgment and authority to resolve it. The principal is the final escalation point, not because they should be in every decision, but because there has to be a point where a decision genuinely lands. The discipline of operations is that the routine is absorbed by the system and the exceptional reaches the principal on a path that is known in advance, so that when the exceptional happens nobody has to figure out who to call while the problem is burning.

## Why this is the entity behind the compounding

Pull the four pillars together and the thesis of the lesson is clear, and it is worth stating precisely because it is what distinguishes a firm from a freelancer with help. Lesson 13 built assets that compound across engagements. This lesson builds the entity that owns them, protects them, funds them, and keeps them running, and without that entity the compounding is fragile in a way that the assets themselves cannot fix. A reference architecture is only worth something if the entity that owns it exists, has a legal right to it, and is still operating to use it. Internal tooling is only worth something if the company that built it has the cash to keep it maintained and the operations to review it on a schedule. The delivery method is only worth something if the firm that captured it survives the departure of the people who first ran it. The legal, financial, tooling, and operational pillars are not overhead on top of the real work. They are the container that lets the real work compound instead of evaporating.

This is also the honest answer to why so many consultants never make it, and it is worth saying plainly because it is the failure mode this lesson exists to prevent. The consultant who never forms the entity, never separates the money, never writes down the method, never schedules the reviews, is not avoiding overhead. They are choosing to remain a person whose practice disappears when they stop. They have built a reputation they rent and a skill they carry, and both go with them. The principal who does the setup work is building something that can outlive any single engagement, any single client, and any single person, including themselves. That is the difference between owning a practice and renting a career, and it is the whole point of building the firm rather than just doing the work.

## Worked example

Put it together on the arc of a practice that has been running the way this course describes. A principal has been delivering multi-agent operations teams on Hermes Agent, has hit the point where the work is repeatable enough to codify, and has decided to stop being a consultant with a reputation and become a firm. The setup happens in a deliberate order.

The legal work comes first, because it is the foundation. The principal forms a company, works with a lawyer who knows the jurisdiction to choose the structure that separates personal from business liability, allows ownership to move, and is a shape enterprise clients can contract with. They register the entity in the place where the practice actually operates and where the clients are. They set up the agreement backbone: a master services agreement, a statement of work template, an intellectual-property assignment that carves out the practice's own tooling and method from what transfers to each client, and a non-disclosure agreement. From now on, new work is contracted by dropping a statement of work onto standing terms instead of renegotiating the relationship from scratch every time. The compliance map of Lesson 10 now has an owner with a name on the contract.

The financial work comes second. The principal opens business accounts, stops the flow of practice money through personal accounts, and sets up the books so that revenue and delivery cost land in the same place. They price the practice's offers to carry the full cost of the entity, the overhead, the insurance, the never-billable time, not just the delivery hours, and they rely on the Lesson 13 machinery to keep the delivery cost low enough that the price still leaves a margin. They build a reserve sized to carry the practice through a defined number of slow months, and they put a monthly operating review on the calendar that asks the same questions every period, revenue, cost, margin, reserve, receivables, pipeline, so the financial state is known on a schedule rather than discovered in a crisis.

The tooling and operations work come third, and they are where Hermes Agent stops being just the delivery vehicle and becomes the firm's operating system. The principal captures the practice's method into skills, the delivery standards, the scoping approach, the review gates, so the operating knowledge is a system asset a new operator can load instead of a memory only the founder holds. [^6]] Each client engagement and each internal function runs in its own profile, so client material stays isolated and separation is structural rather than remembered. [^7]] The monthly financial review, the quarterly risk-register check, the reference-architecture health checks, the client account reviews, all become scheduled jobs that fire on their own and deliver their findings to their owners, so the cadence runs whether or not anyone is thinking about it. [^8]] Every function gets a named owner. Every engagement, account, and standing obligation has a path by which the exceptional reaches the principal.

The result is a firm that can survive its own success. A new client can be onboarded onto a captured method, staffed with an operator who loads the skills rather than shadowing the founder for six months, and run in an isolated profile with its own audit trail. The principal can step back from the routine, because the routine is absorbed by a system with defined owners and a schedule that runs on its own, and reserve their judgment for the briefs, the reviews, and the exceptions the machine cannot decide. The compounding of Lesson 13 has a vessel that owns it, funds it, protects it, and keeps it alive. The consultant became a company, and the company is worth more than the consultant ever was, because it does not depend on any one person to keep running.

## Try it

You do not need a practice at scale to start the setup, and the faster you start, the cheaper it is, because every setup decision made in a hurry is made badly. The work is to make the decisions while they are optional.

1. Audit your legal frame. If you run client work today, whose name is on the contracts? If the answer is your own personal name, you have made a liability decision by default rather than by choice. Find out what a limited-liability structure costs in your jurisdiction and whether it is worth it at your current exposure, and note the agreement backbone you would sign new work under.
2. Separate your money. Open a business account and route every practice transaction through it, even if you are not yet ready to form an entity. Until the books are separate, you cannot know what the practice costs to run, which means you cannot price it, which means every delivery and pricing decision you make is partly guesswork.
3. Write down one piece of method as a skill. Take the single delivery process you repeat most, the way you scope, the way you review, the way you hand off, and capture it the way Lesson 13 said to capture any compounding asset. If a new operator could load that skill and do the work to your standard, you have started to make the method a property of the firm instead of a property of your head.
4. Schedule one standing review. Pick the single recurring check your practice most needs and does not currently run, whether it is a monthly financial review, a quarterly risk-register check, or a client account review, and put it on a schedule that fires on its own and delivers its output to its owner. One review that actually runs is worth more than ten that wait to be remembered.

If you can do all four cleanly, you are well ahead of most practices, and the work is to extend the frame until it covers the whole firm. If any of them made you uncomfortable, you have found exactly the gap this lesson builds, and the good news is that the raw material is already there. You have the method, the clients, and the compounding assets. What is missing is the entity that owns them, the books that fund them, and the operating system that keeps them alive. That is the layer that decides whether you are building a firm that outlasts you, or renting a career one engagement at a time.

## What's next

You can now set up a consultancy as a real firm: a legal entity that owns and protects the practice's assets, finances that tell the truth about whether it makes money as a company, tooling that runs the firm on the same machinery as the delivery, and an operating rhythm with named owners that keeps it alive on schedule. The next lesson turns from how the practice is structured and run to how it proves it does good work, on quality systems and certification for your practice. Where this lesson built the entity that owns the compounding, the next one builds the evidence that the compounding is real: how a practice defines what quality means, how it builds a quality system that makes good work repeatable rather than accidental, and how certification turns that system into something a client can trust from the outside. Structure makes the firm real. Quality systems and certification make it credible to the clients who pay for it.
