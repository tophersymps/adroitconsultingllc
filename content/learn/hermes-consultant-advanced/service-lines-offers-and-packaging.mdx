# Lesson 2: Service Lines, Offers, and Packaging

Lesson 1 gave you the frame. A principal sells capacity, wins through leverage, and builds the delivery system rather than the deliverables. That frame stays abstract until the practice has something to sell, and most practices fail exactly there. They have capability, which nobody can buy, and no offers, which are the only things a client can buy. This lesson closes that gap. It teaches you to split what you do into service lines, turn each line into priced offers, and package those offers so a client can buy them without you inventing the scope from scratch every time. That act, turning capability into a repeatable product, is the single most important product decision a principal makes. [^1]

## Capability is not an offer

Start with the distinction that most confusion lives in, because the words get used interchangeably and the difference is the whole game.

A service line is a category of work the practice does. It names a domain of capability, not a thing anyone can buy. "Agent implementation," "automation audits," "ongoing agent operations," those are service lines. They describe what the practice is good at. They are not sellable, because a client cannot buy a category. If you say to a client "we do agent implementation," the client has no idea what that costs, what they get, or how long it takes, so they have nothing to say yes to. [^2]

An offer is a specific, priced, scoped thing a client can say yes to. It has a defined deliverable, a defined outcome, a defined price, and defined entry criteria. "We will build you a working customer-support agent on Hermes Agent, with discovery, build, and a two-week handover, fixed at forty thousand dollars, in six weeks, provided you can supply the knowledge sources and a named internal owner," that is an offer. The client can evaluate it, accept it, or reject it. That is the only unit a client can actually transact on. [^3]

The principal's job is to convert the first into the second. A practice with three service lines and thirty offers has a catalog. A practice with three service lines and no offers has a reputation and a phone. The difference is not cosmetic. The difference is whether anyone can buy what you do.

## Why offers are the leverage point

Recall the leverage lesson from Lesson 1. Leverage comes from codification: turning the judgment you use to do the work into a repeatable system a competent person can run. An offer is where codification becomes commercial. Until you define an offer, every sale is a fresh act of invention. A client calls, you spend weeks in discovery figuring out what they need and what it should cost, you write a bespoke proposal, and you price it by feel. Every engagement is its own little start-up. That is the solo consultant's economics, and it is terrible.

Defined offers change that. When an offer is defined, the scope is known before the client is. You know what you will deliver, how long it takes, what it costs you to produce, and what it is worth to the client. That lets you do three things a solo consultant cannot. You can price for value instead of by feel, because the cost basis is known. You can build the delivery machinery for that offer once and reuse it across every sale, which is the leverage. And you can hold margin, because when the scope and cost are pinned, a price that does not clear your cost is a pricing error you catch in the catalog, not a surprise discovered halfway through a project. [^4]

This is why packaging is the principal's real product work. The client deliverable is still the output, but the thing you are actually building is the repeatable offer: the defined scope, the price, and the machine that produces it at a known cost. Lesson 1 said the principal builds the machine that builds the deliverables. This lesson is where that machine gets a price tag.

## Service lines first

Do not start with offers. Start by deciding what the practice does, because the service lines constrain everything downstream. A good service line has three properties, and it is worth testing every candidate line against all three.

First, demand. There has to be a market that will pay for it. This sounds obvious and is routinely ignored, because consultants fall in love with what they are good at rather than what people will buy. Your favorite craft is not a service line. A service line is a category someone has budget for.

Second, competence. The practice has to be able to deliver it well, ideally better than the market average, and ideally without the principal's hands in every engagement. This is the line's connection to the Lesson 1 frame. If a candidate service line only works when you personally deliver it, it is not a service line yet. It is a hobby you bill for.

Third, repeatability. The work has to be similar enough across clients that the practice can build shared method, templates, and standards for it. A service line that is a different bespoke project every time cannot be productized, and a non-productizable service line cannot be scaled. You can keep a small amount of genuinely bespoke work, but it is not where the practice's growth comes from.

Test a candidate line against those three and most candidates fail. "Legacy system rescue" might have demand and competence but no repeatability. "Ongoing agent operations" might have demand and repeatability but no delivered competence yet. That is fine. The test exists to make you say no to lines that will not scale, because a practice that says yes to everything builds nothing. A service line is a commitment. When you name one, you are deciding what the practice will be known for and, just as important, what it will not take. [^5]

## From service line to offer

Once a service line survives the three tests, the real work begins: turning it into offers. An offer needs five things to be buyable, and the discipline is to write all five down.

Deliverable. What exactly does the client receive at the end? Not "an agent." "A deployed customer-support agent on the client's Hermes instance, with a configured knowledge base, a set of escalation rules, and a written operating runbook." The more concrete the deliverable, the more the client can evaluate it and the less scope ambiguity you carry.

Outcome. What does the client get out of it, in terms they care about? This is the value side of the price. "Deflects routine support volume so the human team can focus on escalations." You price the outcome, not the hours, which is the single biggest pricing shift between a consultant and a principal.

Scope boundary. What is in, and more importantly what is out. Every offer needs an explicit list of what is not included, because scope is where margin goes to die. The client who says "we also need it to integrate with our ticketing system" is testing whether your offer has a boundary. It should have one, written down before the sale, so that request is a change order and not an assumption.

Price and duration. The number and the timeline. Fixed offers make this possible, because the scope is known in advance. This is the productization dividend: you can quote a fixed price because you are selling a defined product, not a guess.

Entry criteria. The conditions that must be true for the practice to take the engagement. This is the offer's immune system. "Client supplies the knowledge sources, names an internal owner, grants access to the target systems, and commits to the review cadence." An offer with no entry criteria will accept every bad client, and a bad client is not one you recover margin from. It is one that burns the machine. [^6]

## The packaging trade

Defining an offer is packaging, and packaging is a trade. You are trading away the flexibility of a bespoke engagement in exchange for repeatability, and the trade is almost always worth it, but you should make it with your eyes open.

The thing you give up is fit. A fixed offer will never fit a client as perfectly as a bespoke engagement scoped for them. Some clients need a bigger scope, some a smaller one. A fixed offer either over-delivers or under-delivers for a given client. You manage that by defining a tight core deliverable that most clients in the line actually need, and by keeping a bounded set of add-ons that a client can bolt on for additional price. This is the standard productized structure: a core offer with a short menu of priced extensions. It keeps the core repeatable while giving you an honest answer when a client's needs run past the boundary. [^7]

The thing you get back is control. When the offer is fixed, the practice owns the scope, the method, and the price. That control is what makes leverage possible, because it lets you build the delivery machinery for the offer once and run it on every engagement. It is also what makes margin predictable, because cost and price are both known. A practice that sells fixed, packaged offers runs a business. A practice that sells bespoke projects runs a series of arguments about scope and price.

There is a trap here worth naming, because it is the most common way packaging goes wrong. Productizing is not the same as commoditizing. A packaged offer still has to deliver real value and be priced for the value it delivers. The mistake is to package a service line into a cheap, thin product and compete on price, which destroys the margin the whole exercise was meant to protect. The packaging is the mechanism that lets you charge for the outcome instead of the effort. If you package it down to effort and sell it cheap, you have productized your way into worse economics than a solo consultant. [^8]

## Productization changes what you measure

Here is the quiet payoff, and it ties the commercial work back to the operational frame from Lesson 1. Once offers are productized, the practice's health stops being measured in hours and starts being measured in offer-level economics, which is exactly the portfolio view a principal needs.

For each offer, you now track a cost per delivery and a margin per delivery, because the scope is fixed and the production cost is knowable. You track how many times each offer has sold, which tells you which service lines are earning their place and which are costing you attention without paying for it. You track how long each delivery takes against the plan, which surfaces the offers where the scope boundary is leaking and the machine is running over budget. And you track utilization across the offers, because a principal's real constraint is not billable hours, it is whether the delivery team is producing. [^9]

All of this is the same shift Lesson 1 described, applied to the commercial layer. The consultant asked, did this project make money. The principal asks, which offers are the practice's engine, which are dragging on it, and where is margin concentrated or at risk. The single-project question becomes a portfolio question, and the portfolio is made or broken by the health of the offers in it. [^10]

## Worked example

Run the whole thing on one practice. A principal has a service line they are confident in: building customer-support agents on Hermes Agent. Demand is real, the team can deliver it, and the work repeats across clients. So they turn it into an offer.

They write the deliverable: a deployed support agent, a configured knowledge base, escalation rules, and an operating runbook. They write the outcome: routine support volume deflected so the human team keeps the escalations. They draw the boundary: the core offer includes one knowledge source integration and two escalation paths; a ticketing-system integration and additional knowledge sources are priced add-ons. They set the price from the value, not from hours, and they write the entry criteria: the client supplies the knowledge sources, names an internal owner, and commits to the review cadence. [^11]

Then they build the delivery machine for the offer, once. The build method becomes a skill the team loads and follows, so every delivery runs the same way instead of reinventing the method. [^12] Each client engagement gets its own profile, an isolated environment, so one client's context and history never bleed into another's, and each client gets a clean, safe workspace. [^13] The operating cadence, the weekly health check and the utilization view, runs on a schedule, so the portfolio is checked without anyone remembering to check it. [^14] And the lessons from each delivery accumulate in memory, so the offer gets cheaper to produce and better at what it does the more it sells. [^15]

The payoff shows up in the numbers. The first delivery takes six weeks and the team learns the machine. By the fifth delivery the same offer takes four, because the method is encoded and the team is not rediscovering it. The margin holds because the price was set on value and the cost came down. The principal, meanwhile, is not touching a single delivery. They are watching the offer's cost, margin, and sales count in the portfolio view, and they are deciding which service line to productize next. [^16]

That is the whole lesson in miniature. Capability became a service line, the service line became a priced offer, the offer got a delivery machine, and the machine turned a bespoke craft into a repeatable, margin-holding product the principal runs rather than does.

## Try it

Do not go build an entire catalog. Do the diagnosis on the work you already do.

1. Write the three service lines you would claim the practice is good at. Then test each against the three properties: is there real demand, can the team deliver it without you, and does the work repeat across clients? Mark the ones that pass all three. Those are the only candidates worth productizing.
2. Take your strongest service line and write one offer for it. Fill in all five fields: deliverable, outcome, scope boundary, price and duration, and entry criteria. Force yourself to write the scope boundary. If you cannot name what is out, you do not have an offer, you have an open-ended promise.
3. Price the offer from its outcome, not from your hours. Write the number a client would get out of the deliverable, then set a price that is a fair share of that value. If your price is still basically your rate times your hours, you have not made the pricing shift this lesson is about.
4. Name the one offer you would be embarrassed to put a fixed price on, and say why. The answer is usually the offer whose scope you cannot hold, and that is the one to either fix or drop.

If the diagnosis shows you have service lines but no offers, you have found the gap this lesson is designed to close. The catalog is the deliverable of the next stage of your work, and the next lesson tells you how to build it at the scale of a whole organization.
