# Lesson 6: Hiring, Onboarding, and Running a Team of Consultants

Lesson 1 ended with a test: you are a principal the day a client gets a good result and you were not the one who made it. Lesson 5 gave you the assets to make that possible, the reusable IP and productized offerings the practice owns. This lesson is about the people who run them. A practice scales only as fast as its people, and hiring people is the point where most principals break the delivery system they just spent a course building.

The trap is specific and worth naming up front. When a principal hires, they instinctively reach for the hiring they know: fill the role, find someone who can do the work I used to do, pick a strong individual. That instinct is the solo consultant leaking through. It treats the new person as a substitute for the principal's hands, which is exactly the mistake lesson 1 warned against. A principal does not hire a person to replace themselves. A principal hires a person to run a system the principal already built. The difference changes every decision that follows, from the job description to the interview to how you judge whether the hire worked. [^1]

## Hire for the delivery system, not the resume

The first consequence is that you stop writing job descriptions that describe a person and start writing them that describe a slot in the delivery method. This is not a semantic shift. The prior lessons built a captured method: the discovery questions, the phase exit gates, the design sign-off checklist, the handover runbook, the skills a competent executor loads and follows. [^2] Once that method exists, the role is not "someone who knows consulting." The role is "someone who can run engagement type A, the discovery-to-handover arc, to the standard the playbook defines, without the principal in the room."

That reframes the two things you actually evaluate. The first is whether the person can follow a well-defined method faithfully. The second is whether they know when to stop following it and exercise judgment, which is the part of the work that never gets fully captured. You are looking for a specific combination: a person who treats the playbook as real and load-bearing, not as a suggestion they are free to improve on day one, and who has enough independent judgment to flag the moments the playbook does not cover instead of quietly improvising past them.

Notice what is not on the list. Prior experience with your exact tooling is a nice-to-have, not the point, because the whole reason the method is captured is that a competent person can ramp onto it. Someone who arrives convinced they already know the work and does not need the method is a worse hire than someone with less experience who treats the method as the ground truth. The first is a risk to the system, because they will reinvent it. The second strengthens the system, because they run it as built and reserve judgment for the genuinely new. [^3]

## Rampability is the talent you are buying

Once hiring is about running a system, the single most valuable trait is rampability: how fast and how far a person goes from "first day" to "runs an engagement arc solo behind review gates." You cannot hire the finished consultant, because the finished consultant is a myth and, worse, is exactly the person who will not follow your method. You hire the steepest learning curve and the discipline to climb it inside the guardrails.

This is where a practice that runs on Hermes Agent has a concrete advantage worth making explicit, because it is the same idea the whole course has been building. The delivery method lives as skills, the on-demand procedural documents the agent loads and follows, plus the profiles, the isolated environments where each engagement keeps its own memory and history. [^4] A new consultant is not handed a blank desk and told to figure it out. They are handed the same captured method every engagement runs on. Their ramp is measured, not hoped for. The principal can watch whether the person is actually loading and following the method, where they struggle, and which parts of the playbook need to be better written to carry a less experienced person.

If you want a blunt measure of your onboarding system, ask this: could I onboard a competent person with no consulting background onto my most standard offer, and would they be billable after a defined window? If the honest answer is no, that is not a hiring failure, it is a codification failure. Your method is not actually captured if it requires the principal's implicit knowledge to run. The team is the sharpest test of whether your IP is real. [^5]

## Onboarding is codification in action

Onboarding is not a tour of the office and a welcome lunch. It is the moment the captured method becomes the load-bearing contract for a new person, and it deserves the same engineering you put into the offer itself. That means it has stages, exit criteria, and a defined end state. It is not done when the person feels comfortable. It is done when the person can run a defined engagement arc to the standard the playbook sets, with review gates, without the principal touching a deliverable. That is the exact test from lesson 1, now applied to a hire.

Structure it in three stages, each with its own gate. Stage one is comprehension: the person reads the method, runs the playbook in a sandbox or a simulated engagement, and can explain the exit test for each phase back to you. The gate is a verbal or written demonstration that they understand the system, not that they have memorized it. Stage two is supervised execution: they run a real engagement under the principal's review, with the principal checking the gates and the handover. The gate is a completed engagement that holds up to review. Stage three is independent operation: they run an engagement solo, and the only principal involvement is the standing health cadence that runs on every engagement anyway. [^6]

The key is that the gates are the same gates the practice uses for any engagement, not new tests invented for the hire. You are not checking whether the person is good in some abstract sense. You are checking whether they can run the system, and the system has its own definition of done. That is what makes onboarding measurable and what makes it fast: the new person is learning one thing, not reinventing a methodology from scratch.

## Running the team: quality is a property of the system

With people running the method, the principal's job shifts from supervising individuals to running the machinery that keeps quality consistent regardless of who is on the engagement. This is the portfolio view from lesson 1 applied to people. The principal is no longer asking, is this consultant good. They are asking, is the review process catching problems before they reach the client, is utilization healthy, is any one person carrying an unsafe share of critical work, is quality consistent across the team.

The mechanism that makes this work is delegation with review, not delegation alone. You do not hire people and then trust them, and you do not hire people and then hover. You give them full ownership of the work inside the defined gates, and you put a review layer between the work and the client that does not depend on which individual produced it. In a Hermes-run practice that review layer is built into the operating cadence: cron drives the weekly health checks and the follow-ups that would otherwise fall off the calendar, so no engagement drifts because someone forgot to check. [^7] The principal reads the health reports, checks the gates, and intervenes at the level of the system, not the level of the keystroke. [^8]

This is the discipline most principals find hardest, and it is worth being honest about why. When a junior runs a playbook worse than you would, every instinct says step in. Stepping in is how you protect the client today and destroy the practice tomorrow, because every time you step in you confirm that quality actually depends on you, which is the thing you are trying to get away from. The principal's version of quality is not "the work is done right." It is "the system produces work done right without me." Those sound close and are not. One is a bet on the individual in front of you. The other is a bet on the machine you built. [^9]

## The key-person trap

There is one failure mode that quietly ends practices, and it is the key-person dependency. It happens when one consultant, often the most senior, becomes the only person who can run a critical offer, fix a hard client, or recover a drifting engagement. On paper the practice has a team. In reality it is a solo consultant wearing four people, and the moment that person leaves or burns out, the practice is one client conversation away from collapsing.

The principal's obligation is structural, not sentimental: every capability the practice sells must have more than one person who can run it, or the practice does not actually own that capability, it rents it from an individual. Cross-training is not a nice thing to do for the team. It is risk management on the practice's most valuable asset, its capacity. When the health cadence flags that a critical capability is single-pointed, that is a system problem to fix, not an individual problem to accept. The method, the skills, and the profiles exist precisely so that capability is held by the system and not by the person who happens to know it best. [^10]

The same logic applies to the principal. A principal who is the only one who can do a thing is a consultant with staff, whatever their title says. The day you find yourself indispensable to an engagement, you have found a gap in the delivery system and your job is to close it, not to enjoy being needed.

## Worked example

Put it on one practice. A principal built the machine the earlier lessons describe: a captured delivery method in skills, per-engagement profiles, a cron-driven health cadence, and two productized offers. [^11] The business is good enough that they need a third consultant, and they have to staff the hire deliberately or the whole system wobbles.

They write the role as a slot in the delivery method, not as a person. The requirement is not "five years consulting experience." It is "can run a standard engagement arc to the playbook standard, alone behind review gates, by the end of week eight." They interview for rampability and for discipline with the method. They ask one candidate what they would do if the playbook does not cover a client situation. The candidate who says "I would flag it to whoever owns the method and follow their call" is hired over the candidate who says "I would figure it out myself." The second one, however impressive, is a bet that the person will reinvent the system, and that is a bet the principal has decided not to make.

Onboarding runs in the three stages. Weeks one and two are comprehension: the new person reads the method, runs the discovery phase on a simulated engagement, and explains the exit gates back. Weeks three through six are supervised: they run a real engagement under review, and the principal checks each gate. Weeks seven and eight are independent: they run an engagement solo, and the only principal touch is the standing health report. The gate for onboarding is the same gate as any engagement: the client gets a good result and the principal was not the one who made it. [^12]

Somewhere in month four, the principal's weekly health scan flags that one consultant is the only person who has ever run the new productized offer end to end. That is a key-person dependency in the making. The principal does not wait for a problem. They rotate the second consultant onto the offer for the next engagement, supervised, so the capability moves from one person to the system. When the senior consultant takes a long leave six months later, the practice does not miss a beat, because the offer was never actually held by one person. The practice kept running while its people changed. That is what running a team at scale means. [^13]

## Try it

You do not need a team to do this lesson. You need the delivery system from the earlier lessons, and a willingness to look at your own dependency cold.

1. Write the job description for your next hire as a slot in the delivery method, not a person. List the two things you actually evaluate: faithfulness to the method, and judgment at the gaps. If you cannot write the slot, you do not have a method captured enough to hire against. That is the finding, and it is the one that matters.
2. Write your onboarding exit gate. Be specific: what engagement arc, run to what standard, behind what review, by what date. If you cannot name the gate, your onboarding is a vibe and your hires are a gamble.
3. Map every capability the practice sells to the people who can run it. Highlight any that have exactly one. That list is your key-person risk, and it is the thing to fix before it becomes a crisis.
4. Count the things that would stop if you were out for two months. Each one is a hole in the delivery system, not a sign of your importance. Closing them is the job.

If the exercise exposes more dependencies than you expected, that is not a failure. It is the exact list of work the next few lessons exist to remove, starting with the operational layer that makes utilization and capacity visible enough to staff against.

## What's next

You can now hire for the delivery system, onboard people onto it with a measurable gate, and run a team so quality survives any single person. The next lesson moves from the people to the numbers that tell you whether the whole thing is working: delivery operations, covering utilization, capacity, and margins. Where this lesson built the team that runs the method, the next one builds the operating view that lets you staff it correctly, see when people are billing and when they are not, size the team against the pipeline, and know whether the practice is actually making money on the work it does. That is the layer that turns a team that functions into a business you can steer.
