# Lesson 18: The Financial Model: Units, Margins, and Runway

Lesson 17 scaled a practice by measuring the distance between the principal and the work product, and it ended with the founder of Delta Forge freed from his own delivery so he could build the next rung instead of billing the current hour. That is an inspiring place to stop and a dangerous place to start from, because a founder who has scaled away his busywork still needs cash to fund the scaling. The financial model is what sits between the founder's ambition and the firm's ability to pay for it. It is the translation of the delivery system you have spent this course building into numbers that tell you what the firm can sell, what each sale is worth after delivery, and how long the firm can keep going when sales stop. Without it, the compounding choice from Lesson 17 is a sentiment, not a decision. With it, the compounding choice is a line item you can fund on purpose.

This is the lesson where the practice stops feeling like a big job and starts feeling like a machine with an input, an output, and a cash buffer. The three names in the title are the three dials. Units are what the practice actually sells, the smallest sellable increment of value, and each unit has its own economics. Margins are what each unit leaves behind after delivery, and how those margins aggregate across the whole catalog into what the firm can actually spend. Runway is the months of survival the cash buffer buys, and it is the number that decides every strategic choice the firm makes. Read them in that order, because each one feeds the next, and the advanced skill is not calculation, it is knowing which number decides which decision.

## The unit is the building block

Most principals model their business in annual revenue and annual profit, two yearly totals that tell you almost nothing about how to act. The unit discipline replaces those totals with the smallest thing the practice sells, and it forces the economics down to a level you can actually change. A unit is a defined, sellable increment of value with a price, a delivery cost, and a contribution. The catalog from the earlier lessons is not a list of services. It is a portfolio of units, and each unit has its own numbers. No two units in a consulting catalog, and no two pricing models from Lesson 8, share the same economics.

For the practice this course has been building, the catalog has three kinds of units, and they are worth naming because each has a different financial shape. The flagship deliverable, the productized build from Lesson 8, is a fixed-price unit: one price, a defined scope, a bounded delivery. The engagement revenue is a function of units sold, not hours burned, which is the whole point of productization. The smaller diagnostic engagement is a different unit, cheaper, faster, with its own contribution. And the retained support offer, the monthly availability that keeps a client's automation running, is a unit sold by the month, which makes it the seed of the recurring revenue the next lesson builds on. Three units, three prices, three delivery costs, three contributions. The practice does not have one margin. It has a margin per unit.

The per-unit economics are the language the whole lesson speaks, so define them precisely. For each unit: price is what the client pays. Variable delivery cost is what it costs to produce the unit that scales with the number of units, the compute, the licenses, the direct delivery time, the review hours that attach to that engagement. Contribution is price minus variable delivery cost, and it is the money each unit contributes toward covering the practice's fixed costs and building its runway. [^2] Contribution is the number that matters, because it is the number you can act on per sale. Revenue tells you how big the deal was. Contribution tells you how much of it the practice keeps to live on.

The reason the unit is the building block and not the profit-and-loss statement is that the unit is where the levers live. You cannot act directly on annual profit, because it is the output of a hundred decisions you did not make consciously. You can act on a single unit's price, a single unit's delivery cost, a single unit's sales volume. The financial model is built from the bottom up, unit by unit, and the practice that models at the unit level can answer the questions that actually come up: if I raise this offer's price, what happens to contribution; if I automate part of this delivery, what happens to the unit margin; if I sell one more of this unit, how much does the firm keep. Aggregate revenue modeling cannot answer any of those, because it has already collapsed the units together.

## Margins: the per-unit contribution and the practice margin

Contribution at the unit level is the first margin, but it is not the whole picture, and conflating the two margins is a common and expensive mistake. The unit contribution is what a single sale leaves after its own variable costs. The practice margin is what the whole firm leaves after all the fixed costs that do not scale with units: the leads' salaries, the infrastructure the delivery system runs on, the insurance, the legal and administrative base, the sales and marketing spend. Unit contribution pays the fixed costs, and practice margin is what is left over. A practice can have healthy per-unit contribution and a negative practice margin, if its fixed-cost base is too heavy for the volume of units it sells. The two margins are read together, never one alone, and the relationship between them is the whole firm in one line: practice margin equals total contribution minus fixed cost.

This is where the ladder from Lesson 17 meets the money. The margin structure is different at each stage of the ladder, and the financial model has to be rebuilt as the firm climbs, not assumed to scale linearly. At the solo stage the picture is simple and seductive: high contribution per billable hour, because there is almost no fixed cost and the principal keeps nearly everything the client pays. But the solo's revenue is bounded by one calendar, and its contribution falls to zero the moment the principal is not billing, on holiday, between engagements, or doing the scaling work that produces no bill. High margin per unit, capped volume, and the whole margin collapses to nothing in any quiet month. That is why the solo practice has the best-looking unit economics and the least resilient business.

At the team stage the picture inverts, and this is the honest reckoning every scaling founder has to make. Adding leads and infrastructure raises the fixed-cost base, which lowers the margin on every unit, because each unit's contribution now has to cover its share of a larger overhead. The per-unit margin is thinner. What the practice gains in exchange is volume and continuity: many concurrent units instead of one calendar, and units that keep producing contribution in months when the founder is building the next rung rather than billing. The solo practice has fat margins and no volume in the quiet months. The team practice has thinner margins and contribution that keeps arriving while the founder is out of the building. The financial model is the tool that makes this trade, thinner unit margin for a durable practice margin, legible enough to choose on purpose rather than to drift into.

Set the fixed-cost base deliberately, because it is the second lever on the practice margin and the firm designs it, unlike the per-unit cost which is partly set by the market. The fixed base is how much the practice must sell each month just to stand still, its break-even. [^2] A lean fixed base, a small team and shared infrastructure, gives a long runway and a low break-even, at the cost of capacity. A heavy fixed base buys capacity and continuity, at the cost of a high break-even that every quiet month forces the firm to cover. Neither is right or wrong; the principal chooses the fixed base that matches the revenue the units can honestly generate, and the mistake is letting the base grow to whatever the past revenue happened to support, because the base is what the firm must carry in the month when revenue does not.

## Contribution can be produced, not just discovered

The advanced insight of the margin section, and the one that connects this lesson to the IP work of Lesson 5, is that contribution is a number you can deliberately push up by lowering the unit's variable delivery cost, and the thing that lowers that cost is the captured asset. Every unit in the catalog that runs on the practice's captured method, templates, and tooling costs less to deliver than a unit that reinvents them. At the same price, that lower cost is wider contribution, and it flows straight to the practice margin and the runway. In a practice running on Hermes, the captured IP is not an abstract asset, it is a loadable skill that the delivery workers pull into the path of the work, so the standard and the method ride along with the task instead of being reconstructed fresh on each engagement. [^5] Each reuse is a unit delivered at a lower variable cost, and the compounding is the same compounding the earlier lessons described, now showing up as a number.

This is also why the pricing lesson and the margin lesson are the same argument. Lesson 8 said you do not widen margin by raising price, you widen it by lowering delivery cost. Today's lesson is that statement turned into a model: the lower variable cost per unit is a higher contribution per unit, and contribution aggregates into practice margin and runway. The unit that runs on IP is the margin machine, and a practice that prices its units from value and delivers them on captured IP has engineered contribution at the unit level, which is the most reliable margin there is, because it does not depend on negotiating a better rate in any given month.

## Runway: the number that funds the compounding choice

Contribution and margin answer whether the practice is healthy at a point in time. Runway answers how long it can survive a stop, and it is the number that decides the strategic choices, which is why it is third and last and why it is the one most practices never compute. Runway is the number of months the firm can continue operating on the cash it has, before it runs out, at its current net burn. The standard form is simple: cash divided by net monthly burn. [^6] The treatment of "net" is where the precision lives. Gross burn is the monthly fixed cost, what it costs to keep the doors open. Net burn subtracts the contracted contribution that is still arriving, so a firm with a healthy book of retained and in-flight work burns less than one with an empty pipeline. Runway is a forward number, a projection, and it is only as honest as the revenue you assume keeps landing.

Runway is the number that decides the Lesson 17 choice, and that is the whole reason it matters enough for its own slot in this course. Option A was the founder taking the busy quarter and billing it personally, because it pays the bills. Option B was the founder investing the quarter in the next rung of the ladder and the next offer, because it builds the firm. Any founder would say they prefer option B. The reason most of them take option A is not preference, it is runway. A firm with three months of cash cannot afford to spend a quarter teaching a second reviewer or packaging a new offer, because if revenue stalls, three months do not cover the investment. A firm with twelve months of cash can take the quarter and still pay the team, because the buffer absorbs the time the investment takes to pay back. Runway is the budget that makes the compounding choice available, and the principal who wants to build the firm instead of the busy quarter has to buy that budget in advance with margin.

Set a runway floor and treat it as a governance number, the way the earlier lessons set a review cadence. [^4] A common discipline is to hold the buffered months above a floor below which the firm stops investing in non-delivery work. The concrete guidance in the startup-finance world is that when runway falls below roughly nine to twelve months, the founder should already be addressing it, and below six months the strategic options narrow sharply, because the firm has lost room to make anything other than survival decisions. [^7] The consulting parallel is direct: the runway floor is the boundary between a practice that can invest in its ladder and a practice that must bill everything it can in case the pipeline dries up. Below the floor, the compounding choice is not a choice, it is a luxury the firm cannot afford, and the honest principal treats it that way rather than pretending a three-month runway leaves room for a quarter of ladder-building.

The sophisticated frame, and the note to carry forward, is that runway is not a passive survival number, it is a margin decision made in advance. You do not discover runway in a crisis quarter. You design it every month by pricing units from value, by keeping the fixed base proportionate to honest revenue, by booking the retained and recurring units that keep net burn low, and by banking contribution instead of spending it the month it arrives. Runway is the accumulated output of the whole financial model, and a principal who reads all three dials, contribution per unit, practice margin after fixed costs, and months of runway, on a schedule, is running a practice instead of being run by one.

## The Delta Forge financial model

Return to the firm from Lesson 17 and build its financial model, because the last lesson handed you a founder with recovered capacity and nowhere yet to spend it, and this lesson is where that capacity gets a price. Delta Forge, at the end of the scaling year, sells three units. The flagship productized build carries a fixed price of one hundred twenty thousand dollars and a variable delivery cost of seventy thousand, so each build contributes fifty thousand. The diagnostic audit plus pilot carries a fixed price of forty-five thousand, a delivery cost of twenty-two thousand, and contributes twenty-three thousand per unit. The retained support offer, the monthly availability that keeps a fleet of automation running, sells at fifteen thousand a month, costs six thousand a month to deliver, and contributes nine thousand a month on each retainer. That is the unit catalog, and it is the foundation of everything that follows.

| Unit | Price | Delivery cost | Contribution per unit | Units per year | Annual contribution |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Automation build | $120,000 | $70,000 | $50,000 | 6 | $300,000 |
| Audit plus pilot | $45,000 | $22,000 | $23,000 | 8 | $184,000 |
| Retained support | $15,000/mo | $6,000/mo | $9,000/mo | 1 (12 mo) | $108,000 |

Add the column. Total annual revenue is six builds at one twenty, 720,000, plus eight audits at forty-five, 360,000, plus one retainer at fifteen a month, 180,000, for about 1.26 million, which is the firm's actual trailing revenue from Lesson 17. Total annual contribution is three hundred thousand from builds, one eighty-four from audits, one hundred eight from the retainer, about 592,000 a year. That contribution has to carry the fixed base: two senior leads at one fifty each, a junior at eighty, the agent infrastructure at thirty, insurance, legal, and admin at twenty, and a marketing and sales allocation of twenty, for a fixed base near 450,000 a year. Practice margin is contribution minus fixed, about 142,000 a year, and per unit the blended contribution ratio is roughly 47 cents of every dollar of revenue survives to the fixed base. That is a healthy productized model, and it is only legible because it was built unit by unit.

Now the runway, and this is where the model stops being arithmetic and becomes strategy. Suppose Delta Forge has a cash buffer of 180,000, which is modest, roughly three months of its fixed base. Its gross monthly burn, the fixed cost divided by twelve, is about 37,500. In a normal month its net burn is heavily negative in the good direction, because contracted contribution from the retainer and the in-flight units arrives and covers the base. The honest stress case is the survival number: if every current engagement ended and no new one closed, net burn is the full 37,500, and runway is 180,000 divided by 37,500, about 4.8 months. [^6] That is below the six-month line where the options narrow, and below the nine to twelve where the founder should already have addressed it. [^7] The discovery is not that Delta Forge is failing. It is that a firm with healthy unit economics and a 142,000 practice margin has a dangerously thin buffer, which means the founder's just-recovered capacity has one correct first job, and it is not to build a fourth rung or chase a new offer. It is to convert part of that healthy contribution into a larger cash buffer, and to book the retained and recurring units that keep net burn low, so the runway floor stops being a constraint on the next strategic choice.

Run the compounding decision through this model to see why the financials and the strategy are the same conversation. The founder wants a quarter to develop a second lead to the internal bar, which is the Lesson 17 option B. That quarter costs roughly 60,000 in time the leads would otherwise have contributed, and it produces no billable unit. With a 4.8-month runway, spending the 60,000 and a quarter of lead time is a real bet the firm cannot comfortably afford if a build slips, and the honest founder either defers it, takes a smaller version, or first spends part of the 142,000 practice margin banking a buffer that buys the runway to make the bet. The margin is the fuel and the runway is the tank. A practice with high contribution but a thin tank cannot take the compounding bet, no matter how much it believes in it, and the financial model is what tells the founder to refill the tank before spending the fuel.

## The review, not the calculation

The financial model is a living instrument and it decays the moment it is built, which is why the last skill is the cadence and not the formula. Contribution per unit moves when the fixed-price offer's scope drifts or the delivery cost falls as more of the catalog runs on captured IP. The practice margin moves when the fixed base changes. Runway moves every month that revenue and spend resolve one way and not the other. A model computed once in a spreadsheet and reopened at year end is not a model, it is an autopsy. The discipline, identical to the operating review of Lesson 7, is to read all three dials on a fixed schedule against the same period, and to let the scheduled review, not the quiet hour that never comes, be the thing that forces the reading. In a delivery system running on Hermes, that cadence is a scheduled job that fires the contribution, margin, and runway review on its own and reports the numbers to the owners, so the model is re-read whether anyone remembers to or not. [^4]

The distinction that closes the lesson is between modeling and forecasting, because the two get tangled. Modeling is building the unit-by-unit structure, the prices, the costs, the contributions, the fixed base, the runway formula, so that you can answer what-if questions: what happens to the practice if the audit price rises, if one lead leaves, if a build takes twice as long, if the retainer book doubles. Forecasting is guessing the inputs, how many builds you will actually sell next quarter, and it is always wrong in the details. The craft is to keep the model rigorous and the forecast humble, to spend your attention on the structure you control, the margin and the runway, rather than on pretending the revenue forecast is precise. A principal who models honestly and forecasts humbly knows the practice is solvent to its runway, and that is a much better thing to know than a confident revenue number that turns out to be wrong.

## Try it

Build the financial model of your own practice, or of a practice you advise, on real numbers, and let the runway be the discovery.

1. List your units. Write down every distinct thing you actually sell, not every client but every unit type, and for each one name the price, the variable delivery cost, and the contribution. If you cannot name the cost per unit because you have never separated delivery cost from the rest, that separation is the first deliverable of this lesson, because you cannot read margin on a unit you cannot cost.
2. Compute your blended contribution ratio. Total contribution divided by total revenue. If it is thin, find the unit dragging it down, the one whose price is set below its value or whose delivery runs on no captured IP, and name the single change, price or cost, that raises it most.
3. Separate the two margins. Write down your fixed base, the costs that do not move with units, and compute practice margin as total contribution minus fixed base. If it is thin or negative despite healthy per-unit contribution, you have a fixed-base problem, not a pricing problem, and the lever is the base, not the rate.
4. Compute your runway. Cash divided by monthly net burn, with the stress case being the full fixed burn with no new revenue. [^6] Compare it to the six-month and nine-to-twelve month lines. [^7] If you are below them, the honest conclusion is that your next investment is not a new offer or the next rung, it is banking the buffer and booking recurring units that lower the net burn.
5. Put the review on the calendar, as a scheduled job if you have the machine for it, so the contribution, margin, and runway numbers are re-read on a cadence whether a quiet hour appears or not. [^4]

The test of the lesson is not whether you can state the formulas. It is whether you can look at your runway and know, unambiguously, whether the compounding choice from Lesson 17 is one you can fund this quarter or one you must first earn the runway to make.

## What's next

You now have the practice's financial skeleton: the units it sells and their per-unit contribution, the practice margin after its fixed base, and the runway that decides whether the compounding choice is available. The model this lesson built treats every unit as if it were sold freshly each time, a stream of discrete engagements, which is why runway needs a buffer at all. The next lesson changes that assumption at the root. It moves from the financial model of discrete units to long-term client partnerships and recurring revenue: what happens to unit economics when a portion of contribution is contracted by the month instead of sold one engagement at a time. Where this lesson bought runway with a cash buffer, the next lesson buys it with predictability, and it shows how the retained unit that closed this lesson's model becomes the foundation of a firm whose net burn stops being a crisis number and starts being a contract. That is the final stage of the financial arc, and it is where the practice stops living quarter to quarter and starts owning a book.
