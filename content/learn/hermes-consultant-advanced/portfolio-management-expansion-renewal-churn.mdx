# Lesson 11: Managing a Client Portfolio Strategically

Lesson 10 made the practice safe to grow: it taught you to know your obligations and to decide, deliberately, which risks you carry. This lesson assumes the list is done and puts the growth itself in your hands. Most people hear "grow the practice" and picture the sales pipe: more proposals out, more logos in, a longer pipeline chart. That instinct is half right and half a trap. A practice that grows by signing whatever it can stays busy, but stays busy doing the wrong mix of work. The revenue it needs, the revenue that becomes durable and compoundable, does not come from the new business. It comes from the accounts already in the portfolio: the ones you expand, the ones you renew, and the ones you let walk away on purpose.

This lesson is the account-level management that sits above the engagement-level delivery of the earlier course. You already know how to run an engagement well. The portfolio skill is deciding which engagements, and which relationships, deserve more of a scarce resource: the delivery capability you can point at any one client. That allocation decision is the strategic core of a principal's job, and it is almost always where growing practices quietly fail. The working example throughout uses Hermes Agent, because a portfolio of engagements is a concrete system you can monitor and steer, and the practice that manages it well does so with the same machinery it uses to deliver. [^1]

## Why the portfolio is the real revenue engine

Start by correcting a common accounting. A consulting practice's revenue in any period is the sum of three things: the expansion of existing accounts, the renewal of existing engagements and contracts, and the new business it signs. New business matters most when you are starting out, because there is nothing else. As the practice matures, the balance shifts, and a principal with a few years of history should be able to watch the first two lines carry more and more of the total. That is not a theory. It is arithmetic. Every renewal you hold is a sale you do not have to make. Every expansion of an existing account is a logo you do not have to win again. Growth that comes from the base is vastly cheaper than growth that comes from new logos, because base growth does not carry the acquisition cost, the sales cycle, or the risk that a brand-new client is a bad fit.

The trap is that the base does not grow by itself. Engagements expire. Contracts renew or they lapse. Accounts drift. A client who loved you at handover feels neutral six months later, then forgets you exist, then gets a phone call from a competitor who did not forget. The base is not an asset you own; it is a set of relationships that decay unless something renews them. The principal's job is to be that something, deliberately. You are not waiting to see which accounts stay. You are deciding which ones are worth the attention that will keep them, and spending that attention where the base will pay you back. [^2]

## The three levers, and what each one costs

The portfolio has three levers, and each is a different kind of work. Naming them separately matters because a principal who treats them as one fuzzy activity, "grow the accounts," will do none of them well.

Expansion is selling more into an account you already serve: a second engagement, a broader scope, a productized offering the client now qualifies for. It is the highest-margin lever, because the relationship, the trust, and the institutional memory of the client's business already exist; you are not paying to establish them again. But expansion has a condition. It only works when the work merits it. You can only expand an account if the delivery capability you would point at it is genuinely the right fit, and if expanding it lets you allocate that capability better than some alternative use. Expansion is not a reward for having the client. It is a judgment that more of your scarce delivery capability should live on that account, and the judgment has to survive the comparison against every other place that capability could go.

Renewal is the decision and the work of keeping an engagement or a contract going when its current term ends. The naive view is that renewal is automatic: the client was happy, so of course they renew. The honest view is that renewal is earned continuously and signed at the end. Happiness at handover is a current balance that depreciates; the client renews what they believe will still be valuable next term, and that belief is built or lost in the months when nothing urgent is happening. Renewal work is the cadence of staying useful: periodic check-ins, surfacing ideas before the client asks, proving value in the quiet periods, and beginning the renewal conversation early enough that it is a negotiation over terms, not a race to remind the client you exist. A renewal that is discussed three weeks before the contract ends is not a renewal; it is a referendum on whether you vanished. [^3]

Churn is the inverse, and it is the lever most principals manage by accident. Churn is the set of accounts that end: those that lapse because you did not renew them, and those you decide to end yourself. The discipline of the portfolio is to stop treating churn as the enemy. Some churn is the best news of the year, because the account was costing more capacity than it returned, and letting it go frees capability for an account that will pay. A practice that never churns an account is a practice that has never taken a clear-eyed look at whether every account earns its place. The skill is to make churn a decision, not a discovery: to know, account by account, which ones you are actively keeping and which ones you are letting wind down, and to have a reason for each. [^4]

## The scarce resource underneath

What makes the portfolio a strategy rather than a ledger is that all three levers compete for the same bounded thing: delivery capability. A principal has a team, a set of agents, a methodology, and a finite number of hours that can be pointed at client work. That capability is the practice's scarcest asset, more than cash, because it is what converts every other decision into delivered value. Every choice in this lesson is ultimately an allocation of that capability. Expand an account and you have committed capability to it, which means less of it somewhere else. Renew an account and you have reserved a slot on the team and the machinery for another term. Keep an account that barely earns its place and you have quietly taxed every other account that could have used the same people.

This is the lens that turns the levers into trade-offs instead of separate tasks. The usual failure is to run them as if each brought its own capacity. A solo consultant takes any client that calls, because their own hours are the only truly scarce thing and there is no opportunity cost they feel. A principal has a team and the work they do competes. That is why a principal cannot make portfolio decisions greedily, taking every expansion and renewing every account and chasing every new logo. The portfolio is a closed system. Every yes inside it is a no somewhere else, and the strategic question, the one this whole lesson is about, is whether you are saying yes and no in the right places. [^5]

## The account as the unit of analysis

The engagement was the unit of the delivery work. For the portfolio, the unit changes to the account: the whole relationship, all its past and current and potential engagements, with one client. The reason the unit has to be the account is that the levers cross engagement boundaries. You expand an account by signing a second engagement, but you decide to based on the whole relationship. You renew an account even as specific engagements inside it start and end. A client who hires you for one project is an account; the one who hires you for three and refers you to two peers is the same account with a different shape. Managing at the account level means you track more than a current engagement's health. You track the relationship's trajectory: where it started, where it is now, which direction it is heading, and what it could become.

Every account needs an owner, a person who is accountable for the whole relationship and not just the deliverable currently in flight. This is a governance choice, and it matters most at scale, where relationships outnumber principals. In a practice that runs on agents, the account is a natural boundary: each account, and each engagement within it, runs in its own isolated profile, so the client's context and history accumulate in one place, kept clean and separate from every other client. [^6] The account owner reads that context, sees the whole arc, and makes the expansion, renewal, and churn calls with the full picture in front of them rather than from whatever memo the current engagement happened to produce. That is the portfolio version of the isolation and audit discipline from Lessons 3 and 9, aimed at the relationship instead of the sprint. [^7]

## Expansion: the discipline of earning more

Expansion looks like the easiest lever to pull, and it is the easiest to get wrong, because the temptation is to expand an account for its own sake, as a reward for a good engagement. The discipline is to expand only where the next engagement is genuinely the best use of scarce capability, and to time it deliberately.

The opportunity for expansion usually appears mid-engagement, when you are inside the client's business and can see adjacent problems your current work did not cover. That is the moment to plant the flag: to surface the adjacent problem, show how it connects to the work you are already doing, and frame the next engagement as the natural extension rather than a separate sale. The client is receptive precisely because they are already seeing value. The alternative, waiting until handover and then pitching something new, means you have lost the momentum and the inside view that made expansion cheap.

The judgment underneath is whether the expansion improves the portfolio, not just the month's revenue. An account that can absorb a second engagement with the same team and the same machinery, with reuse of what the first engagement already built, is a compounding account; each engagement makes the next cheaper to deliver. An account that pulls your team into a brand-new domain, with new learning and new risk, is expansion only in name; it is closer to new business wearing familiar clothes. The strategic test is reuse. When the next engagement can lean on the skills, templates, and delivery method you already captured, the marginal cost falls and the margin rises, which is exactly the leverage economics from Lesson 1 doing their work. [^8] Expand where the base makes you more efficient, and you are building compound improvement. Expand where it does not, and you have just created a new-client project with an old client's name on it. [^9]

## Renewal: the work of being worth renewing

Renewal is the least glamorous lever and the one the base actually runs on, so it deserves the most respect. The core idea is that renewal is decided during the term, not at the term's end, and the work is proving value on a cadence rather than hoping the last handover carried the relationship.

A principal treats each account as a value stream with a rhythm. The value you delivered at the last handover is a liability that depreciates; what the client will renew is their sense that you are still worth it, and that sense is built in the months when nothing is on fire. The discipline is a recurring touch that keeps the account warm and the value visible: a periodic review of what you delivered, what changed in their business since, and what is worth doing next, delivered on a schedule so it happens whether or not anyone remembers and whether or not there is a crisis. [^10] This is not newsletter spam. It is an account owner using the practice's machinery to show up, with substance, on a cadence that makes staying with you the path of least resistance.

The renewal conversation itself deserves to start early. It is a strategic conversation, not an administrative one, and its purpose is to re-sell the relationship to a client whose business has probably changed since the original sale. You are not asking, will you sign again. You are establishing, where does the value we delivered sit relative to what you are trying to accomplish now, and what does the next term need to contain so it is worth doing. When that conversation happens months before the term ends, the renewal is the natural outcome of an ongoing argument you won. When it happens weeks before, you are negotiating without leverage against a client who is deciding, from memory, whether you were worth it. [^11]

## Churn: the lever you steer

Churn completes the set, and it is the one that separates a strategic principal from a reactive one. The healthy starting assumption is that not every account should renew, and that a practice that keeps every account is over-allocating capability to accounts that do not earn it.

There are two kinds of churn, and it is worth distinguishing them because the reaction differs. Involuntary churn is the account that leaves despite your best renewal work: the client's budget was cut, the champion left, the company was acquired, the strategy shifted. Involuntary churn teaches you about concentration risk, which Lesson 10 named: when one account is a large share of revenue, its departure is a shock you could have sized in advance. The response is not moral outrage at the client. It is the recognition that some churn is external and the only control is not to have bet the practice on a single point of failure.

The second kind is intentional churn, and it is the strategic lever. An account can be worth deliberately winding down when it consumes more capability than it returns: it demands disproportionate attention, it buys the lowest-margin work, it pulls your team into a domain that does not compound. Intentional churn is hard precisely because it is a no to revenue, and every instinct says revenue is good. But revenue is not good independent of what it costs to produce. An account that ties up your best delivery capability on low-margin work is actively robbing the accounts that would use that capability well. Letting it go is not a loss. It is a reallocation, and it is the same discipline, applied to accounts, that a product manager applies to features. [^12]

The honest challenge is whether you can name your intentional churn. If you cannot point to an account you deliberately ended in the last year, and why, then every account that ended was either an accident or a surprise, and neither is a strategy. The practice that churns on purpose understands its own economics. The practice that churns by drift does not know which accounts it is actually bet on.

## The portfolio cadence

Like compliance and delivery, portfolio management is not a state you reach. It is a cadence you run, and at practice scale it has to run on the machinery rather than on anyone's memory, because the accounts outnumber the attention and the levers do not fire themselves.

The shape of it is a regular review, on a fixed schedule, that walks the whole portfolio account by account and answers the three questions for each: should this account expand, should it renew, and should it be kept at all. The review surfaces every account's current state and trajectory so the principal allocates attention to the exceptions, the accounts where the answer is not obvious, rather than re-deriving the whole portfolio from scratch each time. This is the portfolio view, the single pane over the whole base that replaced the single-project view back in Lesson 1, and it is the instrument the principal actually steers with. [^13]

A practice built on Hermes Agent makes this cadence concrete, because the portfolio is data the system already holds. Each account and engagement lives in its isolated profile with its own sessions, history, and state, so the record of what happened on every account is real and searchable rather than scattered across people's memories. [^6] The recurring review is a scheduled job that gathers the portfolio, checks which accounts are approaching a term boundary, and delivers the working list to the account owners on a rhythm, so no renewal deadline and no flagging account waits for someone to remember it. [^10] The principal reads that periodic output the way they read every other scheduled exception report, and they spend their judgment on the accounts where the trajectory is not obvious. The cadence is what turns the three levers from intentions into a system that produces them. [^14]

## The principal's place

Put it together and the portfolio discipline reads as a single loop, run on a schedule, that a principal owns. The loop is: know every account as a whole relationship, not just its current engagement; ask the three questions, expand, renew, churn, for each; allocate scarce delivery capability according to the answers; and run the review often enough that the base decays without stewarding it for no one.

What the principal does not do is any of it by hand or on hope. They do not keep the accounts in their head, because a mature portfolio is too large for that and too important to lose when a person changes. They do not wait for renewal deadlines to arrive. They build the cadence that brings every account up for review on schedule, and they read the exceptions. And they hold the hardest discipline of all, which is accepting that some accounts are worth leaving behind. The practice that keeps every client is not being loyal. It is being unable to make the allocation call that the strategic part of the role exists to make. The practice that churns on purpose, and expands and renews where the base pays back, is the one whose revenue compounds instead of perishing at every term boundary.

## Worked example

Put the loop on a real practice. A principal runs a delivery team on Hermes Agent with an account base of eight clients. For two years the practice grew opportunistically, taking the work that came in, and it is busy. It is also stuck: revenue flatlines, the schedule is always full, and the principal cannot say which accounts are actually building the practice and which are just filling it. They decide to manage the portfolio instead of letting it happen.

First they assign an owner to each account and make the account the unit. Every client already runs in its own isolated profile, so the context and history are there; the change is that an owner now reads the whole arc and is accountable for the relationship, not just the deliverable in flight. [^6] Next they stand up the cadence: a scheduled portfolio review that runs on a fixed rhythm, gathers every account, flags which are approaching a term boundary, and delivers the working list of expansion, renewal, and churn decisions to the owners. [^10]

Now the loop has something real to decide. Three accounts are clear renewals: they buy high-margin work, they compound, the team delivers them cheaper each time because the method is captured and reused. [^8] Two accounts are expansion candidates: mid-engagement their owners surfaced adjacent problems, and the principals see that a second engagement on each can lean on what is already built. Those get the expansion push. Two accounts are healthy and renew on the normal cadence.

One account, the fifth-largest by revenue, is the tell. It buys the lowest-margin work, it pulls the senior team into a domain that does not compound, and the account owner reports that every engagement starts from scratch and drains attention. The revenue looks good on paper, but in capability terms it is a tax on the practice. The principal names it for intentional churn: a deliberate wind-down, a clear handover, an honest conversation that the practice will not pursue the next term, and the senior capability it frees gets reallocated to the two expansion accounts that will turn it into compounding margin. The churn is not a failure. It is the allocation decision the whole exercise exists to surface.

Within two quarters the numbers tell the story. Revenue does not collapse, because the expanded accounts absorb the freed capability and earn more per hour than the churned account ever did. Renewal rate climbs, because the accounts being stewarded are worth renewing and the renewal conversations started early. And for the first time the principal can say which accounts carry the practice: three compounding renewals, two actively expanding, two steady, one deliberately let go. That is a portfolio being managed, not a pipeline being waited on, and it is the whole difference this lesson teaches.

## Try it

You do not need eight clients to run the loop. You need an honest map of the base you already have.

1. Draw your account map. List every client or engagement you currently serve. For each, write the whole relationship, not just the current deliverable: how long you have served them, what you have delivered across all their work, and where the relationship is heading.
2. Put each account in a bucket. Which are your compounding accounts, delivering cheaper and better each time? Which are expansion candidates, with adjacent work you could earn? Which are steady-but-stable, worth renewing on the normal cadence? Which are quietly draining capability and returning the least?
3. Name your churn. Can you point to an account you deliberately ended in the last year, and why? If you cannot, every departure was an accident, and that is worth sitting with.
4. Pick the single reallocation. Decide on the one account where you ended the exercise unsure it still earns its place, and ask what the capability it holds would be worth pointed at your strongest expansion opportunity instead. That one comparison is the whole strategy in miniature.

If the map shows a busy practice that cannot say which accounts carry it, you have found the gap this lesson builds. The fix is not more sales effort. It is the loop: account owners, a cadence that reviews the whole base on schedule, and the courage to spend scarce capability on the accounts that will pay it back, including the willingness to let the others go on purpose.

## What's next

You can now steer the accounts you have: which to expand, which to renew, and which to let churn. The next lesson turns from managing the existing base to winning the new business the base was never going to provide on its own, on sales and pre-sales for consulting: scoping, proposal defense, and closing. Where this lesson allocated scarce delivery capability across the portfolio, the next one asks how that capability gets sold in the first place, how you scope a proposal that survives a client's scrutiny, how you defend it when the price or the approach is challenged, and how you close a consulting sale cleanly. The portfolio decides what your capability is worth to the accounts you have. The sales lesson decides how you get to charge for it with the ones you do not.
