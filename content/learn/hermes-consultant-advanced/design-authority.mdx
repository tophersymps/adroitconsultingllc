# Lesson 4: Design Authority

Lesson 3 built the machinery. A principal runs a fleet of agents on many engagements at once, orchestrates the work, and governs it with isolation, permission, and audit. That architecture answers two questions: how work moves, and who is allowed to touch what. It does not answer the third, and the third is the one this lesson is about. Who decides what good means? A system of many agents, each producing deliverables under its own bounded context, will quickly and quietly drift in a dozen directions unless someone owns the standard of quality and has the authority to enforce it. That ownership, the reviews and the organizational standards that keep a growing system coherent, is design authority. [^1]

## The drift problem

Scaling a fleet does not multiply consistency. It multiplies inconsistency, because every new agent is a new opportunity for the same job to be done a different way. Give five agents the same open brief and you will get five structurally different designs. Each one is internally reasonable. None of them are the same, and the client does not experience five good designs. They experience one practice that cannot decide how it builds things.

The drift is not the fault of the agents. It is the absence of a standard. This is the trap that catches principals right after they build the machinery from Lesson 3. They have orchestration and governance, the work flows and nobody oversteps, and yet the quality of output starts to vary in ways nobody can point to a single cause for. One engagement ships a design with a review gate. Another ships a draft the agent marked done. One uses the shared component library. Another reimplemented it from scratch because nobody told it the library existed. Nothing failed. The system is working exactly as architected. It is just uncoordinated, because coordination is not a property of a governed system. It is a property of authority. [^2]

## Authority is not governance

It is worth being precise about the difference, because the words blur and the roles are different jobs. Governance, from Lesson 3, answers who is allowed to do what. It is the reach of each agent: what state it can touch, what tools it can call, what it is permitted to attempt. Governance bounds. [^3]

Authority answers a different question: what counts as good. It is not about reach. It is about the standard a deliverable has to meet and who has the power to say a deliverable does not meet it. You can have a perfectly governed system, every agent neatly fenced into its own context with exactly the tools it needs, and still have no authority anywhere, which produces exactly the drift described above. Governance keeps agents from harming each other. Authority keeps them from building the same thing five different ways.

The asymmetry matters. A principal can delegate governance to the architecture itself, the fences and permissions are set once and the system enforces them. Authority cannot be delegated that way. It has to be exercised, continuously, by a person or a role with the standing to reject work and the willingness to do it. The moment authority stops being exercised is the moment drift restarts. [^4]

## A standard that actually works

The first job of design authority is to set organizational standards, and setting a standard is harder than writing one down. The failure mode is the standards document: a well-written, comprehensive, completely inert file that everyone agrees describes how the practice builds things and that nobody actually follows, because following it is optional labor. A standard that lives in a document and depends on everyone choosing to read and obey it is not a standard. It is a suggestion with good intentions.

The property that makes a standard real is that the executor runs it without deciding to. The standard has to be loadable, not advisory. It has to be encoded into the mechanism the work actually runs on, so that a competent executor produces the standard output by following the method, not by remembering to follow it. This is the exact logic of codification from Lesson 1 applied to quality instead of to leverage: the standard is not a document people are asked to respect. It is a playbook the delivery system runs. In Hermes terms this is a skill, the on-demand procedural document an agent loads and follows, which is the literal mechanism for turning "the practice builds engagements this way" into something every executor does without being reminded. [^5] A standard only starts to be a standard when it is loadable. Until then it is an aspiration.

There is a second property a standard needs, and it is the one that separates a useful standard from a static one. It has to be testable. A standard you cannot check against is a standard you cannot enforce, because enforcement means being able to look at a deliverable and say, factually, whether it conforms. The design authority sets the standard and the test for it together, and the test is what turns the standard from an opinion into an organizational fact. When the test exists, conformance is not a judgment call every reviewer makes fresh. It is a check the system can run. [^6]

## Running design reviews

The second job of design authority is the design review, and the review is where authority actually gets exercised, which means it is where the whole discipline lives or dies. A review is only worth anything if it can say no, and it can only say no if it is independent of the builder.

This is the design decision that separates a real review from theater. The reviewer cannot be the builder. If the agent or person who produced the deliverable also approves it, the review is a formality, because nobody reliably rejects their own work and every reviewer is biased toward the work they are closest to. Independence is the whole value of the step. In a Hermes practice this is a distinct role: the builder produces the deliverable, and a separate reviewer, in its own context, checks it against the standard before it moves on. [^7] The separation is structural, not a matter of willpower. The builder cannot approve its own output because the approval is not in its hands. It lives in a role that exists to be skeptical.

A review that is actually a review checks three things, and it is worth naming them because most review failures come from conflating them. Correctness: does the deliverable do what it claims, does it work. Standards conformance: does it match the loadable standard, the canonical way the practice builds this kind of thing. And handoff-ability: could someone who was not in the room pick it up and run it, which is the property Lesson 3 made the unit of work depend on. A review that checks all three catches the failures that actually cost money. A review that only checks correctness catches bugs and lets the drift through, because a reimplementation can be perfectly correct and still be the fifth divergent version of the same component. [^8]

## The review cadence

Independence makes a review honest, but it does not make it happen. Reviews are work, and work does not happen reliably on memory, so the cadence has to be scheduled. The authority does not wait for a review to be requested. It runs on a timer, so that work is checked on the practice's rhythm, not on whoever happens to remember. In Hermes this is a scheduled step in the pipeline: the build hands to a review stage on a cadence, and a stalled or unreviewed deliverable surfaces as an exception rather than silently passing. [^9]

The scheduling matters for a reason beyond reliability. It changes the psychology of the system. When review is a scheduled gate that work flows through, the builder knows in advance that the output will be checked against the standard, and that knowledge does the enforcing work before the review even runs. Agents and people both build differently when they know a skeptical reviewer will see it. The standard stops being something retroactively applied at the end. It becomes the constraint the work is shaped under, because the review is known to be coming. Cadence is the mechanism that makes the standard felt throughout the process instead of only at the gate. [^10]

## The change mechanism

A standard that never changes calcifies, and a calcified standard is its own kind of failure. The practice evolves, new engagements surface better ways to build, and a standard that cannot absorb them becomes an obstacle the team works around, which is drift in a new costume. Design authority therefore has a third job, beyond setting standards and reviewing against them: managing how the standard changes.

The discipline is that the standard has a single owner and a single source of truth, and change happens deliberately, not by accretion. This is the same lesson as governance applied to the standard itself. If every engagement can quietly amend how things are done, you have many standards that disagree. If only the authority can change the standard, and changes are recorded, then the practice has one canonical method and a history of why it evolved. The accumulating record of those decisions is the institutional memory of the practice, and it is exactly the thing that makes the standard smarter over time instead of static. [^11] A practice that captures its design decisions, and revisits them with intent, builds a standard that improves. A practice that writes the standard once and forgets it builds a museum.

## The two failure modes

Design authority sits between two failure modes, and a principal needs to recognize both, because they fail in opposite directions and the temptation is to correct one by sliding into the other.

The first is the tyranny of the bottleneck. One reviewer holds the whole standard in their head, nothing ships until they personally approve it, and the authority that was meant to make the practice coherent becomes the thing that stalls it. This is the heroics trap from Lesson 3 in a review costume. The fix is not to review harder. It is to make the standard loadable and testable so that most conformance checks do not need a human at all, and to reserve the human reviewer for the genuinely novel judgments. A standard that automates the routine checks is what keeps the authority from becoming the bottleneck. [^12]

The second is anarchy. No one owns the standard, everyone reviews everything their own way, and the practice drifts back into five different versions of good. The fix is not to review more often. It is to have a single owner of the standard and a single, recorded, loadable version of it. Authority is a position. It has to be occupied. When no one occupies it, the system fills the vacuum with competing informal authorities, which is worse, because they are unaccountable.

A healthy practice has exactly one owner of the standard, a loadable and testable version of it, an independent review step on a cadence, and a deliberate change mechanism. Everything else is an implementation detail.

## Worked example

Put design authority on a practice that has grown past its coherence. A principal has, after Lesson 3, a working fleet: five engagements, each on its own profile, orchestrated and governed. The machinery is fine. The problem is the output. Deliverables are getting done, nobody is overstepping, and yet the work does not feel like it comes from one practice. Three agents built three different versions of the same shared component. One engagement ships with a review gate, another does not. The principal realizes the architecture handled reach but never handled quality. There is no standard and no one who owns one.

So they set up design authority. First, the standard. They take the way the practice builds this kind of deliverable, the sequence of decisions they used to make from experience, and encode it as a loadable skill the delivery system runs. [^5] Now every agent that builds this kind of thing follows the same method, not because it remembers to, but because the method is the thing it loads. The standard is no longer a document the team is asked to respect. It is the playbook the work runs on.

Second, the test. They define what conformance means and build a check for it, so the routine part of quality is a fact the system can verify rather than a judgment a reviewer makes fresh every time. [^6] The standard and the test are written together, because a standard you cannot check is a standard you cannot enforce.

Third, the review. They make the review a distinct role, independent of the builder, in its own context. [^7] The builder produces the deliverable. The reviewer checks it against the standard for correctness, conformance, and handoff-ability before it moves on. [^8] And they schedule the review on a cadence, so the check happens on the practice's rhythm and a stuck or unreviewed deliverable surfaces as an exception instead of passing silently. [^9]

Fourth, the change mechanism. They name one owner of the standard and record why it changes, so the practice accumulates a history of design decisions instead of drifting. [^11]

Within a quarter the effect shows up. The three divergent components stop happening, because every builder now loads the same standard and runs the same method. The review catches the drift before it reaches a client, because it is independent and scheduled. And the routine checks stopped costing a human's attention, because the testable standard automates them and the reviewer is reserved for the novel judgments. [^12] The fleet still runs five engagements. It just finally produces work that looks like it came from one practice, because for the first time there is one person who owns what good means and a mechanism that makes the rest of the system follow it.

## Try it

Do not go build a standards governance committee. Diagnose the authority in the system you already run, whatever it is.

1. Name the last five deliverables your team produced. How many different ways were the same kind of thing built? If the count is higher than one, you have drift, and the cause is not the team. It is the absence of a standard that is loadable rather than advisory.
2. Pick the standard that matters most and write how an executor would follow it without being reminded. If your only answer is a document people are asked to read, you have a suggestion, not a standard. Write it as a playbook the work runs on.
3. Ask who reviews your work and whether they are independent of the people who built it. If the reviewer is the builder, the review is theater. Name the structural change that would make it honest.
4. Write the test for your standard. If you cannot state, factually, what conformance looks like, your standard is an opinion and you cannot enforce it. Write the check.

If the diagnosis shows work that is governed but uncoordinated, you have found the gap this lesson closes. Authority is not a heavier governance layer. It is a separate job, and it is the one that makes a governed system feel designed.

## What's next

You can now keep a growing practice coherent: you can set standards that actually get followed, review work independently against them, and change them deliberately instead of letting them drift.
