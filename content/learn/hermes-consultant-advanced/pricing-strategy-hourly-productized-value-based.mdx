# Lesson 8: Pricing Strategy: From Hourly to Productized to Value-Based

Lesson 7 closed with a claim that did most of its work by assuming a decision you have not yet made. It said a price is set on the value of the outcome, not on your cost, and that you widen margin by lowering delivery cost, not by negotiating a higher rate. That is true, and it is also the point where most principals stop and hand the question back: then what do I actually charge? This lesson answers it. Pricing is the commercial decision that converts everything the earlier lessons built, packaged offers, captured IP, a delivery system with known capacity and margin, into revenue. Get it wrong and the best delivery system in the world produces work that is systematically underpriced. Get it right and the same work funds the practice and the next engagement funds the one after it. The advanced version of the skill is not learning to raise your hourly rate. It is learning to stop selling time at all, and to price each offer with the model that matches the value it delivers and the risk you are willing to carry. [^1]

## Three models, one real decision

Strip the jargon off pricing and you are left with three ways to charge for the same work: hourly, productized, and value-based. Naming them cleanly matters, because the terms get used loosely and the craft lives in the differences.

Hourly is what it sounds like. You sell a unit of your time at a rate, and the client pays for however many units the work consumes. Productized is a fixed price for a defined, bounded deliverable, the offer from Lesson 2, sold at one number regardless of the hours underneath it. Value-based is a price set against a measured outcome the client cares about, so what you are paid tracks the value the work produces rather than the effort it took or the deliverables it shipped. [^2]

Those three definitions hide the actual decision, which is not which model sounds more sophisticated but who carries which risk. Every pricing model is an allocation of risk between you and the client, and that is the lens the whole lesson runs on. Hourly hands the delivery risk to the client: if the work takes longer than expected, the client pays more. Productized moves that risk to you: you quoted a fixed number, so if the work overruns, you eat it. Value-based shares a different risk, the outcome risk, between you: if the value materializes you get paid for it, and if it does not, you get less than the effort was worth. So the choice of model is really a choice about which risks you are willing to own and how well you can price them. Most pricing mistakes are not miscalculations. They are practices carrying risk they never noticed they agreed to carry, priced as if they were carrying none. [^3]

## Why hourly is a trap, not a safe default

Hourly feels like the safe default because it is the most familiar and the easiest to quote. It is also the model that structurally caps a practice at the size of its calendar, and the principal who wants to build for scale has to see that clearly before moving off it.

The first limit is arithmetic. Revenue from hourly work equals billable hours times rate. A person has a bounded number of billable hours, so a practice that prices its core work hourly has a hard ceiling on what it can earn per person no matter how good the work is. You grow only by adding more people selling more hours, which is the hiring treadmill from Lesson 6, or by raising the rate, which is a thin lever that clients compare directly against the market. Hourly pricing commoditizes you: when you sell time at a rate, the client compares your rate to everyone else's rate and your differentiation is squeezed into a per-hour number. All the value you created through the IP of Lesson 5 is invisible to that comparison, because you are not charging for the asset, you are charging for the hours it took to use it. [^4]

The second limit is the incentive it creates, and it is the one that should bother a principal most. Hourly rewards slowness. The faster and more efficiently you deliver, the fewer hours you bill and the less you earn, which means the entire point of the earlier lessons, build IP, capture method, deliver on templates so each engagement costs less, is penalized by an hourly price. Every engagement that runs on captured IP from Lesson 5 is an engagement you just made cheaper to deliver, and under hourly pricing cheaper delivery means a smaller bill. You have built an efficiency machine and priced yourself to lose the moment it works. That misalignment is why no practice that intends to compound its IP should price its core work by the hour. Hourly is fine for genuinely open-ended work where neither of you can bound the scope. As the pricing model for a practice that has built reusable assets, it turns your biggest advantage into a reason to charge less. [^5]

## Productized: a fixed price on a bounded offer

Productized pricing is the natural next step for the practice that has done Lessons 2 and 5, because it is the commercial expression of packaging. You sell a defined offer at a fixed price, and the client buys certainty: a known number, a known deliverable, a known boundary. What they are buying, and what justifies a premium over your hourly cost, is that certainty plus the speed your IP provides.

Two disciplines make a productized price work, and both come from earlier lessons. First, the offer has to be genuinely bounded, the scoped, entry-criteria, change-controlled offer from Lesson 2, not a vague promise dressed as a fixed fee. If the scope is fuzzy, a fixed price is not productization, it is a gamble that the client will not ask for more. Second, production has to run on IP, the method, templates, and tooling from Lesson 5, so that your cost to deliver is known and low enough to leave margin at whatever price you set. This is where the IP lesson and the margin lesson converge on pricing: the asset lowers your cost ceiling, and the fixed price converts that lower cost into wider margin without you having to raise your rate. [^6]

Setting the number is where most people go wrong, because they price the productized offer by reverse-engineering from hours: estimate the hours, multiply by your rate, add a bit, quote it. That is hourly thinking wearing a fixed-price coat, and it throws away the whole point. The productized price should be set from the value the offer creates for the client, anchored against what it would cost them to achieve the outcome another way, and then checked only that it clears your cost ceiling with enough margin. The price does not come from your hours. It comes from the client's problem. Your cost ceiling is a floor you must not price below, not the source of the number. If the offer is a good one, the value it creates is larger than your cost of producing it, and the productized price lives in that gap. [^7]

The risk you take on in exchange for that margin is scope creep, because you quoted a fixed number and the client will naturally push the boundaries of what it covers. The mitigation is not to pad the price against every possible ask, which makes you uncompetitive, it is to make the boundary and the change control explicit before you quote, so that additions beyond the boundary are priced as additions and not absorbed into a fixed fee that was never meant to carry them. A productized offer priced on value and protected by a real boundary is the engine that lets a practice charge for output instead of time.

## Value-based: the highest ceiling and the riskiest floor

Value-based pricing is where the ceiling gets highest and the floor gets most dangerous, and the principal has to understand both before deciding it is worth the risk. In value-based pricing you set your price against a measured outcome the client cares about, so what you are paid rises and falls with the value you demonstrably produce. Done well it is the most aligned model there is: the client only pays when they get value, and you are rewarded for exactly the thing you are best at. Done badly it is a way to give away your best work and absorb risk you cannot price. [^8]

The conditions that make value-based pricing legitimate are strict, and naming them is most of the skill. First, the outcome has to be measurable against a credible baseline. You cannot price against value you cannot quantify, because there is nothing to anchor the number or to settle the dispute when the engagement ends. Second, the client has to control enough of the inputs to make the outcome reachable. If the value depends on the client actually using, adopting, and sustaining what you deliver, and they will not or cannot, then you are taking outcome risk for a result you do not fully control. Third, the attribution has to be reasonably clean, meaning the value you are claiming is plausibly caused by your work and not by a hundred other things happening in the client's business at the same time. Where those three conditions hold, value-based pricing is fair to both sides. Where any one of them fails, it is a trap, and the professional move is to decline that pricing model for that engagement, not to grit your teeth and take the risk. [^9]

The structure that makes value-based pricing survivable is a floor plus a share, never a pure contingency. You charge a fee that covers your delivery cost and your baseline margin, so you are not losing money while you work, and on top of that you take a share of the measured value above the baseline. This is critical, because a pure value-based fee, paid only if and when the value lands, converts your delivery system into an unfunded speculation: you carry the cost of delivery and the outcome risk with no floor under you. The floor-plus-share structure keeps you solvent on the work itself and lets the value share be the upside, the reward for outcomes that exceed the baseline. Structure value-based deals this way and they fund the practice instead of draining it. [^10]

## Pricing risk you actually understand

Pull the three models together and the operating rule emerges, and it is the one the principal should carry into every pricing conversation. You choose a pricing model by asking which risks you are willing to own and whether you can price them, not by asking which model sounds most advanced. A practice that jumps straight to value-based because it is the trendy ceiling, without the baseline, the client control, or the delivery cost floor, has taken on the riskiest structure with the least protection. A practice that stays on hourly forever has refused to charge for the asset it built. The mature position is a catalog priced by model, each offer priced with the model that matches how well you can bound its scope and how measurable its value is. [^11]

A productized offer, where the scope is bounded and production runs on IP, supports a fixed price. An outcome with a clean baseline and strong client control supports value-based. Open-ended work with no boundable scope and no measurable outcome supports hourly, and there is no shame in that, it is the honest model for genuinely unboundable work. The mistake is not using hourly where it fits. The mistake is using hourly everywhere by default because you never learned to price the other two, or jumping to value-based everywhere because it sounds prestigious and you never learned where it is safe. A principal prices the catalog offer by offer, and the sophistication is in the matching, not in picking a single model and applying it to everything. [^12]

There is one more discipline that keeps value-based and productized pricing honest at practice scale, and it is the scheduled review from Lesson 7 applied to the price. Prices drift out of alignment the way utilization and margin do: the market shifts, your cost of delivery falls as IP compounds, the value a client gets from a standard offer changes. A productized price set two years ago may now be far below the value it creates, which is a quiet leak, or a value-based share may have been set when you knew less about the risk, which is a quiet over-exposure. The fix is to review the pricing of the catalog on a fixed cadence, the way you review the operating numbers, so that the price of every offer is re-checked against current value, current cost, and current risk. In Hermes this is a scheduled job that fires the pricing and margin review on its own rather than waiting for a quiet moment that never comes. A practice that prices deliberately and reviews the pricing on a schedule keeps every offer priced at the value it delivers, instead of drifting back toward whatever it happened to charge last year. [^13]

## Worked example: moving a delivery practice off the hour

Put it together on the practice from the earlier lessons, the one with packaged offers, captured IP, a delivery system on a bounded agent fleet, and a principal who has been reading utilization, capacity, and margin on a schedule. [^14] The practice still prices its core work hourly, because that is what it has always done and it is easy to quote. Revenue is flat despite better delivery, and the principal finally sees why: the IP that should be making the practice money is making it cheaper, and under hourly pricing cheaper means a smaller bill. Every engagement runs faster on the captured method and bills fewer hours. The efficiency machine is pricing itself into the ground. [^5]

The principal decides to move the catalog. They take the strongest, most repeatable offer, the one whose scope they can bound and whose production runs on the captured method and templates, and they productize it: a fixed price for a defined deliverable, quoted as a number and protected by the change control from Lesson 2. [^6] The price is set from the value the offer creates for the client, checked only that it clears the delivery cost with the margin from Lesson 7, not reverse-engineered from hours. The client buys certainty, the practice sells output, and the falling cost of the IP now flows to margin instead of to a smaller bill. [^7]

Then they take one engagement where the value is genuinely measurable, a client with a clean baseline and real control over adoption, and they structure a value-based deal the safe way: a floor that covers delivery cost and baseline margin, plus a share of the measured value above baseline. [^10] They do not jump the whole catalog to value-based, because most of it does not meet the conditions, clean baseline, client control, clean attribution. They price those offers productized, and they keep hourly only for the genuinely open-ended work where neither side can bound the scope. [^11]

Six months later the practice earns the same revenue from fewer billable hours, because the productized and value-based offers convert the value of the outcome, not the time spent, into price. Margin is wider, because the IP lowers cost and the price no longer falls with it. The calendar is less full, because they are not selling hours, which leaves the spare capacity from Lesson 7 to go into the next productized offer. The practice stopped selling time. It now sells certainty and outcomes, and the delivery system it spent the earlier lessons building finally earns what it is worth. [^12]

## Try it

Diagnose how your practice prices today, and find the one offer you are systematically underpricing.

1. List your current pricing by offer, not in the abstract. Which model does each offer actually use, hourly, fixed, or outcome-based? Most practices believe they use several and discover, when forced to write it down, that nearly everything is hourly in some form.
2. For your single most repeatable offer, estimate the hours under it, then name the value it creates for the client. If the value is several times the hourly total, you have found an underpriced offer, and the gap is what productization is for. You are charging for the time when the client is paying for the outcome.
3. Ask of any value-based deal you are tempted to take: is the outcome measurable against a baseline, does the client control the inputs, and is the attribution clean? If you cannot answer yes to all three, you are about to carry outcome risk you cannot price. Decline or restructure.
4. Check the structure. Would your value-based fee leave you losing money while you work if the value never landed? If so, it has no floor. Add a cost-covering fee and make the value a share on top, never a pure contingency.
5. Put the catalog review on the schedule. When did you last re-check a productized price against the value it now creates? If the answer is never, the drift from Lesson 7 is already quietly leaking margin.

If the diagnosis shows you sell your best work by the hour and your efficiency makes your bills smaller, you have found the pricing gap this lesson closes. The value is the price. The hours are just your cost, and your cost is your problem to manage, not the number you should charge the client.

## What's next

You can now set a price on value instead of time, choose the pricing model that matches each offer's scope and measurable outcome, and structure value-based deals so they fund the practice instead of draining it. The next lesson moves from the price you charge to how you govern the large engagements that price now lets you win: steering committees, status cadence, and escalation. Where this lesson taught you to convert value into revenue, the next one teaches you to protect that revenue once the engagement grows big enough that a single consultant-client relationship cannot hold it. Large-engagement governance is the layer that keeps a big, high-value engagement from collapsing into misaligned stakeholders and missed expectations, and it is the discipline a principal needs the moment the practice starts landing the productized and value-based work this lesson taught you to price.
