# Lesson 5: Building Reusable IP and Productized Offerings

Lesson 2 taught you to turn capability into offers, and Lesson 4 taught you to keep the delivery system coherent with standards and review. This lesson sits between them and answers a question neither one fully asked: what does the practice actually own? An offer is something a client can buy. The intellectual property underneath it, the method, the templates, the reference architecture, the accumulated lessons, is something the practice owns and can reuse across every sale. That distinction, between the offer and the IP that produces it, is the difference between a practice that sells its time and a practice that sells an asset it keeps getting better at producing. This lesson is about building that asset deliberately. [^1]

## The offer is not the asset

Start with the distinction, because conflating the two is how practices stay small without realizing why.

An offer, from Lesson 2, is a specific, priced, scoped thing a client can say yes to. It has a deliverable, an outcome, a boundary, a price, and entry criteria. It is the unit of transaction. [^2] The IP is everything the practice uses to produce that offer that it does not have to rebuild from scratch each time: the method for doing the work, the templates the deliverable is built from, the reference architecture that says how the system is put together, the internal tooling that automates the repetitive parts, and the accumulated lessons from every prior delivery. The offer is what the client buys. The IP is what makes the offer cheap to produce and hard to copy.

The reason the distinction matters is that they compound differently. An offer is consumed when it is sold. You deliver it, the client has it, and it is gone. IP is not consumed. It is used, and using it makes it better. Every delivery that runs on the method sharpens the method. Every engagement that uses the template improves the template. The offer is a transaction. The IP is an asset, and assets are what a practice is actually worth. A practice with a great offer and no IP is a practice that has to reinvent its own method on every engagement, which is the solo consultant's economics wearing a nicer suit. [^3]

## Why IP is the compounding asset

The whole case for building IP rests on one property: it compounds. And it compounds in a specific way that is worth being precise about, because it is the mechanism that turns a practice from a job into a business.

The first thing IP does is lower the cost of production. The first time you deliver an offer, you are building the method, the templates, and the architecture as you go, and that is expensive. The fifth time, you are running a method that already exists, filling in templates that already exist, and assembling against an architecture that already exists. The cost per delivery falls, and it falls without the price falling, because the price was set on the value of the outcome, not on your cost. That gap, between a falling cost and a stable value-based price, is margin, and it is the direct product of IP. [^4]

The second thing IP does is raise the ceiling on what the practice can take on. A practice with a reference architecture can take a larger, more complex engagement than a practice that has to design the architecture from scratch every time, because the hard design work is already done and the team is executing against a known shape. IP is not just a cost saver. It is a capability multiplier. It lets the practice do work it could not otherwise do, because the risk of the unknown is already retired. [^5]

The third thing IP does is make the practice harder to copy. A competitor can see your offer, the price and the deliverable, and copy the offer. They cannot see the method, the accumulated lessons, the internal tooling, the years of delivery history encoded in how you work. That invisible layer is the moat. It is the reason two practices can sell the same offer and one of them makes money while the other loses it, because one has the IP and the other only has the offer. [^6]

## What counts as IP

It helps to name the categories, because most practices have far more IP than they realize and far less of it is captured than they think. There are five kinds, and a healthy practice builds all of them.

Method. The sequence of decisions and steps that turns a client's problem into a delivered outcome. This is the procedural knowledge of how the practice does the work. In Hermes terms this is a skill, the on-demand procedural document an agent loads and follows, which is the literal mechanism for encoding method so that any competent executor runs it the same way. [^7]

Templates. The reusable starting points for deliverables: the proposal, the discovery brief, the runbook, the report. A template is IP because it encodes the structure of good output, so the team is not reinventing the shape of a deliverable every time. The value is not the file. It is the accumulated judgment about what a good deliverable contains, frozen into a reusable form.

Reference architecture. The canonical design for how the practice builds a class of system. This is the answer to the question "how do we build this kind of thing," written down and loadable, so every engagement starts from a known-good design instead of a blank page. It is the architectural IP that lets the practice take on larger work with less risk. [^8]

Internal tooling. The scripts, automations, and checks that do the repetitive parts of delivery. Anything the practice builds once and runs on every engagement is tooling IP. In Hermes this includes the scheduled jobs that run the operating cadence and the verification scripts that gate quality, the machinery that makes delivery repeatable and safe. [^9]

Accumulated lessons. The record of what worked and what did not, from every delivery. This is the institutional memory of the practice, and it is the IP that makes everything else smarter over time. In Hermes this is the memory layer, the durable record that accumulates across sessions so the practice gets better at what it does the more it does it. [^10]

## The capture discipline

Here is the hard truth that separates practices that build IP from practices that leak it. IP is not built by doing good work. It is built by capturing the good work, deliberately, while it is happening. The work itself is consumed. The capture is what turns it into an asset.

The failure mode is the practice that does excellent work and has nothing to show for it afterward. Every engagement is a fresh act of invention, the method lives in the heads of the people who did it, the templates were improvised and discarded, the lessons were never written down. The work was great and the practice is no richer for it, because none of it was captured. This is the most common way consulting practices stay small, and it is not a talent problem. It is a capture problem.

The discipline is to make capture a scheduled, structural part of delivery, not an afterthought. The method gets written down as it is developed, not reconstructed at the end. The templates get saved and improved after each use. The lessons get recorded on a cadence, so the accumulation happens reliably instead of on memory. In Hermes this is the difference between a practice that runs a scheduled review of what it learned and one that hopes to remember. The capture has to be loadable and it has to be scheduled, or it does not happen. [^11]

There is a second part to the discipline, and it is the one that makes captured IP actually useful. Captured IP has to be loadable, not just stored. A method that lives in a document nobody loads is not IP, it is a suggestion. The test is whether a competent executor, without the person who built it, can load the method and run it. That is the same test Lesson 4 applied to standards, and it applies to IP for the same reason: IP only compounds if it is actually used, and it is only used if it is loadable. [^12]

## From IP to productized offering

The payoff of building IP is that it lets you productize, and productization is the commercial expression of the asset. A productized offering is an offer whose production runs on IP: the method is encoded, the templates exist, the architecture is known, the tooling is built. The offer is the same as any offer from Lesson 2, with one difference. It is cheap to produce, because the IP does the heavy lifting, and it is repeatable, because the IP makes every delivery run the same way.

The productization move is to take the IP you have accumulated and package it into an offer that a client can buy without you inventing the scope. This is where the IP and the offer finally connect. The offer is the wrapper the client sees. The IP is the engine underneath it. A productized offering is the point where the practice stops selling its time and starts selling the output of an asset it owns. [^13]

The discipline of productization is to resist the urge to make every offering bespoke. The bespoke instinct is the enemy of IP, because a bespoke engagement produces no reusable asset. It is a one-off that consumes the practice's best judgment and returns nothing the practice can use again. Productization is the deliberate choice to build a bounded set of repeatable offerings, each running on captured IP, and to say no to the work that would not build the asset. This is the same discipline Lesson 2 applied to service lines, applied to the asset layer: a practice that productizes builds a catalog that compounds. A practice that does everything bespoke builds a reputation and nothing it owns. [^14]

## The ownership question

There is a question that every principal building IP has to answer, and it is the one that most practices get wrong by not asking it at all: who owns the IP?

The distinction is between client IP and practice IP. Client IP is the specific, confidential material a client brings to an engagement: their data, their internal processes, their proprietary knowledge. That belongs to the client, and the practice has no right to reuse it. Practice IP is the method, the templates, the architecture, the tooling, the lessons, the generalizable way of doing the work. That belongs to the practice, and it is the asset this lesson is about building. [^15]

The discipline is to draw the line explicitly, in the engagement terms, before the work starts, so that the practice's right to reuse its own method and templates is not in dispute. The client owns what they brought and what is specific to them. The practice owns how it does the work and the reusable machinery it built to do it. A practice that does not draw this line risks losing its own IP to a client contract, or, just as bad, quietly reusing client-specific material it has no right to. Both are failures, and both are prevented by naming the boundary up front. [^16]

## Worked example

Put it together on a practice that has been delivering well and wants to start owning what it delivers. A principal has, after Lessons 2 through 4, a working fleet, packaged offers, and a coherent standard. The work is good. The problem is that none of it is accumulating. Every engagement is a fresh act of invention, and the principal realizes the practice is not getting richer for all the good work it does.

So they start capturing. They take the way the practice builds its core deliverable and encode it as a loadable skill, so the method is no longer in the heads of the people who did it but in the system any executor loads. [^7] They save the templates from the last engagement and improve them after each use, so the shape of a good deliverable stops being reinvented. They write down the reference architecture for the class of system they build, so the next engagement starts from a known-good design instead of a blank page. [^8] They build the internal tooling that automates the repetitive parts, the scheduled jobs and verification checks that make delivery repeatable. [^9] And they run a scheduled capture of lessons, so the accumulation happens on a cadence instead of on memory. [^11]

Then they productize. They take the strongest service line and turn it into a productized offering that runs on the captured IP. The offer is the same shape as any offer from Lesson 2, but now it is cheap to produce, because the method, templates, architecture, and tooling already exist, and it is repeatable, because every delivery runs the same way. [^13] The cost per delivery falls, the margin holds because the price was set on value, and the practice finally has an asset that compounds instead of a series of one-off transactions. [^4]

The compounding shows up in the numbers. The first productized delivery costs the most, because the IP is being built as it is used. By the fifth, the cost has fallen, because the method is encoded and the team is not rediscovering it. The practice can now take on larger engagements, because the reference architecture retired the risk of the unknown. [^5] And a competitor can see the offer and copy it, but they cannot copy the method, the lessons, the tooling, the years of delivery history encoded in how the practice works. That is the moat. [^6]

That is the whole lesson in miniature. The practice stopped selling its time and started selling the output of an asset it owns. The offer is what the client buys. The IP is what makes the offer cheap, repeatable, and hard to copy. And the practice that builds the IP deliberately is the one that compounds, while the practice that only delivers is the one that stays small.

## Try it

Do not go build a whole IP portfolio. Diagnose the assets in the practice you already run, whatever it is.

1. List the last three engagements you delivered. For each, ask: what did the practice keep afterward? If the answer is nothing, you have identified the leak. The work was consumed and no asset was captured.
2. Name the method you use to deliver your core service. Could a competent person, without you, load it and run it? If the method lives only in your head, you do not have IP, you have experience. Write the method down as a loadable playbook.
3. Find the template you improvise most often. Save it, then improve it after the next use. That single act, turning an improvised deliverable into a reusable template, is the smallest unit of IP building.
4. Draw the ownership line. Write, in one sentence, what belongs to the client and what belongs to the practice on your next engagement. If you cannot write it, you have not decided it, and the decision will be made for you by a contract you did not read closely enough.

If the diagnosis shows you do excellent work and keep nothing, you have found the gap this lesson closes. The work is not the asset. The capture is. Build the capture and the work starts compounding.

## What's next

You can now turn a repeatable delivery method into intellectual property the practice owns, and package that IP into productized offerings that compound instead of being consumed. The next lesson moves from the assets to the people who run them: hiring, onboarding, and running a team of consultants. Where this lesson built the reusable machinery the practice owns, the next one builds the team that operates it, how to hire for the delivery system rather than for a single role, how to onboard people onto the captured method, and how to run a team so the practice can grow past what one principal can personally supervise. That is the layer that turns a practice with great IP into a practice that can scale.
